\begin{abstract}
GBFPlus publishes occupancy-marked exclusive-or (XOR) shares to support authorized tracing
of cross-domain data flows. Its stated confidentiality goals include hiding
adjacent-domain relationships from ordinary ledger observers. We show that an
honestly generated filter containing one successful insertion reveals its
adjacent-domain identifier exactly: the observer XORs its occupied cells and
reads the identifier suffix. Such a filter is permitted by time-window
finalization. More generally, a public occupancy count equal to the insertion
count times the number of shares certifies disjoint insertion supports and
reveals the XOR of their adjacent-domain identifiers. These results hold for
every share realization without learning a query token or data identifier.
We supply finite exhaustive checks and a share-level implementation, distinguish
this disclosure from the paper's identifier-privacy game, and compare the
exposure with the padding and access pattern of the original garbled Bloom
filter. The contribution is a protocol-specific confidentiality counterexample,
not a new general result about XOR sharing.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} garbled Bloom filter; metadata confidentiality;
public occupancy; cross-domain attestation; XOR sharing

\section{Introduction and the confidentiality claim}

Qin et al. propose GBFPlus for privacy-preserving on-chain attestation of
cross-domain data transfers~\cite{qin2026gbfplus}. Section 4.2 includes adjacent
domain relationships among the metadata to be concealed from ordinary ledger
observers; Table 1 also presents the adjacent domain as protected. The purpose
of this note is to identify an honest execution in which the public filter
directly discloses that payload.

\subsection{Published representation and observer model}

The attack uses the published representation and Algorithm 4. A filter has
$m$ cells, each with an occupancy indicator and a $\lambda$-bit payload. An
unoccupied cell is distinguishable from an occupied cell containing the all-zero
word. A successful insertion selects $\kappa$ distinct positions and enforces
\begin{equation}
  \bigoplus_{j\in S_i} Z_j = T_i,
  \qquad T_i=P_i\mathbin\Vert N_i, \qquad |S_i|=\kappa,
  \label{eq:row}
\end{equation}
where $N_i$ is the $\ell$-bit adjacent-domain identifier and $P_i$ is a
$(\lambda-\ell)$-bit truncated hash of a secret-derived value. Registered
domain identifiers and their associations are public (Section 5.1).
The attacker need not know $P_i$, the data identifier, the hidden index seed,
or the selected positions in advance.

An insertion reserves one empty selected cell, fills the other empty selected
cells independently at random, reuses previously occupied values, and places
the required XOR residual in the reserved cell. If no selected cell is empty,
the insertion fails without modifying the filter. Occupied words are never
overwritten by a later successful insertion, so previous equations remain
valid. The filter starts empty.

Section 5.2 permits finalization at the end of a configured time window, as
well as upon capacity or insertion failure. It does not specify a minimum
successful insertion count. The transaction described following Equation (5)
contains the finalized filter and the successful insertion count $c$.
Section 6.2 explicitly accounts for occupancy and sparse payload storage.
Thus a time window containing one successful insertion can publish $c=1$ and
its complete occupied share set. This is an allowed execution, not a claim
about how often singleton windows occur in any deployment.

We assume an honestly generated, authentically committed snapshot. Public
dimensions and count alone do not prove that a maliciously formed table has
such a history. The observer reads the public transaction but has no regulator
secret, authorization token, or active query capability. The view is frozen:
reading it twice gives the same cells. The attack already succeeds on one
snapshot and needs neither adaptive queries nor independent reconstructions.

\section{Exact disclosure from occupancy}

Let $O$ be the publicly occupied set and $w=|O|$. Since the filter starts empty
and only successful insertions add cells,
$O=\bigcup_{i=1}^{c}S_i$. Failed insertions do not change this union or $c$.
Write $\suffix_\ell(x)$ for the final $\ell$ bits of a word $x$.

\begin{proposition}[Publicly certified disjoint supports]
\label{prop:disjoint}
For an honestly generated snapshot with $c\geq1$, if $w=c\kappa$, then
\begin{equation}
 \suffix_\ell\!\left(\bigoplus_{j\in O}Z_j\right)
       =\bigoplus_{i=1}^{c}N_i.
 \label{eq:leak}
\end{equation}
In particular, every finalized singleton reveals its adjacent-domain
identifier with probability one over all construction randomness.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each support has $\kappa$ distinct positions. Equality in
$|\bigcup_i S_i|\leq\sum_i|S_i|=c\kappa$ implies that the supports are pairwise
disjoint. Therefore XORing the occupied words is the same as XORing the
left-hand sides of~\eqref{eq:row} once each. This gives $\bigoplus_i T_i$.
Taking the suffix commutes with XOR and proves~\eqref{eq:leak}.
When $c=1$, the occupied set is exactly $S_1$, so the condition always holds.
The argument is an identity for every valid realization.
\end{proof}

The observer scans occupancy, counts occupied cells, and accumulates their
XOR. It returns the suffix only if $c\geq1$ and $w=c\kappa$; otherwise this
particular attack abstains. A singleton costs $O(m+\kappa\lambda)$ elementary
bit operations and $O(\lambda+\log m)$ auxiliary bits when the input can be
scanned. The general certified case costs $O(m+c\kappa\lambda)$. This does not
include downloading, parsing, or authenticating a ledger transaction. No
dictionary of candidate data identifiers or search over hidden supports is
needed. For $c>1$ the conclusion is an aggregate XOR, not recovery of the
individual endpoints or their multiset.

\begin{proposition}[Uniform individual shares, distinguishable joint views]
\label{prop:joint}
Fix a singleton support, prefix, and $\kappa\geq2$. Each occupied payload word
is uniformly distributed on $\{0,1\}^{\lambda}$, independently of the adjacent
identifier. Nevertheless the complete payload views for two distinct adjacent
identifiers have disjoint supports.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Algorithm 4 samples $\kappa-1$ independent uniform words and fixes the remaining
word to satisfy~\eqref{eq:row}. Each sampled word is uniform. The residual word
is uniform because its XOR includes at least one independent uniform word.
However the suffix of the XOR of the entire view is always the specified
identifier. Two different identifiers cannot yield the same complete view.
\end{proof}

This gives a simple confidentiality comparison. Fix the public registration
table, publishing administrator, data identifier, direction, time window,
configuration, count, and occupied positions. Choose one of two different
registered adjacent-domain identifiers with equal probability and construct
the singleton honestly. The hidden position derivation in Algorithm 4 does
not use the adjacent-domain suffix, so these structural quantities can be the
same in both cases. The observer identifies the choice with success
probability one by Proposition~\ref{prop:disjoint}. Occupancy alone is identical
between the cases; the leak comes from the public payloads selected by it.
Digests and signatures authenticate the challenge-dependent filter and are
part of its public view, not structural metadata required to remain identical.

This comparison formalizes adjacent-payload confidentiality. It is not claimed
to be the paper's $G_{\mathrm{qpriv}}$ game. That game selects data identifiers,
and Theorem 4 addresses identifier privacy and fresh blinding. Revealing the
same adjacent identifier in both worlds would not by itself distinguish those
identifier choices. Nor does our result identify the anonymous signer, recover
raw data, reconstruct a complete path, or produce an authorized query token.

For example, with a 3-bit target $101$ and selected positions $(5,1,3)$,

values XOR to $101$, disclosing the suffix $01$ despite the zero residual.
Tests also cover overlapping supports, unchanged previous row equations,
atomic insertion failure, position-order changes, empty filters, and frozen
repeated reads. The seeded checks use a reproducible general-purpose PRNG;
the theorem holds for every share choice and does not rely on its security.

\section{Discussion and established baselines}

The underlying algebra is established. Linear coset-encoding analyses already
characterize information in joint linear observations by rank deficits; see
Silva and Kschischang, Lemma 6~\cite{silva2011universal}. Marginal distribution
shaping does not replace a joint-view argument. Accordingly, this note does not
claim a new general leakage calculus or a break of oblivious key-value stores
under their own value-distribution and access assumptions. Its increment is
the publicly identifiable complete share set in the stated GBFPlus execution.

The original garbled Bloom filter is a necessary contrast. Dong, Chen, and
Wen's construction fills all remaining empty cells with random words
(Algorithm 1, lines 22--26); its private-set-intersection protocol uses
oblivious transfer to deliver the intersection structure~\cite{dong2013gbf}.
It does not expose an unauthorized observer to the same full server structure
and genuine occupancy selector as this attack assumes. The authors also
distinguish recovering a stored value from a false-positive membership answer.

For the specific all-cell XOR statistic, one independent uniform padding word
makes the result uniform if the observer cannot remove it using a genuine
occupancy selector. Filling unused GBFPlus cells while retaining that selector
does not help: the observer still selects exactly $O$. These facts do not
prove that deleting occupancy alone secures GBFPlus against other attacks.
Independent secret masks on endpoint suffixes would also hide this statistic,
but secret masking is an established technique: for example, Hao et al.
encode arbitrary values after PRF masking in their OKVR construction
\cite{hao2024okvr}. A protocol repair must specify authorized demasking,
key management, and separation across instances. Reusing a pad across releases
can expose differences. No complete repaired GBFPlus protocol is claimed here.

\section{Conclusion and limits}

Equation (27) of the source paper analyzes acceptance of guessed tuples under
its random-share model, using both a prefix test and a domain-membership test.
The observer here
does not validate the unknown prefix: it reads the adjacent-domain suffix of
a complete share set identified by public occupancy. Accidental query acceptance
and confidentiality of that suffix are different events.

The protocol therefore permits endpoint disclosure beyond its explicitly
visible count, occupancy, direction, and timing. Enforcing a minimum count
would exclude singleton publication but would not by itself invalidate the
disjoint-support aggregate in Proposition~\ref{prop:disjoint}. The frequency
and impact of these conditions in operational workloads remain unmeasured.
A revised confidentiality claim or construction should address the complete
public payload view, including admissible partial batches. This narrow
counterexample does not require a broader attack on the cryptographic primitives.
