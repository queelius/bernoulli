                                                   Abstract
          GBFPlus supports authorized tracing of cross-domain data transfers through public ledger
      attestations. Its published description treats the adjacent-domain identifier as protected metadata.
      We show that an allowed one-insertion attestation reveals that identifier exactly. Public occupancy
      identifies all shares of the inserted word; their exclusive-or (XOR) reconstructs the word, including
      its unmasked identifier suffix. This holds for every construction realization and requires neither
      a query token nor a candidate data identifier. A public occupancy test also identifies some larger
      batches in which the same operation reveals an aggregate XOR, rather than individual identifiers.
      We prove these statements and reproduce them with finite exhaustive checks and stored share
      arrays. The disclosure concerns adjacent-domain metadata, distinct from the source paper’s
      data-identifier privacy game and signer anonymity. The stated metadata-confidentiality claim
      therefore needs to account for allowed partial batches and the complete public payload view.

Keywords: garbled Bloom filter; metadata confidentiality; public occupancy; cross-domain attesta-
tion; XOR sharing


1     The confidentiality claim and public view
Qin et al.’s GBFPlus protocol records cross-domain data transfers in public ledger attestations while
supporting authorized tracing [1]. The source article lists the adjacent domain among the protected
metadata (Table 1 and Section 4.2); Section 6.2 specifically describes recovering its identifier only
after the consistency-prefix and assigned-domain checks. We identify an allowed execution in which
an ordinary ledger observer recovers that identifier without either check: a time window closes
after one successful insertion, and the publicly occupied cells contain exactly that insertion’s shares.
XORing their payloads reconstructs the inserted word, whose suffix identifies the adjacent domain.
     Three features of the published specification suffice for this disclosure. First, Algorithm 4 encodes
an unmasked adjacent-domain identifier in the shared word. Second, Section 5.2 publishes the
finalized filter and its successful insertion count in the transaction following source Equation (5);
Sections 5.2 and 6.2 make occupied cells distinguishable from empty cells. Third, Section 5.2 finalizes
a filter when its time window ends, without specifying a minimum insertion count. Thus an honest
administrator can initialize a directional filter, insert one transfer, receive no further transfer in
that direction, and publish the filter when the window closes. The source’s honest-administrator
model (Section 4.3) and these rules admit this execution; they do not establish how often it occurs
in deployment.

    The observer therefore needs only the occupied words and the suffix width. It does not have to
guess the data identifier, the prefix, or the selected positions. Figure 1 distinguishes an occupied
zero word from an empty cell: the zero word counts toward occupancy but does not change the
XOR. A scan takes O(m + κλ) elementary bit operations and O(λ + log m) auxiliary bits for a
singleton; ledger transfer, parsing, and authentication are outside this scan cost.
    The disclosure is compatible with uniform individual shares. Conditional on any honestly
reached singleton position set and prefix, and for κ ≥ 2, each free word is uniform, and the residual
is uniform because its XOR includes at least one independent uniform word. Nevertheless, the
complete array always satisfies (2). Two different identifiers therefore give disjoint sets of possible
complete views, even though each individual share has the same distribution.

    For c > 1, this is an aggregate, not a reconstruction of the individual identifiers or their multiset.
The condition requires cκ ≤ m; otherwise it is impossible. Its informational value also depends on
the candidate histories. For example, in a reachable disjoint history, changing one eligible adjacent
identifier while fixing the others changes the aggregate and is distinguishable. If all admissible
histories already have the same aggregate, the statistic adds no information. The attack abstains
when w ̸= cκ; no distribution or frequency of disjoint batches is assumed.

Which confidentiality claim is challenged? Consider a publisher with two eligible neighboring
domains distinct from it. Fix the public registration table. Internally fix the same publisher, its
secret state, data identifier, direction, window, and configuration, and choose either neighboring
identifier with equal probability for a singleton insertion. The observer is not given the publisher’s
identity or secret state. In source Algorithm 4, the adjacent identifier affects the target suffix but
not the derived positions or prefix. Those quantities can therefore be the same in both cases, while
the free shares are sampled in each case. Equation (2) identifies the choice with probability one.
This comparison concerns one snapshot and its authenticating transaction; digests, signatures, and
other ledger records need not be identical between cases.
    The recovered identifier names the neighbor encoded in that attestation. It does not identify
the anonymous publisher, associate the transfer with a particular data identifier, or reconstruct a
complete named edge or path. This is also distinct from the source’s Gqpriv game and Theorem 4,
which concern distinguishing data identifiers. Separately, source Equation (27) analyzes guessed
tuples passing prefix and domain-membership checks under its random-share model. The observer
here performs neither validation test: it reads a suffix from a complete share set already identified
by occupancy. Those analyses do not establish confidentiality of this suffix.

4    Implications and existing protections
The protocol-specific issue is that public occupancy identifies a complete set of shares in an allowed
partial batch. The underlying distinction between individual and joint linear observations is
established; Silva and Kschischang’s coset-encoding analysis provides a general precedent [2]. The
original garbled Bloom filter is a relevant construction contrast: Dong, Chen, and Wen fill unused
cells with random words (Algorithm 1, lines 22–26), and their private-set-intersection protocol uses
oblivious transfer to supply an intersection structure [3]. That protocol does not give the same
unauthorized observer the full server filter together with genuine insertion occupancy.
    For the all-cell XOR alone, one independent uniform padding word makes the statistic uniform.
An observer who retains the genuine occupancy selector can still omit the padding and recover the
original occupied-cell XOR. Fresh uniform secret masks on the adjacent identifiers also hide this
statistic when the masks are independent of the identifiers and one another. Secret PRF masking of
arbitrary values is already used in oblivious key-value retrieval [4]. Neither observation proves a
complete repair of GBFPlus. A repair must account for the entire public view, authorized recovery,
and key and mask reuse across releases.
    A minimum batch size above one excludes the singleton execution. It excludes the disjoint-
position certificate only if accepted batches cannot satisfy w = cκ, for example when their counts
always exceed m/κ. The occurrence and impact of either condition in operational workloads
remain unmeasured. The exact singleton counterexample nevertheless shows that the published
controlled-recovery claim for adjacent identifiers does not hold for every allowed release. A revised
claim or construction must address these partial batches explicitly.
