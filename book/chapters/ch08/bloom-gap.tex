\section{The Bloom Filter's Gap}
\label{sec:bloom-gap}

\Cref{thm:entropy-floor} put a floor under every FNR-zero approximate
set: at least $-\log_2 \fprate$ bits per element, no matter what the
encoding looks like. The standard Bloom filter is FNR-zero by
construction (\Cref{def:bloom-filter}), so it is in the family the
theorem governs. The question this section settles is how far above
the floor the standard construction sits. The answer is a single
constant, $\log_2 e \approx 1.443$, and the constant is the same at
every $\fprate$. Bloom pays a fixed $44\%$ markup on the entropy
floor and never closes it; chapter~14 builds a different
representation that does.

\subsection*{Setup}

Recall the result of \Cref{thm:bloom-optimal-k}. A Bloom filter
parameterized by bit count $m$, hash count $k$, and insertion count
$n$ achieves its minimum false-positive rate at $k^* = (m/n) \ln 2$,
the choice that leaves the bit array half-occupied in expectation.
At that $k^*$ the optimal rate is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bloom-optimal-fpr-recall}
  \fprate^* \;=\; 2^{-(m/n) \ln 2}.
\end{equation}
The design parameter the engineer controls is the bit-per-element
ratio $m/n$. The error rate $\fprate^*$ that comes out is a fixed
function of that ratio. Larger $m/n$ buys a smaller $\fprate^*$, and
the trade is exponential.

\subsection*{Bits per element at a target FPR}

The engineering question is usually posed the other way around. A
designer chooses a target false-positive rate $\fprate$ and asks how
many bits per element the filter needs. Solving
\eqref{eq:bloom-optimal-fpr-recall} for $m/n$ at $\fprate^* =
\fprate$ inverts the relation. Take logarithms base 2 of both
sides:
\[
  \log_2 \fprate \;=\; -(m/n) \ln 2,
\]
which rearranges to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bloom-bits-per-element}
  \frac{m}{n}
  \;=\; \frac{\log_2(1/\fprate)}{\ln 2}
  \;=\; \log_2(1/\fprate) \cdot \log_2 e.
\end{equation}
The second equality uses the identity $1 / \ln 2 = \log_2 e$. So
the bit budget per element for a standard Bloom filter at target
$\fprate$ is a constant multiple, $\log_2 e$, of the quantity
$\log_2(1/\fprate)$, which is the entropy-floor cost from
\Cref{thm:entropy-floor}.

\subsection*{Comparing to the floor}

The floor of \eqref{eq:entropy-floor} reads, in the same units as
\eqref{eq:bloom-bits-per-element},
\[
  \frac{m_{\min}}{n} \;\ge\; \log_2(1/\fprate).
\]
Dividing the Bloom expression by the floor gives the ratio
\[
  \frac{m / n}{m_{\min} / n}
  \;=\; \log_2 e
  \;=\; \frac{1}{\ln 2}
  \;\approx\; 1.4427.
\]
The ratio is independent of $\fprate$: every Bloom filter, regardless
of the target error rate, pays the same $\log_2 e$ multiplier on the
floor.

\begin{proposition}[Bloom filter overhead]
\label{prop:bloom-overhead}
At every target false-positive rate $\fprate \in (0, 1)$, the optimal
standard Bloom filter (\Cref{thm:bloom-optimal-k}) uses
\[
  \frac{m}{n} \;=\; \log_2(1/\fprate) \cdot \log_2 e
\]
bits per element, which is $\log_2 e \approx 1.4427$ times the
information-theoretic floor of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor}. The relative
overhead is a constant, independent of $\fprate$.
\end{proposition}

The number $\log_2 e$ has surfaced before, in the chapter~5
derivation of $k^*$: the optimal hash count $(m/n)\ln 2$ is the one
that produces a half-occupied bitmap, and $\ln 2$ is precisely
$1/\log_2 e$. The same constant that pins down the optimal hash
count also pins down the gap to the floor. The two facts are dual
statements about the same construction: half-occupancy is
information-theoretically the wrong target, but it is the best target
available to a representation built from $k$ independent hashes and a
flat bit array.

\subsection*{The 44\% overhead in absolute terms}

The constant $1.44$ understates how much storage the gap costs at
small $\fprate$. Multiplying a fixed factor onto a function that
grows like $\log_2(1/\fprate)$ produces an absolute overhead that
also grows, and at the error tolerances people actually deploy the
overhead is several bits per element. The table makes this
concrete:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|rrr}
\hline
  $\fprate$ & floor (bits/el) & Bloom (bits/el) & gap (bits/el) \\
\hline
  $10^{-2}$ & $6.64$  & $9.59$  & $2.95$ \\
  $10^{-3}$ & $9.97$  & $14.38$ & $4.41$ \\
  $10^{-6}$ & $19.93$ & $28.76$ & $8.83$ \\
  $10^{-9}$ & $29.90$ & $43.14$ & $13.25$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
At a one-percent false-positive rate the standard Bloom filter pays
about three extra bits per element on top of the floor. At a
one-in-a-million rate it pays about nine. The relative overhead is
fixed at $44\%$, but the absolute overhead grows linearly in
$\log_2(1/\fprate)$ along with the floor itself. Doubling the bit
budget (in floor-equivalent terms) doubles the absolute waste. For a
million-element approximate set at $\fprate = 10^{-6}$, the gap
between Bloom and the floor is about $8.83$ megabits, or $1.10$
megabytes per million items, that the standard construction simply
cannot recover.

A more sympathetic reading is also available. The Bloom filter pays a
constant factor, not a growing one, and a $44\%$ markup on an
already-small quantity is acceptable in many deployment settings.
The information-theoretic floor is a target, not a deadline; the
Bloom filter is competitive even though it is suboptimal. The point
of \Cref{prop:bloom-overhead} is to quantify the slack, not to
condemn the construction. Bloom filters dominate the
approximate-set ecosystem for a reason. The question we now turn to
is what part of the Bloom design forces the slack and what kind of
construction would avoid it.

\subsection*{Why the gap is structural}

The Bloom filter pays its $\log_2 e$ overhead because of three
design choices that compose into the standard recipe:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{$k$ independent hash functions} write into a single
    shared bit array.
  \item The \emph{shared bit array is flat}: bit $j$ is independent
    of bit $j'$ in the encoding's structure, and the only correlation
    between bits comes from the random hash collisions during
    insertion.
  \item The \emph{query is a conjunction of bit reads}: $\textsc{Query}(x)$
    accepts iff every one of the $k$ bits read at positions
    $h_1(x), \ldots, h_k(x)$ is $1$.
\end{itemize}
The analysis of \Cref{thm:bloom-optimal-k} treats the $k$ bits read
at query time as independent Bernoulli$(p)$ variables with $p = 1 -
e^{-kn/m}$. The product $p^k$ is the FPR formula
\eqref{eq:bloom-fpr}, and the optimum of $p^k$ over $k$ for fixed
$m/n$ sits at $p = 1/2$, where the bit array is half-occupied. A
half-occupied array carries one bit of Shannon entropy per array
bit, which is the maximum a binary variable can carry. The
representation is therefore information-dense at the bit level. But
the entropy floor is set by the membership-test problem, not by the
information density of the encoding, and the two coincide only when
the encoding's bits are tuned to the problem they encode rather than
to a generic balance condition. The Bloom filter optimizes the wrong
thing. It maximizes per-bit entropy and then takes the $k$th power
of that, getting a clean closed form and a clean optimum but
spending bits on the encoding's internal balance rather than on the
discrimination it was supposed to perform.

A representation that achieves the floor must abandon at least one
of these three design choices. The choice chapter~14 abandons is
the second: the flat shared bit array. The Bernoulli Hash Function
(BHF) of \Cref{ch:optimal-bernoulli-sets} replaces the flat bit
array with a \emph{perfect-hash-based encoding}, in which each
inserted element gets a deterministic location chosen by a perfect
hash, and the encoding stores a short fingerprint at that location.
The fingerprint length is exactly $\log_2(1/\fprate)$ bits, matching
the floor; the perfect hash ensures that collisions in the
fingerprint table are themselves the only source of false positives,
so the per-element cost is dominated by the fingerprint and the
$\log_2 e$ overhead disappears. The detailed construction is
chapter~14's business. The point to register here is that the gap
is not a deficiency of analysis. It comes from a specific structural
choice in the Bloom design, and a different structural choice can
in principle close the gap.
