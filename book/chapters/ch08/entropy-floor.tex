\section{The Entropy Floor for Approximate Sets}
\label{sec:entropy-floor}

The bit-budget comparison of \Cref{sec:one-sided-two-sided} placed the Bloom
filter at roughly $9.6$ bits per item at $\fprate = 0.01$ against a floor near
$6.64$. This section states that floor as a theorem, the classical counting
bound of \textcite{carter-et-al-1978}: any approximate set with $\fnrate = 0$
and false-positive rate at most $\fprate$, in any representation whatever,
must store about $\log_2(1/\fprate)$ bits per element. It then gives the
two-sided form, due to \textcite{pagh-rodler-2001}, and the constructive
picture.

\subsection*{Setup}

Fix a universe $\Universe$ with $|\Universe| = N$ and a target $A \subseteq
\Universe$ with $|A| = n$. An \emph{approximate-set representation} is a pair
$(R, \mathrm{query})$ with $R \in \{0,1\}^L$ a bit string and
$\mathrm{query}(R, \cdot) : \Universe \to \{\top, \bot\}$ a deterministic
membership algorithm; $L$ is the cost to bound. Following \Cref{def:one-sided}
we restrict to the FNR-zero family containing the Bloom filter:
$\mathrm{query}(R, x) = \top$ for every $x \in A$ (no inserted element is
missed), and at most a $\fprate$ fraction of the $N - n$ non-members is
accepted. The \emph{accepted set} $A_R = \{x : \mathrm{query}(R, x) = \top\}$
then satisfies $A \subseteq A_R$ and $|A_R \setminus A| \le \fprate(N - n)$, so
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:accepted-size}
  |A_R| \;\le\; M \;:=\; n + \fprate(N - n).
\end{equation}
The verdict on $x$ depends on $R$ only through which side of $A_R$ the query
lands, so $A_R$ is the representation-independent object that does the work.

\subsection*{The theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Entropy floor for \mbox{FNR-zero} approximate sets]
\label{thm:entropy-floor}
\index{space lower bound!approximate membership}
Suppose that an FNR-zero representation scheme $(R, \mathrm{query})$ of bit
length $L$ produces, for every $n$-element set $A \subseteq \Universe$, an
accepted set $A_R \supseteq A$ with $|A_R \setminus A| \le \fprate(N - n)$.
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:entropy-floor}
  L \;\ge\; n \log_2 \frac{1}{\fprate + n/N},
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \frac{L}{n} \;\ge\; \log_2 \frac{1}{\fprate} - o(1)
  \quad\text{as } \frac{n}{\fprate N} \to 0.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The argument is a pigeonhole count. Fix a representation $R$ and its accepted
set $A_R$. By FNR-zero $A \subseteq A_R$, and by \eqref{eq:accepted-size}
$|A_R| \le M$, so the true set is one of at most $\binom{\lfloor M \rfloor}{n}$
$n$-subsets of $A_R$, all of which $R$ reports identically. Every one of the
$\binom{N}{n}$ possible targets needs some $R \in \{0,1\}^L$ whose accepted
set contains it, and each of the $2^L$ strings covers at most
$\binom{\lfloor M \rfloor}{n}$ targets, so
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pigeonhole}
  2^L \binom{\lfloor M \rfloor}{n} \;\ge\; \binom{N}{n}.
\end{equation}
The ratio of the binomial coefficients is a product,
\[
  \frac{\binom{N}{n}}{\binom{\lfloor M \rfloor}{n}}
  \;=\; \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{N - i}{\lfloor M \rfloor - i}
  \;\ge\; \Bigl(\frac{N}{M}\Bigr)^{n},
\]
because $(N - i)/(K - i) \ge N/K$ whenever $N \ge K > i$. Taking logarithms,
$L \ge n \log_2(N/M)$, and $M/N = \fprate + (1 - \fprate)\,n/N \le \fprate +
n/N$, which gives \eqref{eq:entropy-floor}. The per-element form follows from
$\log_2(1/(\fprate + n/N)) = \log_2(1/\fprate) - \log_2(1 + n/(\fprate N))$.
\end{proof}

The condition $n/(\fprate N) \to 0$ is the deployment regime: $\fprate$ fixed
and the target embedded in a much larger universe. There the floor is sharp;
when $\Universe$ is barely larger than $A$, the $n/N$ term matters and the
per-element reading degrades, and at $\fprate = 1$ the bound is vacuous, met by
the trivial always-accept structure that stores nothing. The standard
formulation of the bound also allows the accepted superset to be random
\parencite{pagh-pagh-rao-2005}; the deterministic statement above is its core.

\subsection*{Form of the bound}

The bound has no parameters to tune: the per-element cost is a logarithm of the
inverse error rate, independent of how the representation is
built\index{space--accuracy tradeoff}.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|ll}
\hline
  $\fprate$ & $-\log_2 \fprate$ (bits/element) & bits for $n = 10^6$ \\
\hline
  $10^{-1}$ & $\approx 3.32$  & $\approx 0.42$\,MB \\
  $10^{-2}$ & $\approx 6.64$  & $\approx 0.83$\,MB \\
  $10^{-3}$ & $\approx 9.97$  & $\approx 1.25$\,MB \\
  $10^{-6}$ & $\approx 19.93$ & $\approx 2.49$\,MB \\
  $10^{-9}$ & $\approx 29.90$ & $\approx 3.74$\,MB \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A million-element FNR-zero set at one-in-a-billion false-positive rate cannot
fit in less than about $3.74$ megabytes, whatever the encoding. The cost grows
\emph{additively}, by $\log_2 10 \approx 3.32$ bits per element for each order
of magnitude in $1/\fprate$, not geometrically. The universe size $N$ does not
enter the leading term: one might have expected the $\log_2\binom{N}{n}$ cost
of identifying $A$ exactly, but the FPR budget buys back precisely that
universe-dependent excess, leaving the residual $n\log_2(1/\fprate)$.

\subsection*{The two-sided case}

The proof of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor} uses FNR-zero to force $A \subseteq
A_R$, and that containment is what makes $\binom{\lfloor M \rfloor}{n}$ the
right count. If members may be rejected\index{two-sided error}, $A$ need not
sit inside $A_R$ and the count changes. The answer is nevertheless known to leading order.

\begin{proposition}[Two-sided floor; Pagh and Rodler]
\label{prop:two-sided-floor}
\index{space lower bound!with false negatives}
Let a representation of $n$-element subsets of a universe of size $N$ reject
at most $\gamma n$ of the members of each set and accept at most a fraction
$\fprate$ of its non-members. Then its length satisfies
\[
  L \;\ge\; (1 - \gamma)\, n \log_2 \frac{1}{\fprate + n/N} \;-\; O(n).
\]
\end{proposition}

This is Proposition~1 of \textcite{pagh-rodler-2001}, stated there for the
lossy dictionaries they introduce; we do not reprove it. The bound is matched
to leading order in $\log(1/\fprate)$ by the obvious construction: keep any
$(1 - \gamma)n$ members in a space-optimal FNR-zero structure and drop the
rest. In the per-element model of this book a false-negative rate $\fnrate$
rejects each member independently, so the number rejected concentrates near
$\fnrate n$, and up to that concentration step the proposition applies with
$\gamma \approx \fnrate$. Tolerating false negatives therefore buys space only
linearly: the saving is about $\fnrate \log_2(1/\fprate)$ bits per element,
the cost of the members one chooses not to store. To leading order, no
two-sided representation blurs the identity of $A$ more cheaply than by
forgetting part of it.

\subsection*{The constructive question}

\Cref{thm:entropy-floor} forbids: no FNR-zero scheme beats $-\log_2 \fprate$
bits per element. That the floor can be met, up to lower-order terms, is also
established. \textcite{pagh-pagh-rao-2005} gave an optimal replacement for the
Bloom filter, and \textcite{dietzfelbinger-pagh-2008} reduced approximate
membership to retrieval\index{retrieval}, storing a $\log_2(1/\fprate)$-bit
fingerprint function in near-optimal space. Practical descendants come
close: xor filters\index{xor filter} are within about $23\%$ of the bound and
binary fuse filters\index{binary fuse filter} within about $13\%$
\parencite{graf-lemire-2020,graf-lemire-2022}, and ribbon
filters\index{ribbon filter} \parencite{dietzfelbinger-et-al-2026-ribbon}
closer still. For structures
that must accept insertions one at a time, as the Bloom filter does,
\textcite{lovett-porat-2010} prove a lower bound strictly above the static
floor at constant $\fprate$. The standard Bloom filter pays a factor of about
$1.44$ (\Cref{sec:bloom-gap}). Chapter~14 (\Cref{ch:optimal-bernoulli-sets})
presents the static perfect hash filter, a minimal perfect hash plus
fingerprints at $\log_2(1/\fprate) + c$ bits per element with $c \ge \log_2
e$, and a seed-search\index{seed search} construction, the Bernoulli Hash
Function\index{Bernoulli hash function}, which meets the floor in space but
needs on the order of $\fprate^{-n}$ trials to build; it is a reference point
rather than a practical structure.
