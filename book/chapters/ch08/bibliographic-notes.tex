\paragraph{The entropy manuscript.}
The entropy floor of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor} and the standard-Bloom
overhead calculation of \Cref{prop:bloom-overhead} both follow
\textcite{towell-bernoulli-entropy}. That manuscript is the primary
source for \Cref{sec:entropy-floor} and \Cref{sec:bloom-gap}: the
counting argument that converts the FNR-zero approximate-set
specification into a lower bound on representation length, the
asymptotic form $-n \log_2 \fprate$, and the comparison to the
$\log_2 e$ overhead of the standard Bloom filter are all taken from
it. The chapter's contribution beyond the manuscript is the
pedagogical scaffolding: the entropy refresher of
\Cref{sec:entropy-refresher} written for readers who have not seen
$\Entropy(X)$ before, the BSC bridge in \Cref{sec:bsc-capacity} that
ties the floor to Part~I's running channel example, and the explicit
forward pointers to chapter~14's construction that closes the gap.

\paragraph{Universal hashing and the counting argument.}
The counting-argument shape of the proof sketch in
\Cref{sec:entropy-floor} owes its structure to the universal hashing
program of \textcite{carter-wegman-1979}, who showed that one can
reason about the worst-case behavior of randomized hash-based data
structures by counting collisions across an explicitly chosen family
of hash functions rather than by appeal to the random-oracle model.
The same combinatorial style of bookkeeping is what makes
\Cref{thm:entropy-floor} go through: for every representation $R$ of
length $L$, count the accepting sets $A_R$ that $R$ can describe; for
every true set $A$ that has to be represented at FPR $\le \fprate$,
count the candidate $R$'s that work. The ratio is the bound. The
Carter-Wegman framework is the prior art that makes this kind of
argument standard rather than ad hoc.

\paragraph{Entropy and channel capacity.}
The information-theoretic background of \Cref{sec:entropy-refresher}
and \Cref{sec:bsc-capacity} is standard textbook material; the depth
reference for readers who want more than the working summary given
here is \textcite{cover-thomas}, whose first three chapters cover
$\Entropy(X)$, mutual information $\MutualInfo(X;Y)$, the binary
symmetric channel, channel capacity, and the source-coding theorem.
The BSC capacity result $\Capacity = 1 - \Entropy(\fprate)$ that
\Cref{sec:bsc-capacity} sketches is treated rigorously there, as is
the source-coding interpretation that underwrites
\Cref{thm:entropy-floor}: any encoding of a structured object with
inherent entropy rate $h$ bits per symbol needs at least $h$ bits per
symbol on average. The entropy floor is the specialization of that
generic statement to the FNR-zero approximate-set object whose
inherent rate is $-\log_2 \fprate$ bits per element. Shannon's
original 1948 paper \parencite{shannon-1948} remains the cleanest
short presentation of the source-coding idea for readers who prefer
to go to the source.

\paragraph{Compressed Bloom filters.}
\textcite{mitzenmacher-2002} observed that the standard Bloom filter
operating at the optimal $k = (m/n) \ln 2$ produces a bit array in
which roughly half the bits are $1$ and half are $0$. The array's
empirical entropy is therefore close to $1$ bit per array bit, and
arithmetic coding of the array recovers a representation whose length
matches the entropy floor of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor} rather than the
$\log_2 e$-inflated cost of \Cref{prop:bloom-overhead}. The
compressed Bloom filter trades the constant-time random-access query
of the bitmap for the smaller stored size, which is appropriate when
the filter is transmitted across a network (the original setting of
the 2002 paper) but inappropriate when the filter is queried in place
on disk or in cache. Mitzenmacher's paper is the right entry point
for readers who want to understand how much of the $1.44$ gap can be
closed without abandoning the bitmap structure, and it sets up the
contrast with chapter~14's BHF, which closes the gap by abandoning
the bitmap entirely in favor of a perfect-hash-based representation
whose query is again constant time. The Mitzenmacher and Upfal text
\parencite{mitzenmacher-upfal} cited in earlier chapters is a useful
companion for the probabilistic background.
