\section{The Bernoulli Framework as Error Model}
\label{sec:bernoulli-error-model}

\Cref{def:cipher-map} stated the cipher-map abstraction in terms of
two probabilities: a false-positive rate $\fprate$ and a false-negative
rate $\fnrate$. That choice of notation was not cosmetic. The
$\fprate$ and $\fnrate$ in the definition are the same objects that
chapters 1 and 4 introduced for the Bernoulli atom; the
$\fprate, \fnrate$ in a chained cipher-map composition obey the same
laws as the $\fprate, \fnrate$ in a chained noisy-classifier
composition. The point of this section is to make that equivalence
operational: the algebraic identities the framework spent earlier
chapters proving carry over to cipher maps without change.

\subsection*{The connection to Bernoulli rates}

Recall the atomic setup from chapter 1 \S\ref{sec:noisy-bit}. A
Bernoulli predicate $\tilde A : X \to \{0, 1\}$ has a per-query
behavior summarized by two numbers:
\[
  \fprate \;=\; \Prob{\tilde A(x) = 1 \,\Given\, x \notin A},
  \qquad
  \fnrate \;=\; \Prob{\tilde A(x) = 0 \,\Given\, x \in A}.
\]
Chapter 4 \S\ref{sec:bernoulli-bool-formal} formalized this through
two axioms (independence of error events across distinct inputs;
independence of per-block rates across blocks) and showed that the
two rates together with the input prevalence specify the joint
distribution of any finite sequence of queries.

A cipher map $\tilde f : \mathsf{Enc}(X) \to \mathsf{Enc}(Y)$ for a
Boolean predicate $f : X \to \{0, 1\}$ has exactly the same per-query
behavior. Let $x \in X$. The cipher map produces a (possibly
randomized) encrypted output $\tilde f(\mathsf{Enc}(x))$, which the
client decrypts to obtain a Boolean bit $\mathsf{Dec}(\tilde
f(\mathsf{Enc}(x))) \in \{0, 1\}$. From the client's point of view,
the only observable is the decrypted bit, and that bit follows a
Bernoulli distribution conditional on the plaintext input class:
\[
  \fprate \;=\; \Prob{\mathsf{Dec}(\tilde f(\mathsf{Enc}(x))) = 1
                      \,\Given\, f(x) = 0},
\]
\[
  \fnrate \;=\; \Prob{\mathsf{Dec}(\tilde f(\mathsf{Enc}(x))) = 0
                      \,\Given\, f(x) = 1}.
\]
The probability is taken over whatever randomness $\tilde f$ uses
internally (hash-family choice, ciphertext noise, key schedule). The
encryption and decryption maps drop out of the rate calculation
because $\mathsf{Dec} \circ \mathsf{Enc}$ is the identity on $X$ for
correctly decrypted outputs. What remains is a Bernoulli atom in the
sense of chapter 4, with the cipher-map error profile playing the
role of the atomic $(\fprate, \fnrate)$.

The framework's earlier work transfers directly. Every theorem in
chapters 1 through 15 that took $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ as input also
applies when those rates come from a cipher map rather than from a
Bloom filter or a classifier. The framework is indifferent to where
the error originates; it tracks only how the error propagates.

\subsection*{Per-query channel matrices}

The two-rate summary is convenient but limited. For a Boolean cipher
map, the per-query behavior is fully captured by a $2 \times 2$
\emph{channel matrix} $Q$, whose entries are conditional
probabilities indexed by (input class, output class):
\[
  Q \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \fprate & \fprate \\
    \fnrate & 1 - \fnrate
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
with rows indexed by the true plaintext class ($f(x) = 0$ in row 1,
$f(x) = 1$ in row 2) and columns indexed by the observed cipher-map
output. The Bloom-based cipher map of \S\ref{sec:what-is-cipher-map}
has $\fnrate = 0$ and so a matrix with a zero in the lower-left,
encoding the one-sided error of the Bloom construction.

For cipher maps whose outputs are not Boolean (encrypted
classifiers, encrypted set-of-results queries, encrypted ranked
retrieval), the $2 \times 2$ channel matrix generalizes to the
$K \times L$ channel matrix of chapter 9 \S\ref{sec:n-ary-channels}.
A cipher map for an n-ary classifier $f : X \to \{1, \ldots, L\}$
produces a per-query matrix $Q \in [0, 1]^{K \times L}$ with
\[
  Q_{k, \ell} \;=\; \Prob{\mathsf{Dec}(\tilde f(\mathsf{Enc}(x))) = \ell
                          \,\Given\, f(x) = k},
\]
where $K$ is the number of input plaintext classes and $L$ the
number of output classes. \Cref{def:n-ary-channel} gives the precise
definition. The Boolean case is the special case $K = L = 2$.

The channel-matrix representation has two virtues for cipher-map
analysis. First, it captures the full per-query behavior in a single
algebraic object that can be composed by multiplication. Second, it
makes contact with the information-theoretic machinery of chapter 8
(channel capacity, mutual information, the data-processing
inequality), which will be needed in \Cref{sec:maximizing-confidentiality}
to quantify confidentiality.

\subsection*{Chained cipher-map composition}

The reason a single channel matrix is the right summary becomes
clear when two cipher maps are composed. Suppose the client wants
to compute $h = f \circ g$ on encrypted data:
\[
  X \;\xrightarrow{g}\; Y \;\xrightarrow{f}\; Z.
\]
A cipher map $\tilde g$ computes $g$ on $\mathsf{Enc}(X)$ and returns
an intermediate $\mathsf{Enc}(Y)$. A cipher map $\tilde f$ then
computes $f$ on this intermediate and returns the final
$\mathsf{Enc}(Z)$. The client decrypts the final output and reads
$h(x)$, possibly with error.

The error in the final output depends on both stages. If $\tilde g$
makes a mistake at stage 1, the intermediate ciphertext encodes a
wrong $y' \ne g(x)$, and $\tilde f$ is then asked to compute
$f(y')$. Even if $\tilde f$ is errorless on its own inputs, the
composed output decrypts to $f(y') \ne f(g(x))$. The cipher-map
error of stage 1 propagates through stage 2 even when stage 2 is
exact.

This is exactly the situation that the chapter 4 Kronecker theorem
(\Cref{thm:kronecker}) and its n-ary generalization
(\Cref{thm:n-ary-kronecker}) were built to analyze. A composed
cipher map operating on the input distribution induced by the
stage-1 output is a sequence of two Bernoulli atoms whose joint
behavior factors. The factorization is matrix multiplication.

\subsection*{The composition theorem for cipher maps}

\begin{theorem}[Cipher-map composition]
\label{thm:cipher-composition}
Let $\tilde g : \mathsf{Enc}(X) \to \mathsf{Enc}(Y)$ and
$\tilde f : \mathsf{Enc}(Y) \to \mathsf{Enc}(Z)$ be cipher maps
realizing $g : X \to Y$ and $f : Y \to Z$, with per-query channel
matrices $Q_g \in [0, 1]^{|X| \times |Y|}$ and
$Q_f \in [0, 1]^{|Y| \times |Z|}$ respectively. Suppose
\Cref{ax:element-indep} (Axiom 1) holds: the cipher-map errors at
the two stages are mutually independent conditional on the inputs.
Then the composed cipher map $\tilde f \circ \tilde g$ realizes $h
= f \circ g$ with per-query channel matrix
\[
  Q_h \;=\; Q_g \, Q_f,
\]
where the product is ordinary matrix multiplication, with
$Q_h \in [0, 1]^{|X| \times |Z|}$.
\end{theorem}

The proof reuses the chapter 4 argument. The entry $(Q_g Q_f)_{x, z}$
sums over intermediate values: for each candidate $y \in Y$, the
probability that stage 1 produces $y$ (entry $(Q_g)_{x, y}$) times
the probability that stage 2 then produces $z$ (entry $(Q_f)_{y, z}$),
summed over $y$. The conditional-independence hypothesis is exactly
Axiom 1 applied to the two-stage sequence; the unconditional version
follows from the rate-deterministic regime that chapter 4 has used
since \Cref{thm:kronecker}.

For Boolean cipher maps the theorem reduces to a $2 \times 2$ matrix
product. For n-ary cipher maps the theorem reads against
\Cref{thm:n-ary-kronecker} with $m = 2$ stages instead of $m$
independent queries; the algebra is identical. The same result
governs any finite chain $\tilde f_n \circ \cdots \circ \tilde f_1$
by induction: the composed channel matrix is the product of the
per-stage matrices in left-multiplication order.

A direct consequence: the error rates of a chained cipher-map
computation grow predictably, not catastrophically. If each
$\tilde f_i$ has Boolean error $(\fprate_i, \fnrate_i)$, the
composed Boolean cipher map has rates bounded by chapter 3's
composition formula $1 - \prod_i (1 - \eta_i)$ on each error type.
This was Property 4 of the cipher-map design discussed in the
trapdoor-computing literature (the framework's composition law is
the same property the cryptographic side has long required).

\subsection*{The Bernoulli geometry applies}

The perfect-filter line of chapter 6 \S\ref{sec:perfect-filter-line}
gave a coarse classification of approximate sets by their
$(\fprate, \fnrate)$ position in the rate plane. A set with
$\fprate = 0$ (no false positives) and positive $\fnrate$ sits on the
horizontal axis; a set with $\fnrate = 0$ (no false negatives) and
positive $\fprate$ sits on the vertical axis; a set on the
perfect-filter line $\fnrate = 0$ is one-sided. \Cref{def:perfect-filter-line}
makes the classification formal.

The same classification carries over to cipher maps. A cipher map
with $\fnrate = 0$ is \emph{safe} in the sense that it never misses
a real positive output: a query for $x \in A$ always returns a
ciphertext that decrypts to the correct answer. The client never
loses real results to the cipher map's noise. The cost is that
$\fprate > 0$: some queries for $x \notin A$ also return positive
results. The Bloom-based cipher map of \S\ref{sec:what-is-cipher-map}
sits on this line; so does any cipher map built from a
perfect-filter-line approximate set by encryption.

A cipher map with $\fprate = 0$ sits on the opposite axis and is
\emph{exact when positive}: any output that decrypts to a positive
answer is correct, but some real positives are missed
($\fnrate > 0$). This regime is less common in cipher-map
constructions but appears in some randomized-encoding cipher maps
where the encoder occasionally rejects valid plaintexts. The
classification by perfect-filter-line position is geometric and
applies to cipher maps as a special case of approximate functions.

\subsection*{Worked example: two-stage encrypted classifier}

A representative composition appears in encrypted information
retrieval. The client wants to retrieve and rank documents matching
an encrypted query, without revealing the query to the server. The
computation has two stages.

\textbf{Stage 1: encrypted set membership.} The server holds an
encrypted Bloom filter $\tilde B_A$ representing a candidate set
$A \subset X$ (the documents that potentially match the query). The
client sends $\mathsf{Enc}(x)$; the server returns
$\tilde g(\mathsf{Enc}(x))$, an encryption of the membership bit. The
stage-1 channel matrix has $\fprate_1 = 0.01$ (a 1\% false-positive
rate for non-matching documents) and $\fnrate_1 = 0$ (Bloom
one-sidedness), giving
\[
  Q_g \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0.99 & 0.01 \\
    0 & 1
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]

\textbf{Stage 2: exact ranking on stage-1 positives.} For documents
flagged by stage 1, an exact (FHE-based) ranking computes a score.
The stage-2 channel matrix is the identity (no error introduced):
\[
  Q_f \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 0 \\
    0 & 1
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]

\textbf{Composed channel matrix.} \Cref{thm:cipher-composition}
gives
\[
  Q_h \;=\; Q_g \, Q_f \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0.99 & 0.01 \\
    0 & 1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 0 \\
    0 & 1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0.99 & 0.01 \\
    0 & 1
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
which is just $Q_g$ again, because stage 2 is exact. The composed
cipher map has $\fprate = 0.01$ and $\fnrate = 0$, inherited from
stage 1. If stage 2 had its own error (say $\fprate_2 = 0.005,
\fnrate_2 = 0$, an FPR-only stage), the product $Q_g Q_f$ would
combine the two:
\[
  Q_h \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0.99 \cdot 0.995 + 0.01 \cdot 0 & 0.99 \cdot 0.005 + 0.01 \cdot 1 \\
    0 \cdot 0.995 + 1 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 0.005 + 1 \cdot 1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \;\approx\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0.985 & 0.015 \\
    0 & 1
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
giving composed $\fprate \approx 0.015$, matching the chapter 3
composition formula $1 - (1 - 0.01)(1 - 0.005) \approx 0.015$. The
$\fnrate$ stays at zero because both stages are one-sided.

The lesson is that the framework's composition law applies to cipher
maps with no modification: matrix multiplication of channel
matrices, with Axiom 1 of chapter 4 supplying the conditional
independence that makes the product correct. The next section
unpacks the operational consequences: when the cipher map's $\fprate,
\fnrate$ are also confidentiality parameters, the same matrix
algebra governs how confidentiality accumulates across composed
queries.
