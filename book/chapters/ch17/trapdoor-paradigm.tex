\section{The Trapdoor Computing Paradigm}
\label{sec:trapdoor-paradigm}

The cipher map of \Cref{def:cipher-map} is the mathematical object; the
trapdoor computing paradigm is the operational setting in which it earns
its keep. The setting has three commitments: the data is encrypted before
it leaves the user, the computation runs entirely on the encrypted form,
and the result can be read only by a holder of a short secret key, the
trapdoor. The cipher map carries out the computation and the trapdoor
unlocks the answer; everything else, the plaintext records, the queries
that select among them, the intermediate results, stays hidden from any
party without the key.

\subsection*{The paradigm}

The paradigm predates the modern encryption-on-the-cloud framing.
Goldreich's work on software protection in the late 1980s asked how a
program could run on an untrusted machine without revealing what it
computed; the same question, in other language, drives oblivious RAM,
secure multiparty computation, and private information retrieval. What unites them is the
conviction that \emph{compute} and \emph{know} can be
separated: a machine can do useful work on data whose meaning it cannot
see. This program uses the name \emph{trapdoor computing}\index{trapdoor computing}
for one form of that separation, the one built from keyed approximate
structures. The system has two
distinguished resources, an encrypted dataset and a trapdoor key, both
needed to read anything meaningful: the dataset without the key is opaque,
the key without the dataset useless. Computation is the operation of
cipher maps on the encrypted dataset, producing encrypted outputs the key
holder can decrypt and no one else can. The Bernoulli framework enters at
decryption: because the cipher map is approximate, the decrypted output
is a Bernoulli observation rather than a deterministic fact, and the
chapter 4 axioms govern how it behaves across queries. The cryptographic
side sets up the problem (compute without revealing); the Bernoulli side
tracks the consequence (read with controlled error).

\subsection*{Two-party setup}

The cleanest instance has a \emph{server} holding the encrypted dataset
and running the cipher maps, and a \emph{client} holding the trapdoor and
issuing queries. The server sees ciphertexts in and ciphertexts out,
nothing else. The encryption keeps the query private; the cipher map's
bounded error keeps the dataset partially hidden even from a client who
probes many times. The standard threat model is the
\emph{honest-but-curious} server, which follows the protocol but records
the encrypted traffic for later analysis; bounding what that analysis can
recover needs a leakage model; the Bernoulli framework supplies the error
side of it. A malicious server that deviates
from the protocol needs cryptographic integrity checks beyond this
chapter. The client's recovery quality is set by the $(\fprate, \fnrate)$
profile: an $\fnrate = 0$ map loses no real positives but admits some
false ones, an $\fprate = 0$ map trusts every positive but misses some
real ones.

\subsection*{Confidentiality versus correctness}

The trade-off that distinguishes the paradigm from cleartext computation
is between confidentiality and correctness, but it is a partial one. False
positives give a reported $\top$ plausible deniability: an observer who
sees it cannot tell a real member from a spurious one with certainty.
Tight error rates remove that cover; loose rates add it at the client's
expense. The rates do not touch other channels of leakage: a
deterministic encoding answers the same query identically every time, so
repetition, frequency, and access patterns leak regardless of
$(\fprate, \fnrate)$, and only randomized encodings or leakage-suppressing
constructions address them \parencite{kamara-moataz-ohrimenko-2018}. The
$(\fprate, \fnrate)$ point is therefore one confidentiality knob among
several. On the perfect-filter line of \Cref{def:perfect-filter-line} an
$\fnrate = 0$ map puts its cover entirely in false positives (every real
positive returned, recall preserved), an $\fprate = 0$ map in false
negatives (every positive answer correct, but real positives missed); the
first suits recall-sensitive retrieval, the second precision-sensitive
contexts. \Cref{sec:maximizing-confidentiality} quantifies the first kind
of cover under an explicit observation model.

\subsection*{Multiple queries: leakage accumulates}

A single query reveals little; a million can reveal much. Under
\Cref{ax:element-indep} (Axiom 1) the error events at distinct queried
items are mutually independent, and the joint law of their reports
factors as the Kronecker product\index{Kronecker product} of the per-query channel matrices
(\Cref{thm:kronecker}). When the reports are conditionally independent
given the plaintext, the information they carry about it is at most the
sum of the per-report informations (the subadditivity of mutual
information for conditionally independent observations), so a per-query
leakage bound gives a budget linear in the number of queries. Two
qualifications keep this honest. Repeating a query against the same
structure is not a fresh draw: the frozen structure returns the same
answer, adding nothing about that item but revealing that the query
repeated. And querying the same item against several independently keyed
structures is a fresh draw each time, so a member, positive in all of
them, separates from a non-member, positive in each with probability
$\fprate$, geometrically fast (\Cref{prop:multi-query-leakage}).
Rotating the key resets the linkage between old and new queries but, if
old structures are retained by the observer, creates exactly those
independent instances.

\subsection*{Adaptive adversaries}

The independence the Kronecker analysis depends on fails when the stream
is adaptive: the adversary chooses each query from the encrypted outputs
seen so far. This is exactly the adversarial-input setting of
\Cref{sec:adversarial-inputs}, and the trapdoor paradigm inherits its
failure modes, with the encrypted outputs (not plaintext ones) driving
the strategy. The bounds shift from average-case (iid Kronecker products)
to worst-case: a map at $\fprate = 0.01$ on iid queries may reach
$\fprate$ near $1$ on a query crafted from the preceding outputs. The
response, as in chapter 16, is worst-case accounting: bound the maximum
plaintext bits any adversary extracts per query and budget around that.

Consider a trapdoor membership map on a plaintext set $A$ of $N = |A|$
records, with $\fprate = 2^{-k}$ and $\fnrate = 0$, realized by a
Bloom-style construction whose $k$ probe positions come from a keyed hash
family. If the family is weak (say linear, or otherwise learnable from
input-output pairs) an adversary who can submit chosen queries and see
the outcomes learns the key with a number of queries proportional to its
length, after which it can test membership offline and manufacture false
positives at will: total compromise from a small budget of crafted
queries, against an iid attacker who learns only what random queries
happen to reveal. With a pseudorandom function in place of the weak family
the key cannot be learned faster than by brute force, and the remaining
adaptive attack is the one \Cref{sec:adversarial-inputs} described:
collecting and replaying the false positives the adversary encounters.
The formal treatments of that setting
\parencite{naor-yogev-2015,clayton-patton-shrimpton-2019,filic-et-al-2022}
state what keyed constructions do and do not guarantee. Both stories are
needed in the trapdoor setting: the average case for sizing under benign
workloads, the worst case for security against adversarial ones.
