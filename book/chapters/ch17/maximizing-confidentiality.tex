\section{Maximizing Confidentiality}
\label{sec:maximizing-confidentiality}

The trapdoor-computing paradigm of \Cref{sec:trapdoor-paradigm}
named a duality the framework has not yet been asked to quantify:
the cipher-map error rates $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$ govern both
correctness and confidentiality. Looser rates make the server's
output less faithful to the plaintext function $f$, but they also
make the server's output less informative to an adversary who
inspects it. The previous sections treated the first half of that
duality; this section treats the second. The question is concrete:
given a cipher map at fixed $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ on a workload with
known prevalence $\pi$, how much information does the server's
output reveal about the underlying plaintext? The chapter~7 PPV
machinery answers the question without any further apparatus.

\subsection*{Confidentiality as a posterior}

The setting is the one \Cref{sec:trapdoor-paradigm} sketched. A
server holds an encrypted representation of a plaintext object
(say, a set $A$) and answers queries that pass through a cipher
map. An adversary observes the server's outputs over many queries
and tries to recover the plaintext. The adversary's recovery
problem is a posterior-inference problem: each observed output is
a noisy report about the plaintext, and Bayes' rule combines the
observations with whatever prior knowledge the adversary has.

For a single Boolean query of the form ``is $x \in A$?'' the
adversary's posterior is the most basic of inferential targets:
\[
  \Pr\bigl[\,x \in A \;\big\vert\; \tilde f(x) = \top\,\bigr].
\]
The adversary asks: given that the server reported $\top$, how
sure can I be that $x$ is actually in $A$? The reported $\top$
might be a true positive (the server faithfully reported a real
member) or a false positive (a non-member that the cipher map
spuriously labelled as a member). The posterior weights the two
possibilities by their likelihoods and by the prior probability
$\pi = \Pr[x \in A]$ that the queried element is a member of $A$
at all.

Reading the posterior is straightforward. If the value is close to
$1$, the server's report has effectively pinned down membership:
the adversary has learned almost everything. If the value is close
to $\pi$, the server's report has revealed nothing beyond the
prior: the report and the prior agree. The confidentiality of the
cipher map is the gap between the prior and the posterior, or,
equivalently, the share of the posterior probability mass that
remains \emph{outside} the adversary's claim. We take the formal
confidentiality measure to be
\[
  C \;=\; 1 - \PPV,
\]
the probability that the server's $\top$-report is a false positive
rather than a true positive. Confidentiality is high when most
$\top$-reports do not actually identify members of $A$, and it is
zero in the FHE limit, where every $\top$-report is correct and
membership is fully exposed.

\subsection*{The chapter~7 PPV formula is the confidentiality
formula}

The posterior $\Pr[\,x \in A \mid \tilde f(x) = \top\,]$ is exactly
the positive predictive value of \Cref{def:ppv}. The chapter~7
derivation took $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$ as the conditional error
rates of a noisy bit and $\pi$ as the workload prevalence, and
arrived at
\[
  \PPV
  \;=\;
  \frac{\pi (1 - \fnrate)}
       {\pi (1 - \fnrate) + (1 - \pi)\,\fprate}.
\]
The cipher-map setting reads the same formula in a new way. The
numerator $\pi(1 - \fnrate)$ is the rate at which the server
correctly reports $\top$ on a true member; the denominator adds
the rate $(1 - \pi)\fprate$ at which the server spuriously
reports $\top$ on a non-member. The posterior is the fraction
that is genuine. Confidentiality is the complementary fraction,
\[
  C
  \;=\;
  1 - \PPV
  \;=\;
  \frac{(1 - \pi)\,\fprate}
       {\pi (1 - \fnrate) + (1 - \pi)\,\fprate},
\]
the share of $\top$-reports that protect the plaintext rather
than reveal it. The expression is symmetric in the obvious way:
zero false positives gives $C = 0$, and a cipher map that
returns $\top$ unconditionally (informationally trivial) gives
$C = 1 - \pi$. Everything in between is a quantitative trade-off
that the framework already exposed in chapter~7.

The same machinery handles the negative case. For a $\bot$-report
the relevant measure is $\NPV$, and the confidentiality of a
$\bot$-report is $1 - \NPV$, the share of $\bot$-reports that
mistakenly hide a real member. In typical encrypted-search
workloads $\pi$ is small (most queries are non-members of any
given set), so the bulk of confidentiality comes from $\top$-side
ambiguity. The $\bot$-side analysis is the mirror.

\subsection*{Trading rate-budget for confidentiality}

The cipher-map designer faces a one-dimensional trade-off on the
perfect-filter line of \Cref{def:perfect-filter-line}. A cipher
map with $\fnrate = 0$ (faithful when reporting $\bot$, but
allowed false positives) sits on the $\fprate$-axis of the
Bernoulli square; the design parameter is the false-positive rate.
Increasing $\fprate$ raises the rate at which $(1 - \pi)\fprate$
non-members produce $\top$-reports, and the confidentiality
expression rises monotonically. Symmetrically, decreasing
$\fprate$ tightens the cipher map's reports and lowers
confidentiality. The geometric reading is that walking along the
perfect-filter line away from the origin trades correctness for
confidentiality, and walking back trades confidentiality for
correctness.

The trade-off is not free. Chapter~5's space formula gives the
storage cost of a Bloom filter as a decreasing function of
$\fprate$, so the more confidentiality the designer buys, the
less storage the structure occupies. The relation runs the
``wrong'' way from a naive intuition: \emph{lower-storage cipher
maps are more confidential, not less}. That is one of the
framework's small surprises, and it follows entirely from the
chapter~7 PPV formula without any cryptographic argument. The
explanation is that storage and accuracy are coupled through the
entropy bound of chapter~8, and accuracy is coupled to
confidentiality through chapter~7's posterior. The system has one
parameter and three coupled costs; the designer picks a point on
the perfect-filter line and reads off all three.

The design question ``what point on the line maximizes
confidentiality?'' has a degenerate answer in the abstract: walk
to $\fprate = 1$ and the cipher map returns $\top$
unconditionally, $\PPV = \pi$, $C = 1 - \pi$. The map is now
informationally trivial and operationally useless. The right
formulation constrains the operational error: maximize $C$
subject to $\fprate \le \fprate^{*}$ for some application-imposed
rate cap. The optimum is the cap itself, on the perfect-filter
line. The cipher-map designer's job is to set $\fprate^{*}$ at
the largest value the application can tolerate, and to read off
the corresponding $C$ from the PPV formula.

\subsection*{A worked example}

Take a trapdoor set on a workload with prevalence $\pi = 0.01$:
one query in a hundred is a real member of $A$. Set the cipher
map's rates to $\fprate = 0.05$ and $\fnrate = 0$, which puts
the map on the perfect-filter line at the $\fprate$-axis. The
PPV formula gives
\[
  \PPV
  \;=\;
  \frac{0.01 \cdot 1}
       {0.01 \cdot 1 + 0.99 \cdot 0.05}
  \;=\;
  \frac{0.01}{0.0595}
  \;\approx\;
  0.168.
\]
The corresponding confidentiality is $C \approx 0.832$. Read
operationally: when the server reports ``$x \in A$,'' the
adversary's posterior assigns $\sim 16.8\%$ probability to the
hypothesis that $x$ is genuinely in $A$ and $\sim 83.2\%$ to the
hypothesis that the report is a false positive. The membership
question is far from settled by a single query.

Compare with the FHE baseline of \Cref{sec:fhe-sidebar}. An FHE
scheme reports membership with zero error: $\fprate = \fnrate =
0$, so $\PPV = 1$ identically, and $C = 0$. Every report is
correct, and every report fully reveals the underlying plaintext
fact. The confidentiality contrast is stark: the cipher-map
operator pays a $5\%$ per-query false-positive rate and receives
in return an $83\%$ confidentiality margin. The FHE operator
pays a $10{,}000\times$ space and time overhead and receives a
zero confidentiality margin. Whether the trade is worth taking
depends on the application, but the framework gives the design
problem a quantitative shape.

The example also makes the workload dependence concrete. Hold
$(\fprate, \fnrate) = (0.05, 0)$ fixed and consider a different
workload at $\pi = 0.5$. The PPV formula gives
\[
  \PPV
  \;=\;
  \frac{0.5}{0.5 + 0.5 \cdot 0.05}
  \;=\;
  \frac{0.5}{0.525}
  \;\approx\;
  0.952,
\]
and the confidentiality drops to $C \approx 0.048$. The same
cipher map is confidential on a rare-positive workload and
revealing on a balanced workload. The framework keeps the
parameters separate: the cipher map carries $(\fprate, \fnrate)$;
the workload carries $\pi$; the confidentiality $C(\fprate,
\fnrate, \pi)$ is a single function the designer can plot and
read. Chapter~7 did the algebra; chapter~17 inherits it.
