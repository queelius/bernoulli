\section{Boolean Algebra Over Trapdoor Sets}
\label{sec:boolean-trapdoor}

The trapdoor cipher maps of \Cref{sec:trapdoor-paradigm} are useful in
isolation and more useful combined. A common request in encrypted search
is the conjunctive query, \emph{is $x$ in $A$ and in $B$?}, asked of two
trapdoor sets on the same server. The answer requires Boolean reasoning
over encrypted representations, and this section asks how Boolean algebra
behaves when its operands are trapdoor sets. The framework's
set-composition rules carry over, with the same three-region subtlety
chapter 6 raised for plaintext approximate sets.

\subsection*{Trapdoor sets}

\begin{definition}[Trapdoor set]
\label{def:trapdoor-set}
Let $A \subseteq X$ be a plaintext set and $k$ a secret key. A
\emph{trapdoor set}\index{cipher set} for $A$ under $k$, written $\tilde A$, is an
encrypted data structure with a membership cipher map $\tilde m_A :
\mathsf{Enc}_k(X) \to \mathsf{Enc}_k(\{0,1\})$ such that
$\mathsf{Dec}_k(\tilde m_A(\mathsf{Enc}_k(x))) = \mathbf{1}[x \in A]$ with
bounded rates $\fprate_A, \fnrate_A$ in the sense of
\Cref{def:cipher-map}. Without $k$ the membership query is
computationally infeasible; with $k$ it is as cheap as a plaintext
lookup.
\end{definition}

The Bloom filter of \Cref{sec:bloom-from-scratch} with keyed probe
positions is canonical (the secure index of \textcite{goh-2003}): the
server holds the bit array, the client sends the $k$ keyed positions for
its query, and the server returns the AND of those bits. The map has $\fprate_A = (1 - e^{-kn/m})^k$ and $\fnrate_A
= 0$, so $\tilde A$ sits at $(\fprate_A, 0)$ on the perfect-filter line of
\Cref{def:perfect-filter-line}. A perfect-hash-filter trapdoor set
(chapter 15) sits at $(2^{-r}, 0)$; a counting variant supports deletions
but can produce false negatives when counters overflow or a non-member is
deleted \parencite{guo-et-al-2010}. What they share is the structure of
\Cref{def:trapdoor-set}: encrypted representation, key-dependent query,
bounded Bernoulli error.

\subsection*{Boolean operations and their rates}

The three primitive operations lift to the trapdoor setting, the server
computing on encrypted representations. \emph{Union}\index{union} returns the
encrypted OR of the two membership bits (a bitwise OR of the arrays when
the scheme is additively homomorphic over bits); \emph{intersection} the
encrypted AND (run both queries, combine with a homomorphic AND, since
the bitwise AND of two Bloom arrays is not the filter of the
intersection); \emph{complement} the flipped bit, which by
\Cref{prop:complement-axis-swap} moves the result to the opposite axis.

These are \emph{parallel} combinations of two membership tests on one
query, not the \emph{sequential} chains \Cref{thm:cipher-composition}
governs, so their rates are the set-composition rates of chapter 6 rather
than a channel-matrix product. For two FNR-zero trapdoor sets under
union, a query outside $A \cup B$ is outside both, a single region, so
\[
  \fprate_{A \cup B} \;=\; 1 - (1 - \fprate_A)(1 - \fprate_B),
  \qquad
  \fnrate_{A \cup B} \;=\; 0,
\]
the chapter 3 monotone formula (\Cref{thm:composition}). Intersection\index{intersection} is
subtler.\index{composition!of sets} A query outside $A \cap B$ falls into one of three regions, $A
\setminus B$, $B \setminus A$, or outside $A \cup B$, and the false
positive arises differently in each: in $A \setminus B$ the $A$-test is
correct and only the $B$-test must err (rate $\fprate_B$), in $B \setminus
A$ only the $A$-test (rate $\fprate_A$), and only outside $A \cup B$ must
both err (rate $\fprate_A \fprate_B$). By \Cref{prop:intersection-fpr-fnr}
the rate is the partition-weighted sum \eqref{eq:intersection-fpr},
\[
  \fprate_{A \cap B} \;=\; w_1 \fprate_B + w_2 \fprate_A
  + w_3 \fprate_A \fprate_B,
  \qquad
  \fnrate_{A \cap B} \;=\; 0,
\]
with $w_1, w_2, w_3$ the masses of the three regions among non-members of
$A \cap B$. The weighted sum is a prior-averaged rate\index{prior-averaged rate}: the
rate a non-member drawn from the stated workload sees. The per-region
rates $\fprate_B$, $\fprate_A$, $\fprate_A \fprate_B$ are the conditional
rates of individual queries, and it is those, not the average, that
compose exactly through further operations
(\Cref{prop:latent-propagation}, \Cref{ex:two-rate-counterexample}). The bare product $\fprate_A \fprate_B$ is the $w_3 \to 1$
limit, where queries land almost entirely outside $A \cup B$; it is the
best case, not the general one. The conditional independence behind the
$w_3$ term is Axiom 1 applied to the two trapdoor sets, automatic when
their keys are generated independently.

The distinction matters for the conjunctive precision often claimed for
encrypted search. A $k$-way conjunction reaches $\prod_i \fprate_i$, an
exponential sharpening, only in the sparse limit where queries miss every
set; in general the rate is dominated by the largest single-miss region,
$\max_i \fprate_i$ weighted by the mass of queries inside all sets but
one. Deep conjunctions are highly precise on a workload of true negatives
and only modestly precise on a workload of near-misses, and the design
must know which regime it is in.

\subsection*{Cancellation escapes the perfect-filter line}

The composition above kept FNR-zero operands FNR-zero, but that is not a
general property: as soon as a complement enters, or operands sit on
opposite axes, the result can leave the perfect-filter line, the
cancellation phenomenon chapter 6 \Cref{sec:cancellation} flagged. Take a
trapdoor union $\tilde A \cup \tilde B$ at $(\fprate_{A \cup B}, 0)$ and
complement it: by \Cref{prop:complement-axis-swap} the complement of an
FNR-zero set is FPR-zero, so $\tilde{\complement(A \cup B)}$ sits at $(0,
\fprate_{A \cup B})$. Now intersect with a third FNR-zero set $\tilde C$
at $(\fprate_C, 0)$. The intersection mixes axes, and the three-region
rate tells the true story. Its false-negative rate is
\[
  \fnrate_{\complement(A \cup B) \cap C}
  \;=\; 1 - (1 - \fprate_{A \cup B})(1 - \fnrate_C)
  \;=\; \fprate_{A \cup B},
\]
since $\fnrate_C = 0$. Its false-positive rate is \emph{not} zero, despite
one operand being FPR-zero: a query in $\complement(A \cup B) \setminus C$
(in the first operand's set, outside the second, hence outside the
intersection) is a false positive whenever the $C$-test errs, at rate
$\fprate_C$, so by \eqref{eq:intersection-fpr}
\[
  \fprate_{\complement(A \cup B) \cap C}
  \;=\; w_1 (1 - \fprate_{A \cup B}) \fprate_C \;>\; 0.
\]
The composed trapdoor set carries \emph{both} a positive false-positive
rate and a positive false-negative rate\index{cancellation}: it sits in the interior of the
Bernoulli square, off the perfect-filter line entirely, not merely on the
opposite axis. This is the cancellation phenomenon chapter 6 documented,
lifted intact to the trapdoor setting, and sharper than a naive
bare-product reading suggests: mixing axes does not just swap one-sidedness
but dissolves it. Designing trapdoor-set Boolean combinations requires the
same care as the plaintext case, tracking operand axes and the three
regions through every composition.

\subsection*{Worked example: conjunctive query}

Take two trapdoor Bloom filters $\tilde A, \tilde B$, each at $\fprate =
0.01$, $\fnrate = 0$, and the query $q(x) = \mathbf{1}[x \in A \cap B]$.
The client sends $\mathsf{Enc}_k(x)$; the server queries both, combines
the encrypted bits under a homomorphic AND, and returns the result, which
the client decrypts. The intersection is FNR-zero (both component queries
return $1$ on a true positive), and by \eqref{eq:intersection-fpr} the
false-positive rate is
\[
  \fprate_{A \cap B} \;=\; w_1 (0.01) + w_2 (0.01) + w_3 (0.01)^2.
\]
On a workload of true negatives ($w_3 \to 1$) this is the product $10^{-4}$,
two orders of magnitude tighter than either component, the precision
gain conjunctive encrypted search is built on. On a workload where the
query is known to lie in one set but tested for the other ($w_1$ or $w_2$
near $1$, the natural case when $A$ and $B$ are correlated keyword sets),
the rate is about $0.01$, the single component's rate, and the
conjunction buys little. The honest design figure is the weighted sum,
read from the application's query distribution, not the optimistic product
alone. The same set-composition law that governs plaintext Bloom-filter
conjunctions governs the trapdoor lift; the next section turns the
rate-algebra story into a confidentiality-algebra story, asking how much
the server recovers from the conjunctive query stream.
