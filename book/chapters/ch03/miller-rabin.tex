\section{Miller-Rabin: A Worked Walkthrough}
\label{sec:miller-rabin}

Part I has built up a small but complete toolkit: $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$
instances with error rates $(\fprate, \fnrate)$, the Boolean algebra over
noisy bits (\Cref{sec:and-under-noise}), and the composition theorem that
governs how error rates accumulate when instances are combined
(\Cref{sec:composition-theorem}). This section deploys that toolkit in full
against a canonical example from number theory.

The Miller-Rabin primality test is one of the most widely used randomized
algorithms in practice. It decides, with adjustable confidence, whether an
integer $n$ is prime. We will show that the algorithm IS a $k$-fold
$\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ composition. Every formula in the Miller-Rabin
analysis---the per-round false-positive bound $\fprate \leq 1/4$, the
$k$-round bound $\fprate_k \leq (1/4)^k$, and the design rule ``choose $k$
large enough''---falls directly out of the framework developed in §§3.1--3.3.
The algorithm contributes one non-trivial mathematical fact; everything else
is the composition theorem applied.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Step 1: The witness predicate}

Fix a positive odd integer $n > 3$ whose primality we wish to decide. A
single \emph{Miller-Rabin witness test} proceeds as follows.

Write $n - 1 = 2^r \cdot d$ with $d$ odd and $r \geq 1$. Choose a witness
$a \in \{2, 3, \ldots, n-2\}$ uniformly at random. Compute the sequence
\[
  a^d \bmod n,\quad a^{2d} \bmod n,\quad a^{4d} \bmod n,\quad \ldots,
  \quad a^{2^r d} \bmod n.
\]
Declare $n$ \textbf{composite} (the definitive outcome) if none of these
values equals $\pm 1 \pmod{n}$, or more precisely: if $a^d \not\equiv \pm 1$
and $a^{2^j d} \not\equiv -1$ for all $j \in \{1, \ldots, r-1\}$.
Declare $n$ \textbf{probably prime} (the uncertain outcome) otherwise.

The test returns one of two verdicts:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{``composite'':} $n$ is definitely not prime. No false negatives
    here---a prime number always passes the test (Fermat's little theorem
    guarantees $a^{n-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$ for any $a$ coprime to $n$, and
    the sequence of square roots must eventually exhibit a trivial square root
    of 1).
  \item \textbf{``probably prime'':} $n$ might be prime, or might be a
    composite that ``fooled'' witness $a$. The latter is possible for
    composites with large \emph{strong-liar sets}.
\end{itemize}

This is already the shape of a $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance, as we make
precise next.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Step 2: Characterizing the test as $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ with
FNR${}=0$, FPR${}\leq 1/4$}

The witness test is a predicate on an integer $n$:
\[
  W_a(n) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \text{composite} & \text{if } a \text{ certifies compositeness,} \\
    \text{probably prime} & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\]
Identify ``composite'' with $\bot$ (false, $n$ is not prime) and ``probably
prime'' with $\top$ (positive result, $n$ might be prime). The true value of
the predicate is whether $n$ is actually prime.

In these terms, two error events are possible:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{False negative ($\fnrate$):} $n$ is prime but $W_a(n) =
    \text{composite}$. This cannot happen: if $n$ is prime, then $a^d \equiv
    \pm 1 \pmod{n}$ or some $a^{2^j d} \equiv -1 \pmod{n}$, and the test
    correctly declares $n$ probably prime. So $\fnrate = 0$.
  \item \textbf{False positive ($\fprate$):} $n$ is composite but $W_a(n) =
    \text{probably prime}$. This can happen when $a$ is a \emph{strong liar}
    for $n$: an element of $\{2, \ldots, n-2\}$ that passes the Miller-Rabin
    test even though $n$ is composite.
\end{itemize}

The Miller-Rabin theorem states that for any composite $n$, the number of
strong liars in $\{2, \ldots, n-2\}$ is at most $(n-3)/4$. A uniformly
random witness therefore satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \fprate \;\leq\; \frac{1}{4}.
  \label{eq:mr-fpr}
\end{equation}
The $1/4$ bound is sharp: some composites (products of two primes of similar
size) come close to having $(n-3)/4$ strong liars.

The single-round witness test is therefore a $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance
with $\fnrate = 0$ and $\fprate \leq 1/4$ (see \Cref{def:bernoulli-bool}
for the formal type).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Predicate recasting: fitting Axiom 1.}
There is a subtle point here that deserves explicit attention, foreshadowed in
\Cref{sec:axiom-1} and \Cref{ax:element-indep}.

The natural description of the Miller-Rabin test is: fix a single candidate
$n$, and draw $k$ independent random witnesses $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_k \in
\{2, \ldots, n-2\}$. In this framing, randomness enters via the witness
draw, not via the input. The input $n$ is \emph{fixed} across all rounds.
But Axiom 1 is a statement about distinct \emph{input elements} to a single
predicate: the error events at distinct inputs are independent.

To apply Axiom 1 cleanly, we recast the test. Define a new predicate
\[
  \tilde{P}\colon \{2, \ldots, n-2\} \;\to\; \{\text{composite},\,
  \text{probably prime}\}
\]
where $\tilde{P}(a)$ is the verdict of the witness test applied to $n$ with
witness $a$. Now the witness pool $\{2, \ldots, n-2\}$ plays the role of the
\emph{universe}, and distinct witnesses $a_i \neq a_j$ are distinct input
elements. Axiom 1 is the claim that the error event at $a_i$ (the event ``$a_i$
is a strong liar for $n$'') is independent of the error event at $a_j$ (the
event ``$a_j$ is a strong liar for $n$''). Whether this independence actually
holds for a given composite $n$ is a fact about the structure of $n$'s
strong-liar set, not something that Axiom 1 grants by fiat. We return to this
point in Step 5. What the recasting achieves is structural: distinct witnesses
are now distinct input elements, and the Axiom 1 framework applies to the
recast predicate $\tilde{P}$ in the same way it applies to any other
$\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ predicate.

The $k$-round Miller-Rabin test is then the AND of $k$ evaluations of
$\tilde{P}$: declare ``probably prime'' only if all $k$ witnesses return
``probably prime''. This is a $k$-fold AND of $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$
instances, exactly the operation analyzed in \Cref{sec:and-under-noise}.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Step 3: Applying the composition theorem for $k$ rounds}

With the predicate recasting in place, the composition theorem
(\Cref{sec:composition-theorem}, \Cref{thm:composition}) applies directly.

Each round is a $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance with $\fnrate_i = 0$ and
$\fprate_i \leq 1/4$. The $k$-fold AND has:

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{False-negative rate of the $k$-round test.}
Since $\fnrate_i = 0$ for all rounds, any prime will pass every round
correctly. The AND of zero-false-negative stages has zero false-negative rate:
\[
  \fnrate_k \;=\; 1 - \prod_{i=1}^k (1 - \fnrate_i) \;=\; 1 - 1^k \;=\; 0.
\]
The $k$-round test never misidentifies a prime as composite, regardless of $k$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{False-positive rate of the $k$-round test.}
For the FNR${}=0$ (one-sided error) case, the composition theorem applies
without the BSC cancellation caveat noted in \Cref{sec:composition-theorem}:
a false positive at any round cannot be ``corrected'' by a later round, because
subsequent rounds with $\fnrate = 0$ correctly pass $\top$ inputs through.
The $k$-round false-positive rate satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \fprate_k
  \;\leq\; \fprate^k
  \;\leq\; \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^k.
  \label{eq:mr-k-round}
\end{equation}
Here the first inequality uses the fact that, when $\fnrate_i = 0$, the
composition theorem's ``at least one error'' formula for the false-positive
rate reduces to the product $\prod_{i=1}^k \fprate_i$, since each round
making a false positive is the only way the chain can make a false positive.

To see this: the $k$-round AND declares ``probably prime'' if and only if every
round declares ``probably prime''. For a composite $n$, this happens if and
only if every witness $a_i$ is a strong liar. By the Axiom 1 recasting, the
events ``$a_i$ is a strong liar'' are independent (under the worst-case
assumption over $n$). The probability that all $k$ witnesses are strong liars
is at most $(1/4)^k$.

Equivalently: the correctness probability of the $k$-round test (the
probability of correctly declaring a composite as composite) is at least
$1 - (1/4)^k$, and the correctness probability of correctly identifying a
prime as probably prime is exactly 1.

The three key error bounds for standard choices of $k$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\hline
$k$ & $\fprate_k \leq (1/4)^k$ & Approximate \\
\hline
$1$ & $1/4$         & $0.250$ \\
$5$ & $(1/4)^5$     & $\approx 9.8 \times 10^{-4}$ \\
$20$ & $(1/4)^{20}$ & $\approx 9.1 \times 10^{-13}$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
At $k = 20$ rounds, the probability of a false positive is below
$10^{-12}$: for practical purposes, the test is definitive.

\subsection*{Step 4: Pseudocode and numerical example}

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
  \caption{Miller-Rabin primality test ($k$ rounds)}
  \label{alg:miller-rabin}
  \SetKwInOut{Input}{input}
  \SetKwInOut{Output}{output}
  \SetKwProg{func}{function}{}{}
  \Input{%
    $n$: odd integer $> 3$; \quad
    $k$: number of rounds ($k \geq 1$)%
  }
  \Output{%
    \textsc{composite} (definitive) or \textsc{probably-prime} (with FPR $\leq (1/4)^k$)%
  }
  \func{\textsc{MillerRabin}($n$, $k$)}{
    write $n - 1 = 2^r \cdot d$ with $d$ odd\;
    \For{$i \gets 1$ \KwTo $k$}{
      choose $a$ uniformly at random from $\{2, \ldots, n-2\}$\;
      $x \gets a^d \bmod n$\;
      \If{$x = 1$ \textbf{or} $x = n-1$}{
        \textbf{continue} \tcp*{witness $a$ passes; try next}
      }
      \For{$j \gets 1$ \KwTo $r - 1$}{
        $x \gets x^2 \bmod n$\;
        \If{$x = n - 1$}{
          \textbf{break} \tcp*{found $-1$; witness passes}
        }
      }
      \lIf{$x \neq n - 1$}{\Return \textsc{composite}}
    }
    \Return \textsc{probably-prime}\;
  }
\end{algorithm}

\noindent\textbf{Worked example: $n = 341 = 11 \times 31$.}

We choose $n = 341$ because it is a pedagogically useful case: $341$ is
a \emph{Fermat pseudoprime to base 2}, meaning the naive Fermat test
($2^{n-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$?) would incorrectly pass it. Indeed,
$2^{340} \equiv 1 \pmod{341}$, so Fermat fails. Miller-Rabin does not.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Setup.} We have $n - 1 = 340$. Factoring out powers of 2:
$340 = 4 \times 85 = 2^2 \times 85$. So $r = 2$ and $d = 85$.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Witness $a = 2$.} Compute the sequence:
\begin{align*}
  a^d \bmod n &= 2^{85} \bmod 341 = 32. \\
  a^{2d} \bmod n &= 32^2 \bmod 341 = 1024 \bmod 341 = 1.
\end{align*}
The test checks: is $a^d \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{n}$? We have $32 \not\equiv 1$
and $32 \not\equiv 340 \equiv -1 \pmod{341}$. So we proceed to squaring.

Next, is $a^{2d} \equiv -1 \pmod{n}$? We have $1 \not\equiv 340 \pmod{341}$.
We have exhausted all $r - 1 = 1$ squarings without finding $-1$.

Therefore, $2^{85} \equiv 32 \pmod{341}$ with $32 \neq \pm 1$, and
$32^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{341}$. This is a \emph{non-trivial square root of 1}
modulo $341$: a value $x \neq \pm 1$ with $x^2 \equiv 1$. The existence
of a non-trivial square root of 1 is a certificate of compositeness (if
$n$ were prime, the only square roots of 1 in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$
would be $\pm 1$). The algorithm returns \textsc{composite}.

\medskip
Why does $341$ fool Fermat but not Miller-Rabin? The Fermat test checks
$2^{340} \equiv 1 \pmod{341}$, which holds---$341$ is a Fermat
pseudoprime. Miller-Rabin is stronger: it checks not just $a^{n-1} \equiv
1$, but whether the sequence of square roots leading to 1 goes through
$-1$ at some point. For a prime $p$, every square root of 1 in
$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is $\pm 1$. For the composite $341$, the Chinese
Remainder Theorem produces elements that square to 1 without being $\pm 1$
modulo $341$; witness $a = 2$ exposes one of them.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Error bounds for $n = 341$.} For a composite with $r=2$,
$d=85$, and $k$ rounds:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\hline
$k$ & $\fprate_k \leq (1/4)^k$ & Interpretation \\
\hline
$1$ & $0.250$ & 1-in-4 chance of passing a random witness \\
$5$ & $\approx 9.8 \times 10^{-4}$ & Better than 1-in-1000 \\
$20$ & $\approx 9.1 \times 10^{-13}$ & Effectively certain \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In practice, $k = 20$ is the standard for cryptographic applications. At
that level the false-positive probability is negligible compared to hardware
error rates.

\subsection*{Step 5: The unification thesis}

We are now in a position to state plainly what this section has shown.

Miller-Rabin primality testing is not a separate technique that happens to use
randomness. It is an instance of the Bernoulli framework applied to a specific
mathematical predicate.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The single-round witness test is a $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance
    with $\fnrate = 0$ and $\fprate \leq 1/4$. This follows from the
    Miller-Rabin theorem about strong-liar counts, not from the framework
    itself.

  \item The $k$-round test is the $k$-fold AND of $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$
    witnesses. This is the composition of \Cref{sec:and-under-noise}.

  \item The bound $\fprate_k \leq (1/4)^k$ is the composition theorem
    (\Cref{thm:composition}) applied to the one-sided-error (FNR${}= 0$)
    special case.

  \item The design rule ``choose $k$ large enough to drive $\fprate_k$ below a
    target $\epsilon$'' is the composition theorem solved for $k$:
    $k \geq \log_{1/4}(\epsilon) = -2\log_2(\epsilon)$.

  \item The correctness of this design rule is guaranteed by the framework
    given one input from the number theory: $\fprate \leq 1/4$ per round.
\end{itemize}

Every structural step above was a consequence of the framework. The only
fact that Miller-Rabin contributes is the $1/4$ bound on the per-round
false-positive rate---that is the mathematical content of the Miller-Rabin
theorem. The rest is Boolean algebra over noisy bits.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{On the independence assumption.}
The analysis in Step 3 applied Axiom 1 to the recast predicate $\tilde{P}$,
treating the error events at distinct witnesses as independent. It is worth
being precise about what this means.

The $(1/4)^k$ bound is a \emph{worst-case} bound over all composites $n$. It
says: for any composite $n$, at most $1/4$ of witnesses are strong liars.
Therefore, if we sample witnesses uniformly and independently, the probability
that all $k$ land in the strong-liar set is at most $(1/4)^k$.

The independence here is \emph{over the uniform random draw of witnesses}, not
a statement about the structure of the strong-liar set itself. For a given
composite $n$ with a fixed strong-liar set $L_n \subseteq \{2, \ldots, n-2\}$,
the event ``$a_i \in L_n$'' and the event ``$a_j \in L_n$'' are independent
when $a_i$ and $a_j$ are drawn independently and uniformly from
$\{2, \ldots, n-2\}$, because membership of a uniform random element in $L_n$
is independent across draws. This is sampling-with-replacement independence,
which holds by construction, not by any special property of $L_n$.

So the application of Axiom 1 here is clean: we draw distinct witnesses, and
the error events are independent by the uniform sampling protocol. The bound
$(1/4)^k$ uses both the $|L_n| \leq (n-3)/4$ size bound (the mathematical
content of Miller-Rabin) and the uniform independent sampling (the protocol
that makes Axiom 1 applicable to the recast predicate $\tilde{P}$). Neither
alone is sufficient; both are needed.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{What this section was not.}
We have not given the most general treatment of randomized primality tests.
The Las Vegas versus Monte Carlo distinction---and the question of what a
\emph{zero-error} primality test would require---belongs to chapter 11
(\Cref{ch:randomized-algorithms}), where the Bernoulli framework connects to
the full theory of randomized algorithms. The present section had one goal:
to show that the core of Miller-Rabin is the composition theorem, nothing more.

\subsection*{Looking ahead}

The Miller-Rabin example sits at one end of a spectrum. At the other end
sits the Bloom filter: also a $k$-fold $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ composition,
also with a per-round false-positive rate that the composition theorem drives
down, but drawn from a different application domain (approximate set
membership rather than primality). \Cref{sec:bridge-to-sets} will make this
connection explicit and show how the Boolean algebra of this chapter
generalizes, in a single structural step, to the algebra of approximate sets.
