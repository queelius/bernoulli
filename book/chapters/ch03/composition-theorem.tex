\section{The Composition Theorem}
\label{sec:composition-theorem}

The AND's false-negative rate \eqref{eq:and-fnr} and the OR's false-positive
rate \eqref{eq:or-fpr-direct} are both instances of one pattern: the
probability that a chain produces no error factors as a product. This section
states it as a theorem and isolates the regime where it is exact.

\begin{theorem}[Composition theorem]
\label{thm:composition}
Let $\hat{b}_1, \ldots, \hat{b}_k$ be the outputs of $k$ chained
identity-with-error stages, stage $i$ correct with probability $1 - \eta_i$,
with error events independent across stages. If any single stage error
corrupts the end-to-end output (the identity-with-error regime), then the
end-to-end correctness probability and error rate are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:composition-correctness}
  \tnrate_{\mathrm{total}} = \prod_{i=1}^{k}(1 - \eta_i),
  \qquad
  \eta_{\mathrm{total}} = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{k}(1 - \eta_i).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By induction on $k$. The case $k = 1$ is immediate. For the step, the chain of
$k$ stages is correct iff the first $k - 1$ are correct (probability
$\prod_{i<k}(1 - \eta_i)$, by hypothesis) and stage $k$ is correct (probability
$1 - \eta_k$); by independence these multiply, giving $\prod_{i \le k}(1 -
\eta_i)$. Complementation gives $\eta_{\mathrm{total}}$.
\end{proof}

The inductive step uses ``if and only if both,'' which holds only when any
single error is fatal. If a later stage can accidentally undo an earlier error,
the formula overcounts; this is the BSC caveat below.

\subsection*{Which rate each direction governs}

The theorem applies to the error direction in which a single stage error
propagates. For the conjunction of $k$ instances (report $\top$ iff all do), a
true $\top$ survives only if every stage reports $\top$, so correctness
multiplies and the false-negative rate is
\[
  \fnrate_{\wedge,k} = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{k}(1 - \fnrate_i),
\]
the theorem with $\eta_i = \fnrate_i$. Dually, the disjunction's false-positive
rate is $\fprate_{\vee,k} = 1 - \prod_i (1 - \fprate_i)$.

The opposite direction is the \emph{dual}: the conjunction false-positives on
an all-negative input only if \emph{every} stage false-positives, so errors
multiply and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:composition-theorem}
  \fprate_{\wedge,k} = \prod_{i=1}^{k} \fprate_i
\end{equation}
in the all-negative regime. This is the mechanism behind Miller-Rabin: its $k$
rounds all test the same composite, the only true input is $\bot$, and the
false-positive rate is the product $\prod_i \fprate_i \le (1/4)^k$
(\Cref{sec:miller-rabin}). The two formulas are not interchangeable: the
conjunction's false-negative rate is $1 - \prod(1 - \fnrate_i)$, but its
false-positive rate is the product $\prod \fprate_i$, not $1 - \prod(1 -
\fprate_i)$.

\subsection*{The BSC caveat}

The theorem computes the probability that at least one stage errs. For the
identity-with-error and one-sided cases this equals the end-to-end error rate,
but the BSC violates the ``single error is fatal'' premise: two flips cancel.
Two BSCs of crossover $\fprate$ in series have end-to-end crossover
$2\fprate - 2\fprate^2$ (the odd-flip probability,
\Cref{eq:bsc-composition}), whereas the theorem's $1 - (1 - \fprate)^2 =
2\fprate - \fprate^2$ counts the both-flip event as an error even though it
returns the correct value. The two agree only when cancellation is impossible:
one-sided channels ($\fnrate = 0$ or $\fprate = 0$), and the AND and OR gates
in their respective monotone directions. For the BSC, the matrix product of
\Cref{sec:channel-composition} is the correct tool, not this theorem. The two
formulas answer different questions about different models.

\subsection*{The Miller-Rabin regime, worked}

Take $k$ one-sided instances ($\fnrate_i = 0$), the Bloom-filter and
Miller-Rabin setting, queried in the all-negative regime, with false-positive
rates $\fprate_i$. The conjunction has $\fnrate_{\wedge,k} = 0$ (no stage ever
suppresses a true $\top$), and its false-positive rate is the product
\eqref{eq:composition-theorem}. For $\fprate = (0.20, 0.15, 0.10, 0.25)$,
\[
  \fprate_{\wedge,4} = 0.20 \cdot 0.15 \cdot 0.10 \cdot 0.25
  = 7.5 \times 10^{-4},
\]
far below any single stage: every stage must independently false-positive for
the conjunction to. With all four at $\fprate = 1/4$ the product is $(1/4)^4
\approx 3.9 \times 10^{-3}$, the Miller-Rabin bound at $k = 4$. The conjunction
of one-sided tests drives the false-positive rate down geometrically, which is
exactly the mechanism Miller-Rabin exploits.

\begin{remark}[Independence is required]
\label{rem:composition-independence}
The product factorizations rest on independence across stages. If the stages
are perfectly correlated, so that all err together or none do, the end-to-end
error rate is the single-stage rate $\eta$ regardless of $k$, not $1 -
(1 - \eta)^k$ or $\eta^k$. Repeated queries of the same deterministic predicate
on the same element are the extreme case: they are perfectly correlated, not
independent, and neither the theorem nor its dual applies. Chain-stage
independence is a structural property of the pipeline, supplied in the
Miller-Rabin analysis by drawing distinct witnesses (\Cref{sec:miller-rabin});
chapter~16 takes correlated stages as an explicit subject. It is distinct from
Axiom~1 (\Cref{ax:element-indep}), which governs independence across input
elements rather than across pipeline stages, though in well-formed pipelines
where each stage queries a distinct element the former follows from the latter.
\end{remark}

The parallel counterpart to this serial result is the Kronecker factorization
(\Cref{thm:kronecker}): correctness multiplies along a chain, while the joint
confusion matrix of $m$ parallel queries factors as $Q_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes
Q_m$. The serial product needs only chain-stage independence; the Kronecker
product needs Axiom~1.
