\section{Why Axioms?}
\label{sec:why-axioms}

Chapters 1 through 3 derived the gate rates, the per-latent-assignment
propagation of \Cref{prop:latent-propagation}, the composition theorem
(\Cref{sec:composition-theorem}), and the Miller-Rabin bound, and at two points
they used independence without naming it. This chapter names the two
assumptions, states them precisely, and proves what their conjunction buys: the
Kronecker factorization that collapses the parameter count of an $m$-query
analysis from exponential to linear, and the formal definition of the type the
first three chapters circled.

The first assumption is element-wise independence\index{independence!across elements}: error events at distinct
inputs are independent. Every product of probabilities in chapters 1 through 3,
the composition theorem's chain, Miller-Rabin's $k$ independent witnesses
(\Cref{sec:miller-rabin}), the AND and OR truth tables of
\Cref{sec:and-under-noise}, rested on it. When it fails, as for two classifiers
trained on shared data whose errors correlate, the product formulas become
bounds at best. We call it Axiom~1 (\Cref{sec:axiom-1}).

The second is rate stability: each block's error rate is a fixed property, the
same on the hundredth query as the first. The AND derivation treated $\fprate$
and $\fnrate$ as constants, assuming the channel does not drift, warm up, or
adapt. We call it Axiom~2 (\Cref{sec:axiom-2}). It rules out fatigue,
history-dependence, and adversarial adaptation, and it is a real restriction,
not a triviality. Together the two axioms force the joint confusion matrix of
$m$ queries to factor as a Kronecker product of $m$ small per-query matrices
(\Cref{sec:kronecker}), after which the formal definition of
\Cref{sec:bernoulli-bool-formal} writes itself: a triple $(X, Y, Q)$ satisfying
the two axioms, of which every example in chapters 1 through 3 is an instance.
