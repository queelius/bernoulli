\section{What Comes Next}
\label{sec:what-comes-next}

We have been building toward a single formal object---$\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$
as defined in \Cref{sec:bernoulli-bool-formal}---and we have arrived. This is
a good moment to take stock of where we stand and where Part~II will take us.

\subsection*{What Part I delivered}

Chapter 4 has completed Part~I's formal work. The two axioms are now stated
with precision (\Cref{sec:axiom-1,sec:axiom-2}), the Kronecker factorization
theorem shows what those axioms license (\Cref{thm:kronecker}), and
Definition~\ref{def:bernoulli-bool} gives $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ a rigorous
identity as the triple $(X, Y, Q)$ under those axioms. Every example in
chapters 1 through 3---the biased coin, the binary symmetric channel, the
classifier verdict, AND and OR and NOT under noise, the $k$-round Miller-Rabin
bound---is now recognizable as an instance or an operation on instances of
this type. The definition was not asserted; it was earned.

\subsection*{Five Core Principles: status after Part I}

The Bernoulli framework rests on five organizing principles. At the close of
Part~I, three are fully in view and two remain ahead.

\begin{description}
  \item[\textbf{Two Axioms} \quad [\textsc{Part I, complete}]]
    Axiom~1 (element-wise independence: \Cref{sec:axiom-1}) and Axiom~2
    (conditional independence of block error rates: \Cref{sec:axiom-2}) are
    both stated formally and proved to jointly imply the Kronecker
    factorization. They are the load-bearing assumptions throughout the rest
    of the book.

  \item[\textbf{Composition Closure} \quad [\textsc{Part I, complete}]]
    The composition theorem of \Cref{sec:composition-theorem} showed that
    chaining $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instances produces a new
    $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance. AND, OR, and NOT are all
    closure-preserving operations. The Kronecker theorem of \Cref{thm:kronecker}
    is the tensor-product face of the same closure property: whether we
    compose instances serially (matrix product) or view them in parallel
    (tensor product), the type is closed. The Boolean universe is self-contained
    under the operations we care about.

  \item[\textbf{Boolean Thread} \quad [\textsc{Part I, complete}]]
    Part~I chose $\Bool = \{\top, \bot\}$ as the input and output domain and
    stayed there. Two elements, two partition blocks, two error rates, a
    $2 \times 2$ channel matrix, two free parameters after
    \Cref{cor:parameter-reduction} applies---the whole Part~I story has Boolean
    structure at every level. This is not a limitation; it is the atom's
    defining feature. The Boolean thread runs from the biased coin in chapter~1
    to the formal definition in chapter~4 without deviation. Part~II lifts it
    to sets; Part~III lifts it to maps.

  \item[\textbf{Parametric Parsimony} \quad [\textsc{visible in Part I, completed in Part II}]]
    The corollary to the Kronecker theorem (\Cref{cor:parameter-reduction})
    showed the parameter count dropping from $n(2^m - 1)$ to $2m$ under
    the two axioms. For the Boolean case, that reduction is already dramatic.
    The full principle---why $2m$ parameters are not merely convenient but
    are, in an information-theoretic sense, the right number---requires
    the entropy-based lower bounds developed in Part~II, chapter~8. The seed
    has been planted; the fruit arrives later.

  \item[\textbf{Space-Accuracy Duality} \quad [\textsc{Part II, chapter 8}]]
    This principle---that there is a fundamental trade-off between the
    space a Bernoulli structure consumes and the error rates it can
    achieve---has not yet appeared. Part~I did not need it because we
    were analyzing a fixed $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance, not asking
    how small we can make one while meeting an accuracy target.
    Space-Accuracy Duality is the central organizing principle of Part~II.
    It arrives in chapter~8.
\end{description}

\subsection*{From bits to sets}

Part~I gave us the atom. Part~II asks: what happens when you build a set
membership predicate from atoms?

The question is less abstract than it sounds. A set $S$ over a universe
$U$ induces, for each element $u \in U$, a Boolean query: is $u \in S$?
An \emph{approximate} set replaces exact membership with a noisy channel:
the predicate may report $u \in S$ when it should report $u \notin S$
(a false positive) or the reverse (a false negative). The per-element
error events are---under Axiom~1---mutually independent. Each element's
query is a $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance. The approximate set as a
whole is a structured collection of those instances, and its behavior is
determined by how those instances are built and indexed.

The canonical first construction is the Bloom filter. A Bloom filter
maintains a bit array of $m$ bits and a family of $k$ hash functions;
it answers set-membership queries with zero false negatives and a
tunable false-positive rate. Understanding the Bloom filter from the
Bernoulli perspective---as a mechanism for manufacturing
$\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instances with $\fnrate = 0$ and a
controlled $\fprate$---is the project of \Cref{ch:approximate-sets}.
The Bloom filter is not a special case we study because it is famous; it
is the cleanest first construction because it makes the $\fnrate = 0$
boundary and the Space-Accuracy Duality both visible in one concrete object.

By the end of \Cref{ch:approximate-sets}, the reader will see not just
how the Bloom filter works but why its analysis is a consequence of the
formal machinery we developed in Part~I.

Part~II then extends: chapter~6 studies how set operations (union,
intersection, complement) propagate error rates; chapter~7 develops the
classification measures that quantify approximate-set quality; and
chapter~8 derives the information-theoretic lower bounds that make
Space-Accuracy Duality precise. The Boolean thread does not disappear in
Part~II---it becomes the foundation on which more complex structures are
built, one $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance at a time.
