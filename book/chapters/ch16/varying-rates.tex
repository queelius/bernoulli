\section{Varying Rates Over Time}
\label{sec:varying-rates}

The first failure mode is the simplest to describe and the one
most commonly encountered in practice. The error rate is not a
fixed property of the channel; it changes with the query index.
Where chapters 1 through 15 wrote $\fprate$ as a scalar number,
the present section writes $\fprate(t)$, a function of time, and
asks what happens to the iid analysis when the scalar reading is
no longer available.

\subsection*{The phenomenon}

Concrete examples set the scene. Each will recur in the chapter as
a diagnostic case.

\textbf{Classifier drift.} A machine-learning classifier was trained
on data drawn from one distribution and is deployed on data drawn
from another. As the deployment distribution drifts further from
the training distribution, the classifier's per-query false-positive
rate climbs. Early queries see $\fprate(t) \approx 0.02$; queries
six months later see $\fprate(t) \approx 0.15$. The change is
monotone in expectation but the individual per-query rates are
themselves random, conditional on whatever drift trajectory the
data actually follows.

\textbf{Bloom filter past capacity.} A Bloom filter was sized for
$n = 1000$ items with $m = 9585$ bits and $k = 7$ hash functions to
achieve $\fprate \approx 0.01$. The application then inserts $5000$
items into it. The fill ratio rises from $30\%$ to nearly $100\%$;
the false-positive rate climbs from $0.01$ early to about $0.5$
late. The formula from \Cref{sec:bloom-from-scratch} predicts
$\fprate(t) \approx (1 - e^{-k t / m})^k$, where $t$ is the number
of inserted items at the moment of the query; this is precisely a
time-varying rate that depends on insertion history.

\textbf{Hardware noise drift.} A sensor's error rate depends on its
operating temperature. Over the course of a day, the temperature
varies, and so does the error rate. The variation may be
deterministic if the temperature is logged, or stochastic if the
temperature is itself uncertain.

What unites the three examples is structural: the rate associated
with the channel is itself a non-constant function of the query
index. Treating it as a constant produces analyses whose error
bounds underestimate the actual error frequency, sometimes by
large factors.

\subsection*{Which axiom is violated}

\Cref{sec:axiom-2} stated Axiom 2 in two equivalent forms: a
conditional-independence claim on the per-block rates, and a
stability claim on the channel matrix $Q$. The stability form is
the one that breaks here. \Cref{ax:block-rates-indep} together
with \Cref{ax:element-indep} implied (via the proposition on stable
channel matrices) that the channel matrix row for any block $B_i$
is the same for every query involving an element of $B_i$,
regardless of query index. Translated to the Boolean-channel case
that runs throughout the book: a single $\fprate$ and a single
$\fnrate$ govern every query.

Time-varying rates contradict this directly. The per-query rate at
step $t$ is $\fprate(t)$, not $\fprate$, and the joint distribution
over a sequence of queries no longer factors through a single $Q$.
\Cref{ax:block-rates-indep} fails not because two different blocks
are correlated (the conditional-independence form) but because the
``block rates are stable across queries'' consequence fails. The
practical signature is that the same query, repeated at different
times, gets different rate parameters.

A clarification matters here. Time-varying rates are compatible
with \Cref{ax:element-indep} (Axiom 1) in many cases. If the
classifier's drift is governed by an exogenous time variable and
errors at distinct queries are still conditionally independent
given the drift state, Axiom 1 still holds; only Axiom 2 fails.
The two axioms are logically independent, and one can break
without the other.

\subsection*{Consequences for the iid tools}

Three workhorses from earlier chapters break when Axiom 2 fails.

\emph{The composition theorem of chapter 3 \S3.3.} The chained
error rate $\eta_{\text{total}} = 1 - \prod_{i = 1}^k (1 - \eta_i)$
assumed that each $\eta_i$ was a fixed per-stage rate. When the
$\eta_i$ are themselves time-indexed and possibly random, the
product expression is no longer the chained error rate; it is an
average over a particular trajectory, and the actual error rate
along the worst trajectory may exceed the product bound
substantially.

\emph{The PPV/NPV machinery of chapter 7 \S7.4.} The PPV formula
$\PPV = \pi \tprate / (\pi \tprate + (1 - \pi) \fprate)$ assumed
that $\fprate$, $\tprate$, and $\pi$ were all fixed. When any of
these is time-varying, the formula still holds at any single query
(this is the \Cref{sec:where-framework-helps} salvage), but the
time-average PPV need not equal the PPV evaluated at the
time-average rates. The averaging order matters.

\emph{The Chernoff bound of chapter 11 \S11.4 (\Cref{thm:chernoff})}
applied to iid Bernoulli sums. The hypothesis of iid is exactly what
fails for $X_t \sim \mathrm{Bern}(\fprate(t))$ with varying
$\fprate(t)$; the $X_t$ are independent but not identically
distributed, and the multiplicative form of the Chernoff bound,
which relied on a single $p$ value, no longer applies directly.
What does still apply is the martingale generalization.

\subsection*{The right tool: martingale concentration}

The Azuma-Hoeffding inequality replaces the Chernoff bound in the
non-iid setting. Where Chernoff bounded sums of iid Bernoulli
random variables, Azuma-Hoeffding bounds martingale differences
that are themselves bounded in size but otherwise unrestricted in
distribution.

\begin{proposition}[Azuma-Hoeffding]
\label{prop:azuma-hoeffding}
Let $\{Y_t\}_{t = 0, 1, \ldots, n}$ be a martingale on a probability
space, with $Y_0 = \mathbb{E}[Y_n]$ and bounded differences
$|Y_t - Y_{t - 1}| \le c_t$ for each $t \ge 1$. Then for any $a > 0$,
\[
  \Pr[|Y_n - Y_0| \ge a]
  \;\le\;
  2 \exp\!\left( -\frac{a^2}{2 \sum_{t = 1}^n c_t^2} \right).
\]
\end{proposition}

The inequality is due to Hoeffding (1963), with the martingale
form developed by Azuma (1967). No proof is given here; the
standard MGF-plus-Markov argument from chapter 11 \S11.4 carries
over to martingale differences once the per-step MGFs are bounded
by the maximum of the per-step difference bounds. The key
observation is that the proof does not need identical distributions;
only the per-step ``how much can $Y_t$ jump?'' bound matters.

Applied to time-varying false-positive rates, the recipe is direct.
Let $X_t \in \{0, 1\}$ indicate a false positive at query $t$, with
$\Pr[X_t = 1 \,|\, \mathcal{F}_{t - 1}] = \fprate(t)$ for some
filtration $\mathcal{F}_{t - 1}$ representing the query history.
Define $Y_t = \sum_{s = 1}^t (X_s - \fprate(s))$; this is a
martingale with bounded differences $|Y_t - Y_{t - 1}| =
|X_t - \fprate(t)| \le 1$. Azuma-Hoeffding with $c_t = 1$ gives
\[
  \Pr\!\left[\,\left|\,\sum_{t = 1}^n X_t - \sum_{t = 1}^n \fprate(t)\,\right| \ge a\,\right]
  \;\le\;
  2 \exp\!\left( -\frac{a^2}{2 n} \right).
\]
This is the non-iid generalization of the Chernoff bound: the total
false-positive count concentrates around its expectation
$\sum_t \fprate(t)$ with Gaussian-shaped tails of width $O(\sqrt{n})$,
even when the per-step rate $\fprate(t)$ is not constant.

\subsection*{Worked example: Bloom filter past capacity}

Take the Bloom filter from the second example above: sized for
$n = 1000$ items, used to hold $n = 5000$ items, with the
per-query false-positive rate growing from about $0.01$ early to
$0.5$ late as items are inserted. Suppose we query the filter
$n = 1000$ times after the over-filling, with each query drawn
from the non-member universe.

Treating the rate as a fixed $\bar{\fprate} \approx 0.25$ (the
midpoint of the trajectory) and applying the iid Chernoff bound to
the expected total false-positive count $250$ would give a
deviation bound of the form
$\Pr[X \ge 1.1 \cdot 250] \le \exp(-250 \cdot 0.01 / 3)$, that is,
$\Pr[X \ge 275] \le e^{-0.83} \approx 0.44$. The bound is uselessly
loose because the constant-rate assumption is wrong.

The Azuma-Hoeffding bound, in contrast, does not assume a constant
rate; it bounds the deviation of $\sum_t X_t$ from
$\sum_t \fprate(t)$, where the second sum is computed honestly
along the trajectory. For our Bloom filter with $n = 1000$ post-fill
queries and $c_t = 1$, the deviation bound is
$\Pr[|\sum X_t - \sum \fprate(t)| \ge a] \le 2 \exp(-a^2 / 2000)$.
Setting $a = 100$ gives a bound of $2 e^{-5} \approx 0.013$: the
total false-positive count lies within $\pm 100$ of the trajectory
expectation $\sum_t \fprate(t)$ with probability at least $0.987$.
The trajectory expectation, computed from the Bloom formula at each
insertion count, is around $\sum_t \fprate(t) \approx 230$.

The contrast is that the iid analysis with a misspecified mean gave
a vacuous bound, while the martingale analysis with the correct
non-stationary trajectory gives a tight bound. The first analysis
fails not because the bound is weak but because the modeling
assumption (constant rate) does not match the actual channel
behavior. The second analysis succeeds by acknowledging the
non-stationarity and using the right tool.

\subsection*{Pedagogical diagnostic}

How does a practitioner know whether to use the iid Chernoff bound
or the martingale Azuma-Hoeffding bound? The diagnostic is direct:
sort the data by time and ask whether the empirical false-positive
rate $\hat{\fprate}$ is stable across time-ordered partitions.

Concretely: partition the query log into early, middle, and late
buckets. Compute $\hat{\fprate}$ on each bucket. If the three
estimates lie within their sampling intervals of each other, the
iid assumption is consistent with the data, and the Chernoff bound
applies. If the estimates differ by more than their sampling
intervals, time-stationarity is broken; drop the iid assumption
and use Azuma-Hoeffding along the realized trajectory.

The diagnostic is a routine check, not a deep statistical test. It
catches the failure mode that this section is about (rate drift in
time) but says nothing about the correlated-noise failure mode of
\Cref{sec:correlated-noise} or the adversarial-input failure mode of
\Cref{sec:adversarial-inputs}. A full audit requires checking each
axiom separately; the time-sorted partition test is the cheapest
check for the Axiom 2 failure specifically.
