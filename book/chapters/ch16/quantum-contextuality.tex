\section[No Latent Value: Quantum Contextuality]{When No Latent Value Exists: Quantum Contextuality}
\label{sec:quantum-contextuality}

The failure modes catalogued so far share a feature that is easy to
overlook: in every one, a definite latent value still exists. A drifting
rate (\Cref{sec:varying-rates}) is the rate of a bit that is genuinely
$0$ or $1$; correlated noise (\Cref{sec:correlated-noise}) garbles
genuine answers with a dependence the factorization missed; an
adversarial input (\Cref{sec:adversarial-inputs}) has a true membership
status, chosen to sit at the tail. In each case the framework
mis-estimates a parameter of a process whose underlying value is never in
question. There is one failure of a different kind, where the assumption
beneath both axioms fails: there is no consistent latent value to
estimate at all. Quantum mechanics is its rigorous physical instance, and
it gives this chapter its sharpest example.\index{contextuality}

\subsection*{The latent value is a hidden variable}

The framework's atom posits a definite truth $t$, garbled by a channel
$Q$ and recovered by Bayes. Read literally, $t$ is a \emph{non-contextual
local hidden variable}: the answer to ``is $x$ a member?'' is a
pre-existing fact, and $Q$ only obscures it. This commitment is stronger
than either axiom and prior to both. It asserts that a single joint
distribution over the answers to all queries exists, of which each
observed rate is a marginal. \Cref{ax:element-indep} (Axiom 1) then adds
that this joint factorizes across instances; \Cref{ax:block-rates-indep}
(Axiom 2) adds that the per-block rates are stable across queries. The
existence of the joint distribution is assumed before either axiom is
written, and it is the assumption this section examines.

\subsection*{Fine's theorem: the latent value is a falsifiable claim}

That the latent value\index{latent assignment} exists looks like a harmless modeling convenience.
It is not. \textcite{fine-1982} proved that, for a correlation
experiment of the Bell-CHSH type (two settings per side, two outcomes
each), the following are equivalent: a deterministic hidden-variable
model exists; a factorizable stochastic model exists; a single joint
distribution over all of the experiment's observables exists and returns
the measured probabilities; and the Bell inequalities hold. Translated
into the framework's vocabulary, the chain reads
\[
\begin{aligned}
  &\text{a latent value exists}\\
  \Longleftrightarrow\;\; &\text{a joint distribution over all answers exists}\\
  \Longleftrightarrow\;\; &\text{the Bell inequalities hold.}
\end{aligned}
\]
The latent-value assumption is therefore a falsifiable physical claim,
not a definitional choice, and it can be tested in the laboratory.

\subsection*{The physical archetype}

Quantum mechanics falsifies it. Two entangled qubits, measured in
suitably chosen bases, produce correlations that violate the Bell
inequalities \parencite{bell-1964}. By Fine's equivalence no joint
distribution over the measurement outcomes exists, so the pair cannot be
written as two $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ values, not with any rates and not
with any correlation correction. This is the precise difference from
\Cref{sec:correlated-noise}: correlated noise widened the composition
theorem into an inequality with correction terms
(\Cref{rem:composition-correlation-bracket}) because a joint distribution
still existed and merely failed to factorize; entanglement removes the
joint distribution itself, so there is nothing left for a correction term
to correct.

A second, independent obstruction appears once measurements can be
incompatible and the state space has dimension at least three. The Kochen-Specker theorem\index{Kochen--Specker theorem} \parencite{kochen-specker-1967} shows that no
assignment of definite values to all observables can be made consistent
across the measurement contexts in which those observables appear. The
honest scope matters here: a single two-outcome measurement in a fixed
basis does admit a hidden-variable model, so the atom in isolation is
safe; the obstructions are entanglement (Bell) and dimension at least
three (Kochen-Specker).

\subsection*{Reading the axioms as physics}

Seen through the framework, \Cref{ax:element-indep} is a locality
assumption, and the Bell inequalities are the constraints every
factorizable latent-value model satisfies; their violation measures how
far an entangled pair sits outside all such models.\index{Bell inequality} The pre-axiom existence of a joint distribution is
non-contextuality. The negative space of this chapter has, in quantum
contextuality, its most rigorous possible example. The earlier failures
are cases where the framework estimates a real parameter badly; this is a
setting where the framework's ontology is provably wrong, with a theorem
certifying the impossibility rather than an experiment merely exhibiting
a hard case.

\subsection*{Where the boundary sits}

For the earlier failures the chapter could name a replacement: a
martingale bound, a correction term, a secret hash family. Here there is
none within the classical framework. The correct object is a probability
amplitude, not a probability, and amplitudes can cancel where
probabilities can only accumulate; the interference this permits has no
representation in a latent-value-plus-noise model.

The boundary is sharp and locatable, and it is a property of the
\emph{measurement}, not of the state. Let a detector have POVM elements
$\{M_b\}$ that are diagonal in the computational basis, $M_b = \sum_t
A(b \mid t)\, \lvert t \rangle\langle t \rvert$, where $A(b \mid t)$ is the probability of
reporting $b$ when the basis state is $t$. Then for \emph{every} density
matrix $\rho$\index{density matrix}\index{detector POVM}
\parencite{nielsen-chuang-2010},
\[
  \Pr[\text{report } b]
  \;=\; \operatorname{Tr}(M_b \rho)
  \;=\; \sum_t A(b \mid t)\, \rho_{tt},
\]
so the outcome statistics depend on $\rho$ only through its diagonal, and
the coherences $\rho_{ts}$, $t \ne s$, never enter. The classical
assignment model, a latent basis value $t$ drawn from the diagonal of
$\rho$ and reported through the channel $A$, is therefore \emph{exact} for
the outcome statistics of such a measurement, whatever the state:
an eigenstate, a mixture, or a coherent superposition. No decoherence
is needed for this; it is a fact about diagonal measurements. The model
stops being sufficient only when the question leaves that setting: when
the measurement is not diagonal in the basis that defines the latent
value (a rotated or entangling measurement, or a coherent operation
before readout), where coherences change the statistics, or when one asks
for joint statistics of incompatible measurements, the contextual case of
this section. Readout error of computational-basis measurements, in which
measured qubits are reported through asymmetric $(\fprate, \fnrate)$
channels\index{quantum readout}, is thus squarely inside the framework;
the experimental literature models it with exactly such assignment
matrices \parencite{bravyi-et-al-2021,nation-et-al-2021,smith-et-al-2021},
and the companion manuscript \parencite{towell-bernoulli-quantum-readout}
applies the error calculus there. Boolean post-processing of several
readouts composes exactly per latent assignment
(\Cref{prop:latent-propagation}), not through a single carried rate pair
(\Cref{ex:two-rate-counterexample}). In this precise sense the Bernoulli
model is the classical description of what diagonal measurements see:
faithful for them, and silent about questions that require amplitudes.
\Cref{sec:where-framework-helps} takes up what survives at this boundary
and elsewhere.
