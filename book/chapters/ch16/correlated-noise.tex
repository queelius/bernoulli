\section{Correlated Noise Across Queries}
\label{sec:correlated-noise}

The second failure mode trades a different axiom. Where
\Cref{sec:varying-rates} watched the per-query rate drift, this section
holds the rate fixed and watches the independence between error events
collapse. The channel matrix is stable in the sense of
\Cref{sec:axiom-1}; what fails is the assumption that the error events
are independent, and the joint distribution of outcomes no longer factors
as the product of marginals.

\subsection*{The phenomenon}

Three examples make the pattern concrete. The first is degenerate but
instructive: one Bloom filter and one query, repeated. The response to
$x \notin A$ is fixed by the bit pattern at $k$ positions; once that
pattern is set, the answer is deterministic, and the second query for the
same $x$ returns the same answer with probability one. The conditional
probability of a second $\top$ given a first is $1$, not the marginal
$\fprate$, and the gap is the entire failure.

The second is the genuine Bloom correlation, internal to a single query.
A query for $x$ reads $k$ bits $h_1(x), \ldots, h_k(x)$ and returns $\top$
only if all are set. These $k$ bit-check events are not independent: a
query's hashes can collide, and a queried bit being set is evidence that
the array is fuller, which raises the chance the others are set too. The
conjunction is correlated within the query.

The third introduces time. An online classifier updates its model after
each response, so the classification at query $t$ depends on the
responses to queries $1, \ldots, t-1$ through the updated parameters.
Sequential queries carry a Markov dependence on the model state; the
per-query rate may be stable on average while the conditional
distribution of the $t$-th error given the history is nontrivial.

\subsection*{Which axiom is violated}

\Cref{sec:axiom-1} stated Axiom 1 as the mutual conditional independence\index{independence!across elements}
of error events at distinct inputs given the true classifications: for
inputs $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ with true labels $y_i$ and observed labels
$\hat y_i$,
\[
  \Pr[\hat{y}_1 = a_1, \ldots, \hat{y}_n = a_n \mid y_1, \ldots, y_n]
  \;=\;
  \prod_{i = 1}^n \Pr[\hat{y}_i = a_i \mid y_i].
\]
This factorization underlies every multi-query bound in the book; when it
fails the joint becomes an inequality, in either direction depending on
the sign of the correlation. The three examples fail it in different
ways: the repeated query fails completely (the conditional is degenerate,
$0$ or $1$); the within-query bit-checks fail positively (one bit set
raises the posterior that the others are); the online learner fails
through a temporal Markov dependence. \Cref{ax:element-indep} can fail
while \Cref{ax:block-rates-indep} (Axiom 2's stability) holds; the two
modes are independent.

\subsection*{Consequences for the iid tools}

The composition theorem\index{composition theorem} of \Cref{sec:composition-theorem}
($\eta_{\text{total}} = 1 - \prod_i (1 - \eta_i)$) used Axiom 1 the moment
it wrote the product: the product of marginal no-error probabilities
equals the joint no-error probability only under independence. Under
positive correlation the joint no-error probability is larger (the system
does better than the product predicts), under negative correlation
smaller, so the formula becomes an approximation whose error direction
tracks the correlation sign.

The Bloom derivation of \Cref{sec:bloom-from-scratch} contains exactly
this approximation. The standard $\fprate \approx (1 - e^{-kn/m})^k$
formula (\Cref{eq:bloom-fpr}) treats the $k$ bit-check events of one
query as independent, writing ``all $k$ set'' as a product of per-bit
probabilities. \Cref{rem:bloom-independence} of chapter 5 recorded that
the factorization is an approximation: the $k$ events are positively
dependent, since a queried bit being set is evidence the array is fuller,
so the conjunction is \emph{larger} than the product and the standard
formula \emph{under}-estimates the false-positive rate. This is a known
correction\index{Bloom filter!false-positive rate}: \textcite{bose-et-al-2008} proved that Bloom's formula is a
strict lower bound on the true rate for $k \ge 2$, and
\textcite{christensen-roginsky-jimeno-2010} gave an exact expression by
conditioning on the number of set bits. The gap vanishes
as $m \to \infty$ with $n/m$ fixed but is detectable at small $m$: the
worked $m = 4$ witness of \Cref{rem:bloom-independence} gives an exact
$13/64 \approx 0.203$ against the formula's $0.155$. This is the Axiom 1
failure scoped within a single query; because the combination is a
conjunction, the positive sign makes the product a \emph{lower} bound,
the opposite direction from the $1 - \prod_i (1 - \eta_i)$ serial form
above, where positive correlation makes the product an upper bound on the
joint error. The sign of the correlation and the form of the combination
together fix the direction.

The PPV machinery of chapter 7 is a per-query quantity and is unaffected
at a single query; the issue arises only when combining PPV estimates
across queries, where the variance of the empirical PPV depends on the
joint distribution of the contingency counts and no longer satisfies the
iid sampling that \Cref{prop:ppv-variance} assumed.

\subsection*{The right tools}

Three replacements are standard. \emph{Conditional bounds via the law of
total probability}: when the dependence has known structure, condition on
the relevant variable and bound each conditional separately. For a query
whose $k$ bit-checks are coupled through the array occupancy, conditioning
on the number of set bits makes the checks exchangeable and the
conjunction computable, which is exactly the route of the exact analysis
cited above.\index{correlated noise} \emph{McDiarmid's inequality} for
bounded-difference functions:

\begin{proposition}[McDiarmid's inequality]
\label{prop:mcdiarmid}
Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be independent random variables and $f$ a
real-valued function satisfying the bounded-differences property: for each
$i$ there is a constant $c_i$ with
\[
  \sup_{x_1, \ldots, x_n, x_i'}
  |f(x_1, \ldots, x_i, \ldots, x_n) - f(x_1, \ldots, x_i', \ldots, x_n)|
  \;\le\; c_i.
\]
Then for any $a > 0$,
\[
  \Pr[|f(X_1, \ldots, X_n) - \mathbb{E}[f]| \ge a]
  \;\le\;
  2 \exp\!\left( -\frac{2 a^2}{\sum_{i = 1}^n c_i^2} \right).
\]
\end{proposition}

The inequality is McDiarmid's (1989). Here $f$ might be the empirical
false-positive count of a fixed filter under a fixed query stream, a
function of the hash-family choices; the function value moves by a bounded
amount when one hash is resampled even though the bits a single hash sets
are not independent, so the bounded-differences form gives a usable bound
where the strict iid Chernoff bound does not apply. \emph{Negative
association as a salvage}: when the error indicators are known to be
negatively associated, the Chernoff-Hoeffding tail bounds for their sum
remain valid, since such indicators concentrate at least as well as
independent ones. Sampling without replacement against sampling with
replacement is the classic instance; identifying negative association lets
one keep the iid tail analysis as conservative. (This salvage concerns
tails of counts; for the probability that \emph{at least one} error
occurs, negative association works against the designer, as the remark
below records.)

\subsection*{Worked example: when shared hashes do and do not correlate}

Take two Bloom filters $B_A$ and $B_B$ built with a shared hash family
$h_1, \ldots, h_k$, $k = 3$, $m = 100$ bits each, holding $n_A = n_B = 20$
items. The per-filter rate from \Cref{eq:bloom-fpr} is
\[
  \fprate \;=\; (1 - e^{-k n / m})^k \;=\; (1 - e^{-0.6})^3 \;\approx\; 0.092,
\]
so the iid prediction for ``both say $\top$'' on a query $x \notin A \cup
B$ is $\fprate^2 \approx 0.0085$. A natural worry is that the shared hash
family correlates the two answers. It does not, when $A$ and $B$ are
independent. For a fixed query $x$ both filters read the same positions
$h_1(x), h_2(x), h_3(x)$, but $B_A$'s bits at those positions depend only
on $A$ and $B_B$'s only on $B$; with $A$ and $B$ independent the two bit
patterns are independent, so the answers are independent and the joint is
the product exactly. Computing position by position confirms it: a given
position is set in $B_A$ with probability $p_A \approx 0.45$ and
independently in $B_B$ with $p_B \approx 0.45$, set in both with
$p_A p_B \approx 0.20$, and all three set in both with $(p_A p_B)^3
\approx 0.0085$, the same $\fprate^2$. Sharing the hash family fixes
\emph{which} positions are read, not \emph{whether} the two independent
arrays have them set.

Correlation appears when the sets overlap. If $A$ and $B$ share elements,
those elements set the same bits in both filters, coupling the answers;
in the extreme $A = B$ the two filters are identical and the joint
probability of two $\top$ responses is the marginal $\fprate$, not
$\fprate^2$, the iid prediction undershooting by a factor $1/\fprate
\approx 11$. The lesson sharpens the section: shared randomness is
necessary but not sufficient for correlated error. The error events must
\emph{jointly} depend on it, as the $k$ bit-checks within one query do
(through the shared array occupancy) and as two filters on overlapping
sets do (through the shared elements), but as two filters on independent
sets do not.

\begin{remark}[What marginals alone determine]
\label{rem:composition-correlation-bracket}
A designer who knows only the marginal stage rates $\eta_1, \ldots,
\eta_k$ knows only that the probability of at least one error lies in
the Fr\'echet (Boole) interval
\[
  \max_i \eta_i \;\le\; \Pr[\text{some stage errs}]
  \;\le\; \min\Bigl(1, \textstyle\sum_i \eta_i\Bigr),
\]
both ends attainable, and the independent value $1 - \prod_i (1 -
\eta_i)$ lies inside it. The correlation sign says on which side of the
independent value the truth falls. Under \emph{positive} association of
the stage errors the no-error events are positively associated, so
$\Pr[\text{no stage errs}] \ge \prod_i (1 - \eta_i)$ and the serial
formula is an \emph{upper} bound on the combined error; under
\emph{negative} association it is a \emph{lower} bound. (For two stages the
condition is simply $\Pr[\text{both err}] \gtrless \eta_1 \eta_2$; for
more stages pairwise conditions do not suffice and association in the
Harris or negative-association sense is the standard hypothesis.) The
within-query Bloom conjunction is the dual combination: there the error is
the event that \emph{all} $k$ checks fire, positive association makes that
event \emph{more} likely than the product, and the product formula is a
lower bound on the false-positive rate. The direction is fixed by the sign
of the dependence together with the form of the combination, and without
the sign only the Fr\'echet interval is guaranteed.
\end{remark}

The diagnostic for correlated noise is to ask whether the error events
share a source they \emph{jointly} depend on: the same array occupancy,
the same elements, the same random seed consumed at query time. If they
do, the iid analysis is an approximation whose direction and size need
explicit calculation; \Cref{sec:where-framework-helps} returns to which
parts of the framework survive the correlation.
