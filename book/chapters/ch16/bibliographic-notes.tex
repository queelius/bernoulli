\section*{Bibliographic Notes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Bibliographic Notes}
\label{sec:bib-notes-ch16}

\paragraph{Martingale concentration.}
The Azuma-Hoeffding inequality of \Cref{prop:azuma-hoeffding} has
two parent papers. \textcite{azuma-1967}, ``Weighted Sums of
Certain Dependent Random Variables,'' established the
bounded-difference martingale bound in its modern form;
\textcite{hoeffding-1963}, ``Probability Inequalities for Sums of
Bounded Random Variables,'' gave the independent-case predecessor
that the martingale version generalizes. The joint name reflects
the shared exponential-moment generating-function proof technique
and the historical fact that the modern statement combines the
two contributions into a single inequality. The extension of the
bounded-difference idea to non-martingale functions of dependent
random variables is due to \textcite{mcdiarmid-1989}, ``On the
Method of Bounded Differences,'' which is the right reference for
\Cref{prop:mcdiarmid}. McDiarmid's survey also gives the clearest
expository treatment of the technique, including the diameter-
of-influence-set bound the chapter's prose alludes to.

\paragraph{Non-iid concentration in learning theory.}
The natural textbook reference for non-iid concentration as used
in statistical learning theory is \textcite{devroye-lugosi-2001},
\emph{Combinatorial Methods in Density Estimation}, which
catalogues the martingale, bounded-difference, and chaining
techniques the learning-theory literature has applied to empirical
processes. The book is written for an audience comfortable with
measure theory and empirical-process theory; the worked examples
(kernel density estimators, histogram classifiers, nearest-
neighbor rules) provide templates the practitioner can transcribe
into the Bernoulli setting with minor modifications.

\paragraph{Adversarial machine learning.}
The adversarial-inputs catalogue of \Cref{sec:adversarial-inputs}
draws on the adversarial-ML literature that has grown around
neural-network classifiers since the mid-2010s. The two entry-
point papers are \textcite{goodfellow-2015}, ``Explaining and
Harnessing Adversarial Examples,'' which gave the fast-gradient-
sign method and named the phenomenon of small input perturbations
that flip a classifier's verdict, and
\textcite{carlini-wagner-2017}, ``Towards Evaluating the
Robustness of Neural Networks,'' which introduced the
optimization-based attack methodology that became the standard
benchmark for defenses. Between them the two papers set the
modern adversarial-ML agenda: the former diagnosed the problem,
the latter raised the bar by showing that ad-hoc defenses fail
under a stronger attacker. The Bernoulli framework inherits the
same exposure: an attacker with knowledge of the hash family can
find a colliding input in expected $O(1 / \fprate)$ queries, and
the only honest defenses are cryptographic hash families or
oblivious primitives.

\paragraph{Oblivious computing as the bridge to chapter 17.}
The cryptographic primitives the chapter has pointed to as
replacements for iid analysis in the adversarial regime are
catalogued in the oblivious-computing and secure-computation
literatures, the foundational treatment of which is
\textcite{goldreich-1996}, ``Foundations of Cryptography:
Fragments of a Book.'' The 1996 fragments draft (and the 2001
and 2004 published volumes that grew from it) is the entry point
for secure-multiparty computation, oblivious RAM, and the
private-information-retrieval primitives chapter~17 will unpack
into the cipher-map framework. Readers who want to anticipate
the structure of chapters~17 and~18 should treat Goldreich's
foundations as the prerequisite reading; the Bernoulli-framework
specialization the book develops sits on top of the cryptographic
infrastructure those volumes formalize.
