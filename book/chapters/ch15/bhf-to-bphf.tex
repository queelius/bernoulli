\section{From BHF to BPHF}
\label{sec:bhf-to-bphf}

Chapter~14 settled the FNR-zero side of the perfect-filter line.
This chapter turns the picture ninety degrees and settles the
FPR-zero side. The same architectural template, a perfect hash
addressing a per-element table, produces a structure with the dual
error behavior: every report of membership is correct, and the only
failure mode is silent omission. The two chapters together cover the
constructive realization of both arms of \Cref{def:perfect-filter-line};
neither arm is rhetorical and neither is structurally easier than the
other.

\subsection*{Recall the BHF}

The Bernoulli Hash Function of \Cref{def:bhf} sits on the FNR-zero
arm of the perfect-filter line: every $a \in A$ is reported as a
member with certainty, and the only error mode is a non-member
$x \notin A$ accepted by mistake. The construction uses a perfect
hash $\phi : A \to [n]$ that bijects the designated set onto its
addressing range, plus a fingerprint table $F : [n] \to \{0, 1\}^r$
that stores an $r$-bit string per slot. A query for $x$ reads slot
$\phi(x)$ and compares its contents to the fingerprint $g(x)$;
equality returns $\top$, inequality returns $\bot$. The
fingerprint-width parameter is $r = \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil$,
which buys $\fprate \le 2^{-r}$ by \Cref{prop:bhf-fpr} at a per-element
cost of $r$ bits plus a sublinear perfect-hash correction.

The result is space-optimal in the sense of \Cref{thm:bhf-optimality}:
the BHF reaches the entropy floor of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor} up to
the integer rounding the ceiling function introduces plus the
sublinear correction from $\phi$. The standard Bloom filter of
\Cref{def:bloom-filter} spends $\log_2 e \approx 1.4427$ times the
floor at every $\fprate$; the BHF spends exactly the floor plus one
bit of rounding. The closure properties of \Cref{prop:fpr-zero-closure}
keep BHF compositions on the FNR-zero arm under union and intersection,
so the optimality result is preserved under same-axis composition.
Chapter~14 closed with the construction in hand, the analysis
complete, and a single question deferred: what happens on the other
axis?

\subsection*{The opposite axis}

The FNR-zero arm is the right home for applications that cannot
tolerate a missed member: a routing table that must never drop a
known destination, a security check that must never admit a denied
identifier, a cache that must never miss a stored key. The price of
the guarantee is a small admittance rate for genuine non-members,
which the application designs around (a slow second-stage check
verifies the report) or accepts as the cost of doing business (a few
spurious admissions out of millions is below the noise floor of
operational concern).

A second class of applications inverts the trade-off. Top-$k$
retrieval over a corpus must never surface an off-topic document as
if it belonged in the top set, but can tolerate occasionally missing
a document that should have been included. A precision-oriented
search index must never claim a non-matching record as a match, but
the system as a whole can recover from an undercount by relaxing the
query and re-running. A deduplication layer must never declare two
distinct items the same, but a few duplicates surviving is recoverable
by a periodic second pass. In each setting, a false positive corrupts
the output in a way the application cannot absorb, and a false
negative is a tolerable miss that the surrounding system handles. The
desired guarantee flips: $\fprate = 0$ exactly, with $\fnrate > 0$
the controllable cost.

\Cref{prop:fpr-zero-closure} of chapter~6 already established that
the FPR-zero arm of the perfect-filter line is closed under union
and intersection, mirroring the closure on the FNR-zero arm. The
algebraic shape is symmetric. What chapter~6 deferred and the present
chapter supplies is the constructive question: which data structure
achieves $\fprate = 0$ at a controllable $\fnrate$, and what is the
space cost compared to the FNR-zero alternative?

\subsection*{Construction sketch}

The construction reuses the perfect hash $\phi : A \to [n]$ from
chapter~14. The first ingredient is identical: a bijection between
the designated set and its addressing range, constructed at build
time, behaving as a black-box function on the rest of the universe.
The substitution happens in the second ingredient. Instead of a
fingerprint table storing $g(a)$ for each $a$, the BPHF stores a
\emph{rank value} $\rho(a)$ at the slot $\phi(a)$. The rank is an
integer in $[0, n)$ that encodes $a$'s position in some
build-time-determined ordering of $A$, derived from a secondary hash
or from external metadata such as a document-relevance score. The
table that holds the ranks is $R : [n] \to \{0, 1\}^r$ with
$r = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ bits per slot, enough to distinguish
the $n$ possible ranks.

The query exploits the rank's structure to refuse false positives.
For a query $x$, the BPHF computes $\phi(x) \in [n]$, reads
$R[\phi(x)]$, and applies a consistency check: does the rank value at
slot $\phi(x)$ agree with what $x$'s rank would be if $x \in A$? The
check is a one-bit verdict. If $x \in A$, the check passes with high
probability and the BPHF returns $\top$. If $x \notin A$, the check
fails with certainty, and the BPHF returns $\bot$. The asymmetry in
the two outcomes is the construction's central feature: the check is
designed so that non-members cannot satisfy it, and members satisfy
it almost always. The few members that fail the check are the
controllable $\fnrate$; the absence of any non-member that passes
the check is the $\fprate = 0$ guarantee.

\subsection*{Chapter roadmap}

\Cref{sec:bphf-construction} gives the construction in full, with
the consistency-check pseudocode and the structural argument for
$\fprate = 0$. \Cref{sec:space-time-analysis} computes the per-element
bit budget ($\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ rather than the BHF's
$\lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil$) and the query and construction
times. \Cref{sec:ranked-search} develops the BPHF's archetypal
application, top-$k$ retrieval over a ranked corpus, where the
$\fprate = 0$ guarantee is exactly the precision invariant the
application needs. \Cref{sec:bridge-to-part5} closes Part~IV by
pairing the two constructions of chapters~14 and~15 and pointing
forward to Part~V's catalogue of structures that depart from the
Bernoulli assumptions the present chapter still respects.
