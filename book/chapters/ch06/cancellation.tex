\section{Cancellation and the Loss of One-Sidedness}
\label{sec:cancellation}

The operand rates of \Cref{sec:union-of-sets} and \Cref{sec:other-set-ops}
were drawn from one-sided approximate sets sitting on the perfect filter
line of \Cref{def:one-sided}. The union of two FNR-zero Bloom filters
stayed FNR-zero; the intersection of two FNR-zero Bloom filters stayed
FNR-zero; the worked examples landed back on the same axis the operands
came from. This section asks the natural follow-up question. Are the
operations of chapter~6 always axis-preserving in this way, or can a
chain of operations take an operand off the line entirely? The answer
is that composition can both move a one-sided set to the other axis
and force both rates positive at once. The phenomenon is unavoidable
once the complement operator enters the picture and intersection
involves a mixture of axes, and §6.4 will reread it as a closure
question about the perfect filter line.

\subsection*{Complement swaps which axis}

The simplest escape from a single axis is the complement operation.
\Cref{prop:complement-swap} stated the swap at the rate level: if
$\ASet{A}$ has rates $(\fprate_A, \fnrate_A)$, then
$\complement \ASet{A}$ has rates
$(\fprate_\complement, \fnrate_\complement) = (\fnrate_A, \fprate_A)$.
For a one-sided operand the consequences are immediate.

\begin{proposition}[Complement swaps the perfect-filter axis]
\label{prop:complement-axis-swap}
Let $\ASet{A}$ be a one-sided approximate set in the sense of
\Cref{def:one-sided}. Then $\complement \ASet{A}$ is also one-sided,
but on the other axis: if $\ASet{A}$ is FNR-zero then
$\complement \ASet{A}$ is FPR-zero, and if $\ASet{A}$ is FPR-zero then
$\complement \ASet{A}$ is FNR-zero.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{prop:complement-swap} the complement sends
$(\fprate_A, \fnrate_A)$ to $(\fnrate_A, \fprate_A)$. If $\ASet{A}$ is
FNR-zero then $\fnrate_A = 0 < \fprate_A$, so the swap produces
$(\fprate_\complement, \fnrate_\complement) = (0, \fprate_A)$ with
$\fprate_\complement = 0 < \fnrate_\complement$: the complement is
FPR-zero. The FPR-zero case is symmetric.
\end{proof}

Geometrically the rate plane has two coordinate axes and the
complement operator exchanges them. A Bloom filter with $\fprate = 0.01$
and $\fnrate = 0$ sits at the point $(0.01, 0)$ on the FNR-zero axis.
Its complement sits at the point $(0, 0.01)$ on the FPR-zero axis. The
two structures encode the same information in dual senses. The Bloom
filter is certain about absence and uncertain about presence, and its
complement is certain about presence and uncertain about absence. A
single application of complement walks across the rate plane from one
axis to the other; nothing in between is visited because the operation
is a discrete reflection rather than a continuous deformation.

\subsection*{Worked example: complement of a Bloom union}

The first compound escape combines union and complement. Let
$\ASet{A}$ and $\ASet{B}$ be two FNR-zero Bloom filters with operand
rates
\[
  \fprate_A = 0.01, \qquad \fnrate_A = 0,
  \qquad
  \fprate_B = 0.02, \qquad \fnrate_B = 0,
\]
the same numerical example as \Cref{sec:union-of-sets} and
\Cref{sec:other-set-ops}. The union $\ASet{A} \cup \ASet{B}$ inherits
both rates from \Cref{prop:union-fpr-fnr}: it is FNR-zero with
\[
  \fprate_\cup \;=\; 1 - (1 - 0.01)(1 - 0.02) \;=\; 0.0298,
  \qquad
  \fnrate_\cup \;=\; 0.
\]
Now form the complement $\complement (\ASet{A} \cup \ASet{B})$ and apply
\Cref{prop:complement-swap}. The complement swaps the union's two rates:
\[
  \fprate'
  \;=\; \fnrate_\cup
  \;=\; 0,
  \qquad
  \fnrate'
  \;=\; \fprate_\cup
  \;=\; 0.0298.
\]
The original two operands sat on the FNR-zero axis. The union stayed on
that axis. The complement of the union moved to the FPR-zero axis. A
two-step chain (union, then complement) has walked a one-sided
approximate set from one half of the perfect filter line to the other.
The composed structure is still one-sided, and still has only one degree
of rate-freedom, but the certain direction has flipped. Where the
union answered ``is $x$ in $A$ or in $B$?'' and reported $\bot$ with
certainty, the complement of the union answers ``is $x$ outside both
$A$ and $B$?'' and reports $\top$ with certainty. The rate algebra has
done the bookkeeping for us; the qualitative content of the structure
has changed.

\subsection*{Worked example: intersection with a complemented operand}

The second compound escape composes intersection with a single
complement and produces both rates positive at once. Let $\ASet{A}$
again be FNR-zero with $\fprate_A = 0.01, \fnrate_A = 0$, and let
$\ASet{B}$ be FNR-zero with $\fprate_B = 0.02, \fnrate_B = 0$. The
complement $\complement \ASet{B}$ has rates $(0, 0.02)$ by
\Cref{prop:complement-swap}; it sits on the FPR-zero axis. The mixed
intersection $\ASet{A} \cap \complement \ASet{B}$ asks both operands
to report $\top$ on points of $A \cap \complement B = A \setminus B$;
this is the difference of \Cref{def:difference-of-sets}, which the
intersection-with-complement decomposition makes explicit.

Apply \Cref{prop:intersection-fpr-fnr} to the two operands
$\ASet{A}$ with rates $(\fprate_A, \fnrate_A) = (0.01, 0)$ and
$\complement \ASet{B}$ with rates $(\fnrate_B, \fprate_B) = (0, 0.02)$:
\[
  \fprate_{\cap}
  \;=\; \fprate_A \cdot \fnrate_B
  \;=\; 0.01 \times 0
  \;=\; 0,
\]
\[
  \fnrate_{\cap}
  \;=\; 1 - (1 - \fnrate_A)(1 - \fprate_B)
  \;=\; 1 - (1 - 0)(1 - 0.02)
  \;=\; 0.02.
\]
The composition has landed at $(0, 0.02)$ on the FPR-zero axis in this
specific arithmetic; both numbers came out as integer-times-zero
products of the FNR-zero structure of the operands. The point is not
that the result is generic; the point is that the moment one operand's
FNR is genuinely positive, both output rates also become positive. Take
the same composition with $\fprate_A = 0.01$, $\fnrate_A = 0$ and
$\complement \ASet{B}$ having rates $(\fprate_B^\complement,
\fnrate_B^\complement) = (\fnrate_B, \fprate_B) = (0.005, 0.02)$
(coming from a two-sided $\ASet{B}$ with rates $(0.02, 0.005)$):
\[
  \fprate_{\cap}
  \;=\; \fprate_A \cdot \fprate_B^\complement
  \;=\; 0.01 \times 0.005
  \;=\; 5 \times 10^{-5},
\]
\[
  \fnrate_{\cap}
  \;=\; 1 - (1 - \fnrate_A)(1 - \fnrate_B^\complement)
  \;=\; 1 - 1 \times 0.98
  \;=\; 0.02.
\]
Both rates are now strictly positive. The composition has left both
axes and landed in the two-sided interior of the rate plane. The
operands did not need to be far from the line for this to happen;
even an arbitrarily small leak in the FNR-zero structure of one
operand produces a two-sided composed set.

\begin{proposition}[Mixed-axis intersection escapes the line]
\label{prop:mixed-intersection-escape}
Let $\ASet{A}$ be FNR-zero with $\fprate_A > 0$ and let $\ASet{B}$ be
FPR-zero with $\fnrate_B > 0$. Then $\ASet{A} \cap \ASet{B}$ generically
has both rates strictly positive: $\fprate_{\cap} = 0$ and
$\fnrate_{\cap} = \fnrate_B > 0$ in one boundary regime, while a
two-sided $\ASet{B}$ with any positive $\fprate_B$ in place of the
strict FPR-zero gives $\fprate_{\cap} = \fprate_A \fprate_B > 0$ and
$\fnrate_{\cap} = 1 - (1 - \fnrate_B) > 0$. The line is not preserved by
this composition.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substitute the operand rates into \Cref{prop:intersection-fpr-fnr}. The
boundary case has $\fprate_B = 0$, giving $\fprate_{\cap} =
\fprate_A \cdot 0 = 0$ and $\fnrate_{\cap} = 1 - (1 - 0)(1 - \fnrate_B) =
\fnrate_B$. The non-boundary case has $\fprate_B > 0$, giving the two
positive expressions above. In neither case does the result fall on the
FNR-zero or FPR-zero axis of the operands.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{The escape, viewed geometrically}

The rate plane $[0, 1]^2$ has two distinguished one-dimensional loci:
the FNR-zero axis (the segment with $\fnrate = 0$ and $\fprate > 0$)
and the FPR-zero axis (the segment with $\fprate = 0$ and
$\fnrate > 0$). Their union is the perfect filter line. Three classes
of compositional behavior arise.

\emph{Axis-preserving operations.} Union of two FNR-zero sets stays
FNR-zero. Intersection of two FNR-zero sets stays FNR-zero. Symmetric
statements hold on the FPR-zero side. These are the compositions in
which both operands sit on the same axis and the rate algebra returns a
result on the same axis. The operations of \Cref{prop:union-fpr-fnr}
and \Cref{prop:intersection-fpr-fnr} drive false positives and false
negatives toward zero on the operand's axis without ever ``leaking''
the other rate.

\emph{Axis-swapping operations.} Complement of a one-sided set is
one-sided on the other axis (\Cref{prop:complement-axis-swap}). The
composition does not stay on either axis individually but stays on the
line as a set: it walks across the line, from one half to the other,
in a single discrete step.

\emph{Axis-leaving operations.} Intersection of an FNR-zero set with
an FPR-zero set is generically two-sided
(\Cref{prop:mixed-intersection-escape}). Difference $\ASet{A} \setminus
\ASet{B}$, being intersection with the complemented second operand, is
two-sided unless one of the operands has both rates zero or both
operands sit on the same axis after the complement. These are the
compositions that leave the line entirely; the composed rate lives in
the interior of the rate square, with both directions of report now
uncertain.

The three classes exhaust the qualitative behavior of the chapter~6
operations on the perfect filter line. The line is closed under axis-
preserving operations, mapped to itself by axis-swapping operations,
and exited by axis-leaving operations. \Cref{sec:perfect-filter-line}
will state this classification as a closure proposition; for now it is
enough that we have identified the qualitative regimes and connected
each one to a specific composition.

\subsection*{Why this matters}

The cancellation phenomena are not abstract. Three downstream chapters
depend on whether a composed set sits on the perfect filter line.

\Cref{ch:classification-measures} develops PPV and NPV from the FPR
and FNR. A one-sided structure simplifies the formulas: an FNR-zero
structure has $\NPV = 1$ on its domain, and the confidence-interval
treatment reduces to the one rate that is nonzero. A two-sided
structure exercises the full machinery, with posterior weight on both
directions of report and intervals on both rates.

\Cref{ch:optimal-bernoulli-sets} develops the Bernoulli Hash Function
and the entropy-floor argument for FNR-zero approximate sets. The
information-theoretic lower bound is $-\log_2 \fprate$ bits per element
at false-positive rate $\fprate$, and the BHF achieves this floor up to
a small additive constant. A compositional chain that leaves the axis
puts the result outside the regime the bound measures; the resulting
two-sided structure would need a separate analysis to assess
space-optimality.

\Cref{ch:perfect-hash-filters} develops the perfect hash filter and
achieves FPR-zero on a designated input set $A$, paying with a
construction-time perfect-hash computation. The chapter operates
entirely on the FPR-zero axis. Composing a perfect hash filter with a
Bloom filter via mixed-axis intersection produces a two-sided structure
by \Cref{prop:mixed-intersection-escape}, and the certain $\top$
answer is lost.

The pattern across chapters 7, 14, and 15 is the same. Each chapter is
the constructive realization of one half of the perfect filter line at
the lowest space cost, and each pays a real penalty when a
compositional chain forces the result off the line. Composition
discipline is the price of staying near the floor.

\subsection*{What comes next}

\Cref{sec:perfect-filter-line} states the perfect filter line as a
formal definition and collects the closure results of this section
into a coherent algebraic statement. The FNR-zero axis is closed
under union and intersection but not under complement; the FPR-zero
axis is closed dually; the union of the two axes is closed under no
single operation but is mapped to itself by axis-swapping operations
and exited by mixed-axis intersection. The section organizes the
classification, and chapters 7, 14, and 15 then take up the
consequences of staying on the line or leaving it.
