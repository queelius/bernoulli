\section{Approximate Joins in Databases}
\label{sec:approximate-joins}

\Cref{sec:relation-composition} treated the natural join algebraically:
two approximate relations go in, the conjunction of their membership
predicates comes out, and the FPR multiplies by \Cref{thm:join-rates}.
Nothing in that derivation said how the join is computed. For two
relations of $n$ tuples each, naively evaluating the predicate
$\tilde R_1(t, u) \land \tilde R_2(u, v)$ for every candidate triple is
$O(n^2)$, and for the similarity-join variant of the problem (where
the matching condition is not equality on a shared attribute but
threshold similarity between two rows) it is $O(n^2)$ even without
the predicate evaluations. The database literature has spent thirty
years on sub-quadratic algorithms for this regime. This section reads
one family of those algorithms, the MinHash-based similarity join,
through the framework of \Cref{sec:relation-composition} and shows
that the published FPR/FNR analyses are the chapter 12 join rates
specialized to a particular implementation of the predicate.

\subsection*{The similarity-join problem}

Let $R_1$ and $R_2$ be two relations whose rows are sets drawn from a
common universe of tokens. A row $r$ might be the bag of words in a
document, the bag of $k$-shingles in a string, the bag of bigrams in
a name, or the bag of features extracted from a record. The
\emph{similarity join} at threshold $\theta$ is the relation
\[
  R_1 \bowtie_\theta R_2 \;=\; \{ (r_1, r_2) \in R_1 \times R_2 :
    J(r_1, r_2) \ge \theta \},
\]
where $J(r_1, r_2) = |r_1 \cap r_2| / |r_1 \cup r_2|$ is the Jaccard
similarity of the two sets. The output is the set of cross-relation
pairs whose contents overlap by at least a $\theta$ fraction. The
applications run across record linkage (matching customer entries
across two databases), near-duplicate detection (matching documents
across two crawls), and entity resolution (matching mentions across
two corpora). The classical evaluation is two nested loops: for each
$r_1 \in R_1$, for each $r_2 \in R_2$, compute $J(r_1, r_2)$ and emit
the pair if the similarity is above $\theta$. The cost is $|R_1|
\cdot |R_2|$ Jaccard evaluations, each of which is itself linear in
the row sizes. At million-row scale this is prohibitive.

The natural-join machinery of \Cref{sec:relation-composition} reads
this problem as a single approximate-relation lookup. Build an
indicator $\tilde S_\theta \subseteq R_1 \times R_2$ whose membership
predicate is ``$J(r_1, r_2) \ge \theta$,'' implement it with a sketch
that estimates Jaccard without materializing the full intersection,
and read FPR and FNR off the sketch's error bound. The MinHash sketch
of chapter~10 (\Cref{def:minhash}) is exactly such an estimator.

\subsection*{MinHash similarity join}

The chapter 10 construction (\Cref{sec:minhash-lsh}) replaces a set
$A$ with a $k$-vector of independent minimum-hash values, its MinHash
\emph{signature}. The collision probability of two signatures'
$i$th coordinates is the Jaccard similarity of the underlying sets
(\Cref{prop:minhash-collision}), so the fraction of matching
coordinates is an unbiased estimator of $J(A, B)$. Specialized to the
similarity-join setting:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Build the MinHash signature $\sigma(r)$ of length $k$ for
    every row $r \in R_1 \cup R_2$. Cost: $O(k \cdot \sum |r|)$
    hashes, linear in the total token count.
  \item For each candidate pair $(r_1, r_2)$, count the matches
    $|\{i : \sigma(r_1)_i = \sigma(r_2)_i\}|$ and divide by $k$ to
    get the MinHash Jaccard estimate $\hat J(r_1, r_2)$.
  \item Emit the pair if $\hat J(r_1, r_2) \ge \theta$.
\end{enumerate}

Step 2 is still $O(|R_1| \cdot |R_2|)$ candidate pairs if we leave it
as written, but each per-pair cost drops from $O(\max(|r_1|, |r_2|))$
hash comparisons to $O(k)$ signature comparisons with $k$ a constant
independent of row size. That alone is a useful constant-factor
speedup for short signatures. The sub-quadratic gain comes from the
LSH layer below, which prunes the candidate set before the per-pair
comparison is even attempted.

The framework reading is that the similarity join is implemented as
the approximate relation
\[
  \tilde S_\theta(r_1, r_2) \;=\; [\hat J(r_1, r_2) \ge \theta],
\]
whose two error rates come directly from the MinHash error bound
(\Cref{prop:minhash-error}). False positive: $J(r_1, r_2) < \theta$
but $\hat J(r_1, r_2) \ge \theta$, which requires the estimator to
overshoot the truth by at least $\theta - J(r_1, r_2)$. False
negative: $J(r_1, r_2) \ge \theta$ but $\hat J(r_1, r_2) < \theta$,
which requires the estimator to undershoot the truth by at least
$J(r_1, r_2) - \theta$. Both events are bounded by the chapter~10
Hoeffding-style bound
$\Prob{|\hat J - J| \ge t} \le 2 \exp(-2 k t^2)$, with $t$ equal to
the relevant gap. Larger $k$ shrinks both rates exponentially; the
operating point of the join is a choice of signature length.

\subsection*{LSH bands}

The remaining quadratic factor is the candidate enumeration. The LSH
layer of \Cref{sec:minhash-lsh} replaces the all-pairs scan with a
hash-bucket reduction. Partition the signature into $b$ bands of $r$
rows each (so $k = b \cdot r$), hash each band of each signature into
a bucket, and declare $(r_1, r_2)$ a candidate pair if any of their
$b$ bands collide in any bucket. The candidate set is the union of
within-bucket pairs over all $b$ band-level hash tables.

The probability that two signatures collide on a single band of $r$
rows is $J^r$ (each band is $r$ independent coordinates, each
collision with probability $J$ by \Cref{prop:minhash-collision}).
The probability that they collide on at least one of $b$ bands is
$1 - (1 - J^r)^b$. As a function of $J$, this is the S-curve of LSH:
nearly zero for $J$ below a threshold, nearly one above. The
inflection point is at $J^* \approx (1/b)^{1/r}$; the slope at the
inflection determines how sharp the separation is. Choosing $(b, r)$
to place $J^* \approx \theta$ concentrates the candidate set on pairs
with $J \approx \theta$ or higher and excludes pairs with $J$ much
below $\theta$. The candidate-set size is sub-quadratic in $|R_1| +
|R_2|$ when most pairs have $J \ll \theta$, which is the typical
sparse-similarity regime of the database applications above.

The LSH layer is itself an approximate relation
$\tilde C \subseteq R_1 \times R_2$ whose membership predicate is
``the pair collides on at least one band.'' Composed with the
MinHash-threshold predicate $\tilde S_\theta$ above, the full
similarity-join pipeline is the intersection of two approximate
relations:
\[
  \tilde S_\theta \cap \tilde C
  \;=\; \{(r_1, r_2) : \hat J(r_1, r_2) \ge \theta \text{ and }
    \tilde C(r_1, r_2) = \top\}.
\]
The intersection is the chapter~6 intersection of approximate sets,
and its rates compose by \Cref{thm:join-rates}.

\subsection*{FPR and FNR for the join classifier}

Two error rates govern the similarity-join pipeline as a binary
classifier on cross-relation pairs. False positive: the pipeline
outputs a pair whose true Jaccard is below $\theta$. False negative:
the pipeline omits a pair whose true Jaccard is at or above $\theta$.

For pure MinHash thresholding (no LSH), both rates are read off the
chapter~10 error bound. A pair with true similarity $J$ is emitted
with probability
\[
  \Prob{\hat J \ge \theta \Given J}
  \;=\; \Prob{\text{Binomial}(k, J)/k \ge \theta},
\]
since $\hat J \cdot k$ is binomial with $k$ trials and success
probability $J$. For $J < \theta$, this is the upper-tail
binomial-deviation probability and bounds the per-pair false-positive
rate; for $J \ge \theta$, the complementary lower-tail probability
bounds the per-pair false-negative rate. Both decay exponentially in
$k(\theta - J)^2$, by Hoeffding (\Cref{prop:minhash-error}). The
pipeline rates are the per-pair rates averaged over the distribution
of true similarities across the cross-relation.

The LSH layer modifies the false-negative side. A pair with true
similarity $J$ survives LSH with probability $1 - (1 - J^r)^b$ (the
S-curve), and the MinHash threshold acts on the survivors. A truly
similar pair (one with $J \ge \theta$) is missed if it fails the LSH
collision test or passes LSH but fails the threshold; the two events
combine by the chapter~3 OR-under-noise rule. The LSH layer also
modifies the false-positive rate not by changing the per-pair
probability but by shrinking the denominator: fewer candidates means
fewer chances for the threshold to emit a spurious pair.

The pair $(b, r)$ tunes the trade-off. Increasing $r$ sharpens the
S-curve (fewer false positives from low-similarity pairs, more false
negatives near the threshold); increasing $b$ widens it (more false
positives, fewer false negatives). The standard tuning sets $(1/b)^{1/r}
= \theta$ to centre the inflection on the threshold and then chooses
the band size $r$ to control the steepness. Once $(b, r)$ are fixed,
the MinHash $k = b \cdot r$ is determined, and the per-pair error
decays exponentially in $k$ as above.

\subsection*{Worked example}

Take $|R_1| = |R_2| = 1000$, similarity threshold $\theta = 0.5$, and
MinHash parameter $k = 200$ partitioned into $b = 20$ bands of $r = 10$
rows each. The total candidate space is $10^6$ cross-relation pairs.

\emph{Per-pair MinHash threshold rates.} For a pair with true
similarity $J = 0.3$ (well below threshold), the Hoeffding bound gives
$\Prob{\hat J \ge 0.5} \le \exp(-2 \cdot 200 \cdot 0.2^2)
\approx \exp(-16) \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-7}$. For a pair with true
similarity $J = 0.7$ (well above threshold), the same bound gives
$\Prob{\hat J < 0.5} \le \exp(-16) \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-7}$. Both
rates are negligible at $k = 200$ for pairs that are not on the
boundary. For a boundary pair with $J = 0.5$, the threshold rate is
$1/2$ (the estimator is symmetric around the threshold), and the
sharp gradient of the rate function near the boundary means pairs
within a few percent of $\theta$ contribute most of the classifier
error.

\emph{LSH band rates.} The per-pair LSH collision probability is
$1 - (1 - J^{10})^{20}$. For $J = 0.7$, $J^{10} \approx 0.028$ and
$(1 - 0.028)^{20} \approx 0.566$, so the LSH passes the pair with
probability $1 - 0.566 \approx 0.434$. For $J = 0.5$, $J^{10}
\approx 9.8 \times 10^{-4}$ and $(1 - 9.8 \times 10^{-4})^{20}
\approx 0.981$, so the LSH passes the pair with probability
$\approx 0.019$. For $J = 0.3$, $J^{10} \approx 5.9 \times 10^{-6}$
and the LSH passes with probability $\approx 1.2 \times 10^{-4}$.
The S-curve is steep but not sharp at $\theta = 0.5$ with $(b, r) =
(20, 10)$: most truly similar pairs at $J = 0.7$ survive, half of
boundary pairs at $J = 0.6$ survive, and very few low-similarity
pairs at $J = 0.3$ survive.

\emph{Output size estimate.} Suppose the true similarity distribution
across the $10^6$ pairs has $0.1\%$ at $J \ge 0.5$ (1000 truly
similar pairs), $1\%$ at $0.3 \le J < 0.5$, and the remaining
$98.9\%$ at $J < 0.3$. The candidate set is then
\[
  1000 \cdot 0.434 + 10000 \cdot 0.019 + 989000 \cdot 1.2 \times 10^{-4}
  \;\approx\; 434 + 190 + 119
  \;\approx\; 743
\]
pairs, against a maximum possible $10^6$. The candidate set is three
orders of magnitude smaller than the all-pairs scan, which is the
sub-quadratic speedup. The threshold pass on those 743 candidates is
$O(k)$ per pair, total cost $\approx 1.5 \times 10^5$ signature
comparisons rather than $2 \times 10^8$. Both the false-positive and
false-negative rates of the final classifier are dominated by the LSH
S-curve at this parameter setting; the per-pair MinHash threshold is
sharp enough to add negligible additional error. This is the standard
operating regime of MinHash + LSH similarity joins in record linkage
and near-duplicate detection.
