\section{From Approximate Sets to Approximate Relations}
\label{sec:sets-to-relations}

Chapter 5 (\Cref{ch:approximate-sets}) introduced approximate sets as
the predicate-level lift of the noisy bit: a function from a universe
$\Universe$ to $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$, with a single
$(\fprate, \fnrate)$ pair governing every query. The construction was
forced: a classical set is its membership predicate, and replacing the
predicate with a noisy one is the only place the noise has to live
(\Cref{sec:from-atom-to-set}). The same construction, applied to a
different universe, gives the central object of this chapter.

A binary relation $R \subseteq T \times U$ is a set whose elements are
ordered pairs. The universe is the Cartesian product $T \times U$;
each element is a pair $(t, u)$; the membership predicate
$\SetIndicator{R} : T \times U \to \{\top, \bot\}$ returns $\top$ on
the pairs in $R$ and $\bot$ elsewhere. The classical correspondence
between a set and its indicator function holds with $T \times U$
substituted for $\Universe$. An \emph{approximate relation} is the
predicate-level analogue: a function
\[
  \tilde R : T \times U \longrightarrow \Bernoulli{\BoolType}
\]
whose per-query output $\tilde R(t, u) \in \{\top, \bot\}$ reports
whether $(t, u)$ is in $R$, with the rates of error governed by the
chapter 5 framework. Nothing in the construction is new. The universe
got bigger; the apparatus did not.

The two rates have the same meaning they had in chapter 5, with pairs
substituted for elements. If $(t, u) \notin R$, the correct verdict is
$\bot$ and the structure errs only by reporting $\top$. The
false-positive rate of the approximate relation is the conditional
probability of that error:
\[
  \fprate \;=\; \Prob{\tilde R(t, u) = \top \Given (t, u) \notin R}.
\]
If $(t, u) \in R$, the correct verdict is $\top$ and the structure
errs only by reporting $\bot$. The false-negative rate is the
conditional probability of that error:
\[
  \fnrate \;=\; \Prob{\tilde R(t, u) = \bot \Given (t, u) \in R}.
\]
These are the two rates of \Cref{def:bernoulli-bool}, with the
membership partition of $T \times U$ standing in for the membership
partition of $\Universe$. The block structure is two-block: the
in-relation block $R$ and the out-of-relation block
$(T \times U) \setminus R$. One rate per block.

\subsection*{Two examples to fix the picture}

The first example is the \emph{friend-of} relation on a population
of persons. Let $P$ be the set of persons and let
$F \subseteq P \times P$ be the relation that holds between two
persons when one names the other as a friend. An approximate friend-of
relation $\tilde F$ might be represented compactly as a Bloom-style
structure that stores each known friend pair as a key. A query
$\tilde F(\text{Alice}, \text{Bob})$ returns $\top$ if any of the
hashed positions for the pair $(\text{Alice}, \text{Bob})$ are set in
the underlying bit array. The structure has a structural
false-negative rate of zero (a true friend pair was inserted, so its
bits are set) and a tunable false-positive rate that depends on the
bit-array size, the hash count, and the number of stored pairs, by
exactly the chapter 5 Bloom-filter analysis applied to pairs rather
than to single keys.

The second example is the \emph{word-in-document} relation
$\tilde R \subseteq W \times D$, where $W$ is a vocabulary and $D$ is
a corpus of documents. A pair $(w, d)$ is in $R$ when word $w$ appears
in document $d$. This is the inverted-index relation of classical
information retrieval. The exact representation is a posting list per
word; the approximate representation can be a Bloom filter per
document (storing the set of words it contains) or a single global
filter over $W \times D$. In either case, the per-query predicate
returns a noisy Boolean, governed by the two rates of the chapter 5
framework. The space-versus-accuracy trade-off that chapter 5 derived
for sets carries over verbatim: more bits, smaller false-positive
rate.

\subsection*{What changes structurally}

The chapter 5 framework lifts to relations without modification, but
the chapter 9 framework for $n$-ary functions
(\Cref{ch:approximate-functions}, \Cref{sec:n-ary-channels}) is also
on the table now. The membership predicate
$\SetIndicator{R} : T \times U \to \{\top, \bot\}$ is a binary function
in the chapter 9 sense: two inputs, one Boolean output. At the
predicate level the model order is $|T \times U|$, one row of the
channel matrix per input pair; Axiom~2 collapses this to two rate-rows
indexed by the membership partition, exactly as chapter 5 collapsed
the per-element rates of an approximate set. At the truth level the
order is two: in the relation or not. Whether to view $\tilde R$
through the chapter 5 lens (as an approximate set over the universe
$T \times U$) or through the chapter 9 lens (as an approximate binary
function $T \times U \to \BoolType$) is a matter of which downstream
machinery is easier to reach for. Both views are the same object.

\subsection*{Signpost}

What \Cref{sec:relation-composition} adds is structure on the universe
$T \times U$ itself: the relational operations of natural join,
projection, and selection take approximate relations to approximate
relations, and the propagation of $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ through each
operation follows the chapter 6 composition theorems lifted to pairs.
\Cref{sec:approximate-joins} then specializes the join machinery to
the database setting and recovers the MinHash similarity join of
chapter 10 §10.4 as one instance of the framework.
