\section{From Approximate Sets to Approximate Relations}
\label{sec:sets-to-relations}

A binary relation $R \subseteq T \times U$ is a set whose elements are ordered
pairs, so the chapter-5 construction (\Cref{ch:approximate-sets}) applies with
the universe $T \times U$ in place of $\Universe$; relations of higher arity live
the same way on a product of attribute domains. We use finite relations with set
semantics in the sense of \textcite{codd-1970}\index{relational algebra}: no duplicate rows, no nulls, and
no approximate comparison of attribute values. A relation is its membership
predicate $\SetIndicator{R}$ (\Cref{sec:from-atom-to-set}), and an
\emph{approximate relation}\index{approximate relation} is a noisy version of
that predicate, $\tilde R : T \times U \to \Bernoulli{\BoolType}$, with the two
rates of \Cref{def:bernoulli-bool}.

What the set-level chapters could leave implicit must now be said explicitly,
because relational operators reuse their inputs. Three things are kept apart:
the \emph{exact relation}, which is fixed and carries no prior; the
\emph{construction randomness}, sampled once when the structure is built; and
the \emph{source identity} of each probe, the stored random outcome that the
probe reads.

\begin{definition}[Approximate relation]
\label{def:approx-relation}
Fix a finite tuple universe $\mathcal{D}$ and an exact relation $R \subseteq
\mathcal{D}$. An approximate relation is a family of membership functions
$\tilde R_\vartheta : \mathcal{D} \to \{\top, \bot\}$ indexed by a construction
seed $\vartheta$. The seed is drawn once, and every later probe reads the
resulting frozen representation. For a fixed $t \notin R$ the false-positive
rate is $\Prob{\tilde R_\vartheta(t) = \top}$, and for a fixed $t \in R$ the
false-negative rate is $\Prob{\tilde R_\vartheta(t) = \bot}$, both over
$\vartheta$.
\end{definition}

These are pointwise construction probabilities\index{false-positive rate}\index{false-negative rate} with the relation and the query
held fixed. They are not the probability that an observed positive is false
(that is the predictive value of \Cref{ch:classification-measures}), and repeating
one probe $N$ times supplies one outcome, not $N$ trials.

\begin{definition}[Source model]
\label{def:source-model}
A \emph{source map}\index{source map} $\kappa : \mathcal{D} \to I$ names the stored outcome each
tuple reads, with exact source bits $x_i$ satisfying $\SetIndicator{R}(t) =
x_{\kappa(t)}$; tuples sharing a source (aliases) must share their exact truth
value. Under the \emph{independent-source model} the random source bits $X_i$
are mutually independent, with $\Prob{X_i = 1} = \fprate_i$ when $x_i = 0$ and
$1 - \fnrate_i$ when $x_i = 1$, and $\tilde R(t) = X_{\kappa(t)}$. The
\emph{independent-tuple model} is the special case in which $\kappa$ is injective
and the rates are constant on each truth class\index{independence!across elements}, $(\fprate_i, \fnrate_i) =
(\fprate, \fnrate)$, which is \Cref{ax:element-indep} on the tuple universe.
\end{definition}

Every occurrence of a source reads the same bit, and distinct relations may share
sources. A symmetry-preserving structure that answers $R(a, b)$ and $R(b, a)$
from one canonical key is the motivating case: the query $R(a, b) \land R(b, a)$
then has the single source $X_{\{a, b\}}$ as its lineage, not a conjunction of
two independent bits. The independent-tuple model is an idealized channel, not a
consequence of storing pairs in a Bloom filter: shared bit arrays and finite hash
families introduce dependence (\Cref{ch:not-bernoulli}). Shared \emph{storage}
is also different from shared \emph{outcomes}. In the
perfect-hash-plus-fingerprint construction (\Cref{ch:perfect-hash-filters}), with
an ideal fingerprint function independent of the placement, two absent keys that
land in the same slot still match it independently, each with probability
$2^{-M}$; a repeated canonical key, by contrast, reads one outcome twice.

Two examples fix the picture. The \emph{friend-of} relation $F \subseteq P \times
P$ on a population $P$ stores known friend pairs in a Bloom-style structure: a
probe $\tilde F(\text{Alice}, \text{Bob})$ reads the hashed positions for the
pair, with $\fnrate = 0$ and a tunable $\fprate$\index{Bloom filter}, the chapter-5 analysis applied
to pairs. Conditional on the filter's occupancy, distinct absent pairs test
positive independently at a common rate; unconditionally they are dependent
through that occupancy, a distinction \Cref{sec:query-contracts} turns into a
usable bound. The \emph{word-in-document} relation $W \times D$, the inverted
index of information retrieval, holds $(w, d)$ when word $w$ occurs in document
$d$, representable as one Bloom filter per document.

The chapter-9 view (\Cref{ch:approximate-functions}, \Cref{sec:n-ary-channels})
is equivalent: $\SetIndicator{R}$ is a binary function $T \times U \to \BoolType$
whose channel has one row per input pair, collapsing under Axiom~2 to model order
two (in the relation or not), exactly as chapter~5 collapsed the per-element
rates of an approximate set. \Cref{sec:relation-composition} adds the relational
operators; \Cref{sec:relation-invariants} asks which structural invariants an
approximation can keep; \Cref{sec:query-contracts} states what error guarantee a
whole query inherits from its sources; and \Cref{sec:approximate-joins} reads the
MinHash similarity join of chapter~10 in the same vocabulary.
