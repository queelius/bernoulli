\section{Preservation of Relational Invariants}
\label{sec:relation-invariants}

A relation may be required to be reflexive, symmetric, single-valued, or
transitive in every realization. Per-tuple rates alone do not secure such global
properties. We first compute preservation probabilities under the
independent-tuple model, then compare three ways to enforce a constraint:
sharing a canonical source, adding reported tuples, and deleting them. The three
have different rate and dependence consequences; they are specific constructions,
not a classification of all repairs. Throughout, $R \subseteq A \times A$ (or $X
\times Y$) is fixed and has the named property, and $\tilde R$ has rates
$(\fprate, \tprate)$ with $\tprate = 1 - \fnrate$.

\subsection*{Reflexivity}

\begin{theorem}[Reflexivity]
\label{thm:rel-reflexive}
If $R$ is reflexive, $\tilde R$ is reflexive with probability $\tprate^{|A|}$,
which tends to $0$ as $|A| \to \infty$ for fixed $\tprate < 1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The $|A|$ diagonal pairs\index{reflexivity} are distinct true members, and reflexivity requires all
of their independent trials to accept.
\end{proof}

When reflexivity is known in advance, answering $\top$ on the diagonal enforces it
exactly, with diagonal true-positive rate one and no false positive there. That is
a rule for known forced tuples, not an OR over a singleton trial.

\subsection*{Symmetry and its closure}

For $a \ne b$, symmetry requires the ordered bits of $(a, b)$ and $(b, a)$ to
agree; diagonal pairs impose no constraint.

\begin{theorem}[Symmetry]
\label{thm:rel-symmetric}
Let $P$ and $N$ count the positive and negative unordered off-diagonal pairs of a
symmetric $R$. Then $\tilde R$ is symmetric with probability
\[
  (\tprate^2 + \fnrate^2)^P \bigl((1 - \fprate)^2 + \fprate^2\bigr)^N,
\]
which tends to $0$ as $P + N$ grows when $0 < \tprate < 1$ and $0 < \fprate < 1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A positive pair agrees when both trials accept or both reject, a negative pair
when both reject or both accept; distinct unordered pairs use disjoint trials.
\end{proof}

The symmetric closure\index{symmetric closure} forces agreement by admitting a pair when either ordered
trial fires.

\begin{theorem}[Symmetric closure]
\label{thm:rel-symmetric-closure}
For symmetric $R$, the closure $\tilde R^{s}$, which admits $(x, y)$ when $(x, y)
\in \tilde R$ or $(y, x) \in \tilde R$, is symmetric in every realization. Off the
diagonal its false-positive and true-positive rates are $1 - (1 - \fprate)^2$ and
$1 - (1 - \tprate)^2$; on the diagonal they remain $\fprate$ and $\tprate$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $x \ne y$ the two trials are independent and, by symmetry of $R$, in the same
truth class, so the OR rejects exactly when both reject. For $x = y$ there is one
trial, and reading it twice does not create a second one.
\end{proof}

At $\tprate = 0.9$ the off-diagonal true-positive rate becomes $0.99$, paid for by
a false-positive rate of $1 - 0.95^2 = 0.0975$ at $\fprate = 0.05$. The two
repaired ordered bits within a pair are identical, hence dependent whenever their
common acceptance probability is interior; independence remains only between
distinct unordered pairs, and a later operator must use these shared source
identities (\Cref{thm:projection-shared-sources}).

\subsection*{Single-valuedness}

A relation $f \subseteq X \times Y$ is single-valued\index{single-valuedness} when each key has at most one
reported value. Having no spurious value is sufficient but not necessary: if the
true value is missed, one spurious value still leaves a single-valued row. Keys
without a true value must be counted too.

\begin{theorem}[Single-valuedness]
\label{thm:rel-functional}
Let $v = |Y| \ge 2$, let $d$ keys have a defined value and $u$ keys none, and put
\[
  q_1 = (1 - \fprate)^{v - 1} + \fnrate (v - 1) \fprate (1 - \fprate)^{v - 2},
  \qquad
  q_0 = (1 - \fprate)^{v} + v \fprate (1 - \fprate)^{v - 1}.
\]
Under the independent-tuple model $\tilde f$ is single-valued with probability
$q_1^{d} q_0^{u}$, which tends to $0$ as the number of keys grows for fixed $v \ge
2$, $0 < \fprate < 1$, and $\tprate > 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At a defined key the true-value indicator $T$ (mean $\tprate$) is independent of
the spurious count $K \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(v - 1, \fprate)$, and the row is
single-valued exactly when $T + K \le 1$, with probability $\Prob{K = 0} + \fnrate
\Prob{K = 1} = q_1$. At an undefined key the reported count is
$\mathrm{Binomial}(v, \fprate)$, and at most one report has probability $q_0$.
Distinct rows use disjoint trials.
\end{proof}

The familiar factor $(1 - \fprate)^{v - 1}$ is the probability of \emph{no
spurious report} at a defined key; it equals that row's single-valuedness
probability only when $\fnrate = 0$.

\subsection*{Transitivity}

Transitivity\index{transitivity} requires every reported two-step path to have a reported shortcut.
The premises are reported pairs, not merely true ones, and the triple constraints
overlap, so their probabilities cannot be multiplied in general. A disjoint
subfamily still gives a clean bound.

\begin{theorem}[A sufficient regime for vanishing transitivity]
\label{thm:rel-transitive}
Let $0 < \fprate, \tprate < 1$, $p_- = \min\{\fprate, \tprate\}$, $p_+ =
\max\{\fprate, \tprate\}$, and $n = |A|$. Then
\[
  \Prob{\tilde R \text{ transitive}}
  \le \bigl(1 - p_-^2 (1 - p_+)\bigr)^{\lfloor n/3 \rfloor}
  \longrightarrow 0 .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take $\lfloor n/3 \rfloor$ vertex-disjoint triples $(a, b, c)$. On each, reporting
$(a, b)$ and $(b, c)$ while rejecting $(a, c)$ violates transitivity, an event of
probability at least $p_-^2 (1 - p_+)$ over three distinct independent trials;
the events on disjoint triples are independent, and transitivity excludes all of
them.
\end{proof}

The interior-rate hypothesis matters: an empty exact relation with $\fprate = 0$
stays empty and transitive surely. There is no universal ordering of the four
invariants by preservation probability, and the bound implies nothing about the
cost of repairing transitivity.

\subsection*{Canonical sources and specific repairs}

Fix an equivalence on the tuple universe and call a constraint
\emph{class-induced}\index{class-induced constraint} when its satisfying relations are exactly the unions of
equivalence classes (orbits). Every orbit of an admissible exact relation is then
homogeneous, wholly inside or wholly outside $R$. Symmetry is class-induced, with
orbits $\{(a, b), (b, a)\}$ off the diagonal and singletons on it. Reflexivity is
not (it forces specified tuples), and single-valuedness is a cardinality cap per
row; both are handled separately.

\begin{theorem}[Orbitwise OR-closure]
\label{thm:rel-or-closure}
For a class-induced constraint, admit a whole orbit $O$ whenever one of its
$h(O) = |O|$ independent trials accepts. The result satisfies the constraint in
every realization, is idempotent, and has orbit rates
\[
  \fprate_O = 1 - (1 - \fprate)^{h(O)},
  \qquad
  \tprate_O = 1 - (1 - \tprate)^{h(O)}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
An orbit is rejected exactly when its $h(O)$ homogeneous independent trials all
reject; filling a reported orbit touches no other orbit, so a second application
changes nothing.
\end{proof}

A bound $h(O) \le m$ gives only bounds on the orbit rates; equality holds at the
actual orbit size. The bits within an orbit coincide, so tuple independence is
lost except on singleton or deterministic orbits.

\begin{theorem}[Canonical test]
\label{thm:rel-canonical}
For a class-induced constraint, answer a query $t$ with the single frozen trial on
a fixed representative of its orbit. The resulting $\tilde R^{c}$ satisfies the
constraint surely, keeps the per-tuple rates $(\fprate, \tprate)$, and under the
independent-tuple model has independent trials across orbits.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
All tuples of an orbit receive one answer, so each realization is a union of
orbits; homogeneity makes the representative a member exactly when the queried
tuple is; representatives of distinct orbits are distinct tuples.
\end{proof}

The invariant therefore costs nothing in \emph{marginal} rates\index{canonical representation}. Building the
structure over the quotient universe also reduces the stored keys, to $|R|/2$ for a
symmetric relation with empty diagonal, although encoding and fixed overheads keep
that from being a guaranteed halving of bytes. The price moves elsewhere: the
common orbit answer is a shared source in the sense of \Cref{def:source-model}, so
later operators see dependent tuples, and \Cref{sec:query-contracts} shows the
effect on query error and on the cost of exact inference. The OR-closure has a
different purpose, raising recall at a fixed raw rate while raising the
false-positive rate too, and its increase can be budgeted.

\begin{corollary}[Rate budget, with conditional space accounting]
\label{cor:rel-rate-budget}
If every orbit has size at most $m$, the raw rate $\fprate = 1 - (1 -
\fprate')^{1/m}$ keeps every negative orbit's closure rate at most $\fprate'$, with
equality at size $m$. As $\fprate' \to 0$ for fixed $m$, $\fprate = \fprate'/m +
O(\fprate'^2)$ and $\log_2(\fprate'/\fprate) = \log_2 m + O(\fprate')$.
\end{corollary}

An accounting model that charges $-\log_2 \fprate$ bits per stored key, other
costs fixed, therefore assigns an asymptotic surcharge of $\log_2 m$ bits per key.
This is conditional accounting, not a memory lower bound for two-sided channels:
the one-sided membership bound of \Cref{ch:information-bounds} has $\tprate = 1$
and cannot price a recall improvement.

Deletion enforces single-valuedness instead: keep at most one reported value per
key, breaking ties uniformly with randomness independent of the trials, sampled
once per row and reused. A defined key then retains its true value with
probability
\[
  \tprate\, \Expect{(1 + K)^{-1}}
  = \tprate\, \frac{1 - (1 - \fprate)^{v}}{v \fprate},
  \qquad K \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(v - 1, \fprate),\ \fprate > 0,
\]
with continuous extension $\tprate$ at $\fprate = 0$, using $\Expect{1/(1 + K)} =
\int_0^1 (1 - \fprate + \fprate x)^{v - 1}\, dx$. The retained rate decays as $v$
grows; deletion never raises a false-positive probability but can discard the
true value. A map representation enforces single-valuedness by its output type
instead (\Cref{ch:approximate-functions}).

Transitivity is outside the orbit construction altogether.

\begin{lemma}[No equivalence induces transitivity]
\label{lem:rel-no-quotient}
For $|A| \ge 2$, no fixed equivalence on $A \times A$ has the transitive relations
as exactly its unions of classes.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Unions of classes are closed under union, but $\{(a, b)\}$ and $\{(b, a)\}$ are
transitive while their union, lacking $(a, a)$, is not.
\end{proof}

The lemma excludes this particular canonical-source construction; it excludes
neither other encodings, nor restricted subclasses, nor repairs that add or delete
tuples. One such addition rule is easy to analyze exactly.

\begin{theorem}[Simultaneous transitive completion of a path]
\label{thm:rel-path-cascade}
For $R_0 = \{(1, 2), \ldots, (k, k + 1)\}$ with $k \ge 1$, the iteration $R_{j+1}
= R_j \cup (R_j \circ R_j)$ first reaches the transitive closure at index $\lceil
\log_2 k \rceil$, and the closure has $\binom{k+1}{2}$ pairs, adding
$\binom{k+1}{2} - k$ to the path.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By induction $R_j$ holds exactly the forward pairs at distance at most $\min\{2^j,
k\}$, since composing doubles the maximal distance and every shorter distance
splits into two available ones; the forward pairs on $k + 1$ elements form the
full strict order.
\end{proof}

The iteration thus has no universe-independent bound on its rounds and can add
$\Theta(k^2)$ pairs, a statement about this simultaneous addition rule only. Other
closure algorithms and repair objectives behave differently; deleting every pair,
for instance, yields a transitive relation at the cost of every true answer. The
dependence of repair problems on the constraint class is studied at length in
database repair and consistent query answering\index{consistent query answering} \parencite{bertossi-2011}.

\subsection*{What the constructions change}

The constructions expose how a representation choice moves error between three
places: the marginal rates, the stored-key count, and the dependence structure. A
finer marginal-rate partition does not restore tuple independence: symmetry
enforced by a canonical source has few rate blocks but perfect dependence within
each orbit. Model order\index{model order!versus parameter count} counts rate blocks; it says nothing about the joint law,
and the query-level consequences of shared sources are the subject of the next
section.
