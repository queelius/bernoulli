\section*{Bibliographic Notes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Bibliographic Notes}
\label{sec:bib-notes-ch12}

\paragraph{The approximate-relation framework.}
The relation-level lift of the chapter~5 framework is drawn from the
\textcite{towell-bernoulli-relations} manuscript, which is the
primary source for the construction of an approximate relation as a
$\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ on the pair universe $T \times U$ and for the
join, projection, and selection rate-propagation rules of
\Cref{sec:relation-composition}. The chapter compresses that
manuscript into three sections by reading its rate calculations as
the chapter~5 and chapter~6 frameworks specialized to pairs rather
than as standalone derivations; the lift makes
\Cref{thm:join-rates} a corollary of the chapter~6 intersection
theorem with cross-relation independence playing the role of the
chapter~6 cross-structure independence. A reader who wants the
relational object treated as primary, with the connection to the
set-level framework as derived rather than assumed, should treat the
manuscript as the next reading.

\paragraph{The classical relational model.}
The classical model of a relation as a subset of a Cartesian product,
and the algebraic operators (join, projection, selection, union,
difference) that the chapter approximates, originate with
\textcite{codd-1970}'s ``A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared
Data Banks.'' Codd's paper is the foundation document of the
relational-database tradition that gave us SQL, normal forms, and the
algebraic-query view of databases; the present chapter borrows the
relational operators wholesale and the only additions are the
approximate-relation lift and the rate-propagation analysis under
that lift. The textbook treatment of Codd's algebra appears in any
standard database systems text; the historical narrative and the
original axioms are still best read in the 1970 paper itself.

\paragraph{Approximate-join algorithms in databases.}
The algorithmic instance of \Cref{sec:approximate-joins} is the
MinHash similarity join, whose two ingredients are
\textcite{broder-1997}'s MinHash sketch (the per-pair Jaccard
estimator) and \textcite{indyk-motwani-1998}'s locality-sensitive
hashing (the sub-quadratic candidate-pruning layer). The combined
pipeline as a database operator was systematized by
\textcite{sarawagi-kirpal-2004} for set-similarity joins, and many
later variants (prefix filtering, positional filtering, length
filtering, signature-based pruning) are surveyed in the wider
database literature. The chapter does not enumerate those variants;
the point is that each one is an alternative implementation of the
same approximate relation $\tilde S_\theta \subseteq R_1 \times R_2$,
with FPR and FNR computed by the same chapter~12 join machinery.
\Cref{thm:join-rates} commits to no particular sketch, and the
chapter~10 MinHash error bound is the one used in
\Cref{sec:approximate-joins} only because it is the sketch the book
already developed in chapter~10; an equijoin variant using Bloom
filters (exercise~10) is another instance of the same framework with
different per-pair rates.
