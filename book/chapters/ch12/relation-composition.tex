\section{Composition of Approximate Relations}
\label{sec:relation-composition}

\Cref{sec:sets-to-relations} treated an approximate relation as an
approximate set on the Cartesian product universe and stopped there.
The universe carries structure beyond ``set of pairs,'' though: a
binary relation $R \subseteq T \times U$ admits projection onto its
attributes, selection by a predicate, and join with a second relation
that shares an attribute. Each of these operations is a building
block of classical relational algebra. Each has an approximate
analogue, and the propagation of $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ through each
analogue follows from chapter 3's AND-under-noise calculation
(\Cref{sec:and-under-noise}) and chapter 6's intersection theorem
(\Cref{prop:intersection-fpr-fnr}), with pairs substituted for
elements.

\subsection*{Natural join}

The natural join of two relations
$R_1 \subseteq T \times U$ and $R_2 \subseteq U \times V$ on the
shared attribute $U$ is the set of triples
\[
  R_1 \bowtie R_2 \;=\; \{ (t, u, v) \in T \times U \times V :
    (t, u) \in R_1 \text{ and } (u, v) \in R_2 \}.
\]
A triple is in the join when both component pairs are in their
respective relations. The membership predicate of $R_1 \bowtie R_2$ is
the conjunction of the two component predicates, evaluated at the
matching attribute value:
\[
  \SetIndicator{R_1 \bowtie R_2}(t, u, v)
  \;=\;
  \SetIndicator{R_1}(t, u) \;\land\; \SetIndicator{R_2}(u, v).
\]
The classical case is exact; the approximate case substitutes
$\tilde R_1$ and $\tilde R_2$ for the two predicates and reads the
output as the conjunction of two noisy Booleans:
\[
  (\tilde R_1 \bowtie \tilde R_2)(t, u, v)
  \;=\;
  \tilde R_1(t, u) \;\land\; \tilde R_2(u, v).
\]
The right-hand side is the AND of two $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$
instances, which is exactly the object whose rates chapter 3 derived
(\Cref{sec:and-under-noise}). The lift to relations changes nothing
about the algebra: each $\tilde R_i(t, u)$ is a Bernoulli Boolean and
AND-under-noise applies pointwise to the joined triple. The only
ingredient the lift adds is the cross-relation independence
assumption: the per-query randomness of $\tilde R_1$ at $(t, u)$ is
independent of the per-query randomness of $\tilde R_2$ at $(u, v)$.
That assumption is essentially Axiom~1 of chapter 4 applied across the
two structures rather than within one of them, and it holds whenever
the two relations are built and queried independently, which is the
typical implementation case.

\subsection*{FPR and FNR under join}

The headline result is what chapter 6 already proved for intersection
of approximate sets, restated for relations. It is what chapter 6's
intersection theorem looks like with pairs instead of elements.

\begin{theorem}[Join error rates]
\label{thm:join-rates}
Let $\tilde R_1 \subseteq T \times U$ and
$\tilde R_2 \subseteq U \times V$ be two approximate relations with
false-negative rates $\fnrate_1 = \fnrate_2 = 0$ and false-positive
rates $\fprate_1, \fprate_2$, and assume cross-relation independence
of the per-query randomness. Then the natural join
$\tilde R_1 \bowtie \tilde R_2$ has false-negative rate
\[
  \fnrate_{\bowtie} \;=\; 0
\]
and false-positive rate
\[
  \fprate_{\bowtie} \;=\; \fprate_1 \fprate_2.
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof is the AND-of-Booleans calculation lifted to pairs. A true
join triple $(t, u, v) \in R_1 \bowtie R_2$ requires $(t, u) \in R_1$
and $(u, v) \in R_2$; both component predicates have structural
false-negative rate zero, so both report $\top$ with probability one,
and their conjunction reports $\top$ with probability one. The join
inherits $\fnrate_{\bowtie} = 0$. A spurious join triple is one for
which at least one component pair is outside its relation. For the
join predicate to wrongly report $\top$ on such a triple, both
component predicates must wrongly report $\top$. If exactly one of the
two pairs is outside its relation, the join's false-positive
probability is $\fprate_1 \fprate_2 \cdot 0 + \fprate_i \cdot 1$ for
the offending index; if both are outside, it is $\fprate_1 \fprate_2$.
The conditional rate ``given the triple is not in the join'' is a
mixture of these cases, but each component pair's truth status is
fixed by the triple, so the conditional becomes the product
$\fprate_1 \fprate_2$ exactly. This is \Cref{prop:intersection-fpr-fnr}
of chapter 6 applied to the pair universe $T \times U \times V$, with
the in-join block playing the role of $A \cap B$ and the out-of-join
block playing the role of $(A \cap B)^c$.

Two things are worth noting. First, the product structure is the
chapter 3 AND result. False-positive rates multiply under conjunction
when both inputs have structural false-negative rate zero; the lift to
pairs does not alter the formula. Second, the assumption of
$\fnrate_1 = \fnrate_2 = 0$ is the standard Bloom-style operating
regime. If either relation has positive $\fnrate_i$, the join inherits
a positive $\fnrate_{\bowtie}$ as well, computed by the general
intersection formula of \Cref{prop:intersection-fpr-fnr}; the
clean product formula is the one-sided special case.

\subsection*{Projection}

The projection of a relation $R \subseteq T \times U$ onto its first
attribute is the set
\[
  \pi_T(R) \;=\; \{ t \in T : \exists u \in U, (t, u) \in R \},
\]
a subset of $T$. Projection collapses the $U$ axis by an existential
quantifier. Under an approximate relation, the projection predicate
becomes
\[
  t \in \pi_T(\tilde R) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \exists u \in U \;\text{s.t.}\;
  \tilde R(t, u) = \top.
\]
For each candidate $t \in T$, the projection runs $|U|$ membership
tests across the second attribute and reports $\top$ if any of them
returns $\top$. The rate analysis is the OR-of-Booleans calculation
on $|U|$ independent inputs (\Cref{sec:and-under-noise} derived the
OR rule as the De Morgan dual of AND). If the input relation has
$\fnrate = 0$, the projection inherits $\fnrate = 0$: a true $t$ has
at least one $u$ with $(t, u) \in R$, and that pair reports $\top$
with probability one, so the OR reports $\top$ with probability one.
The false-positive side amplifies. For a non-projecting $t$ (no $u$
with $(t, u) \in R$), every pair $(t, u)$ is outside the relation and
each test reports $\top$ independently with probability $\fprate$; the
probability that at least one reports $\top$ is $1 - (1 - \fprate)^{|U|}$,
which approaches one as $|U|$ grows.

Projection thus inflates the false-positive rate by the cardinality of
the projected-out attribute. This is a structural feature of the
operation, not an artifact: existential quantification over many noisy
tests amplifies any false-positive bias. In practice, projection is
applied to relations whose underlying $R$ has many in-relation pairs
per $t$, so the test never has to traverse all of $U$; in the limiting
case where the projection is over a sparse relation with a large
co-domain, the inflation can be severe and motivates the
approximate-projection variants discussed in
\Cref{sec:approximate-joins}.

\subsection*{Selection}

The selection of $R$ by a predicate
$P : T \times U \to \{\top, \bot\}$ is the subset
\[
  \sigma_P(R) \;=\; \{ (t, u) \in R : P(t, u) = \top \},
\]
those pairs of $R$ on which $P$ holds. Two cases arise in the
approximate setting. If $P$ is exact, the selection predicate is
$\tilde R(t, u) \land P(t, u)$ with the second factor deterministic;
the conditional rates of $\sigma_P(\tilde R)$ relative to
$\sigma_P(R)$ are the same as those of $\tilde R$ relative to $R$
restricted to the support $\{(t, u) : P(t, u) = \top\}$. The rates
$\fprate, \fnrate$ pass through unchanged. If $P$ is itself
approximate, with rates $(\fprate_P, \fnrate_P)$, the selection
predicate is the AND of two noisy Booleans and the rates compose by
the chapter 3 product rule under the same cross-channel independence
assumption: the selected $\tilde R$ has structural
$\fnrate_{\sigma} = 0$ (if both inputs had $\fnrate = 0$) and
$\fprate_{\sigma} = \fprate \fprate_P$.

\subsection*{Worked example: friend-and-coworker}

Let $\tilde F \subseteq P \times P$ be a Bloom-style approximate
friend-of relation with $\fnrate_F = 0$ and $\fprate_F = 0.05$, and
let $\tilde C \subseteq P \times P$ be a Bloom-style is-a-coworker
relation with $\fnrate_C = 0$ and $\fprate_C = 0.02$. Both structures
were built independently and are queried independently, so the
cross-relation independence assumption holds. A natural-join query
$\tilde F \bowtie \tilde C$ on the common attribute $P$ returns
triples $(p_1, p_2, p_3)$ for which $\tilde F$ reports $p_1$ as a
friend of $p_2$ and $\tilde C$ reports $p_2$ as a coworker of $p_3$.
By \Cref{thm:join-rates}, the join has $\fnrate_{\bowtie} = 0$ and
\[
  \fprate_{\bowtie} \;=\; \fprate_F \fprate_C
  \;=\; 0.05 \times 0.02 \;=\; 0.001.
\]
Three orders of magnitude of false-positive rate are bought for the
price of one extra query. The exact same structure underlies the
Bloom-filter intersection of chapter 6: composing two structures with
one-sided error rates is one of the cheapest gains in the framework.
The cost is in the assumptions, not the algebra. Cross-relation
independence has to hold; if the two filters share hash functions or
were built from correlated data, the product formula breaks and the
true $\fprate_{\bowtie}$ moves toward
$\max(\fprate_F, \fprate_C)$. In practice the assumption is enforced
by using independent hash seeds across the two filters.

What the join example does \emph{not} show is the projection-side
inflation. If we project the friend-and-coworker join onto the first
attribute (``persons with at least one friend-coworker chain''), the
existential quantifier over $|P|^2$ candidate chains amplifies
$\fprate_{\bowtie} = 0.001$ by a factor that depends on how many true
chains each $p_1$ has. For populations where most persons have at
least one true friend-coworker chain, the projection inherits the
join's modest rate; for sparse populations it does not.
\Cref{sec:approximate-joins} treats the database setting where the
join, projection, and selection are composed at scale.

\subsection*{Composing the operators}

The three operators (join, projection, selection) compose in any
order, and the rate propagation through a composed expression follows
by induction on the expression tree. A query like
$\pi_T(\sigma_P(\tilde R_1 \bowtie \tilde R_2))$ is evaluated bottom
up: first the join gives an approximate relation with rates
$(\fprate_1 \fprate_2, 0)$ (assuming both inputs have
$\fnrate_i = 0$); then the exact selection passes those rates
through unchanged; then the projection inflates the false-positive
side by the existential-quantification factor derived above and
preserves the structural zero on the false-negative side. The
intermediate rates do not have to be tracked through symbolic
arithmetic; each operator has a closed-form rate-update rule, and
chaining the rules gives the rate-update rule for the whole
expression. This is the same closure-under-composition story chapter 6
told for unions, intersections, and complements of approximate sets:
the rate algebra closes, and rates propagate through expression trees
by the same induction that the rates of an approximate set propagate
through Boolean expressions in chapter 5.

What the chapter 6 story did not have is the projection inflation. A
union of approximate sets is a single Boolean OR per query and has
no existential quantifier over a domain of unknown size. A projection
is an existential quantifier over the full second-attribute domain
and inflates the false-positive rate by a factor that grows with
$|U|$. This is the one structural penalty the lift to relations
introduces, and it motivates the approximate-projection techniques of
\Cref{sec:approximate-joins}: instead of querying every $u \in U$ for
each candidate $t$, we use a sketch (typically a MinHash signature)
that estimates the existential quantifier directly, without paying the
$|U|$ amplification factor in full.
