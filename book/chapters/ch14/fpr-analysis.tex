\section{False-Positive Rate Analysis}
\label{sec:fpr-analysis}

Both constructions have $\fnrate = 0$ by construction, so their accuracy
is their false-positive rate. For the BHF the rate is exact and the
independence across non-members is inherited from the hash; the
subtleties are what the construction may look at, and what happens when
the acceptance set is chosen from the data. For the perfect hash filter
the argument is the classical fingerprint-collision bound.

\subsection*{The BHF}

\begin{proposition}[BHF false-positive rate]\index{false-positive rate}
\label{prop:bhf-fpr}
Under the random-oracle model, let the BHF of \Cref{def:bhf} be built for
$A$ with fixed parameters $(N, t)$, and let $x \notin A$ be a query point
not examined during the build. Then
\[
  \Prob{\textsc{Query}(x) = \top} \;=\; \fprate \;=\; \frac{t + 1}{N},
\]
and the reports for distinct non-members $x_1, \ldots, x_k$ are
independent conditional on the returned salt.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The build's stopping rule depends only on the values $h(a, b)$ with $a
\in A$. For $x \notin A$ the values $h(x, b)$, over all salts $b$, are
independent of those, so conditional on $b^{*} = b$ the value $h(x, b)$ is
still uniform on $\{0, \ldots, N - 1\}$ and lies in $\{0, \ldots, t\}$
with probability $(t + 1)/N$. Distinct non-members contribute values at
distinct pairs $(x_i, b)$, which are independent given $b$.
\end{proof}

The proposition places the BHF inside the model of
\Cref{ch:axioms-kronecker}: the non-member reports are independent
Bernoulli$(\fprate)$ outcomes, as \Cref{ax:element-indep}\index{independence!across elements}
requires, and the rate is a fixed parameter rather than a property of
the particular salt. Two hypotheses carry the weight. The query must be
fresh, not one of the points the build inspected; a construction that
rejected salts according to how they treated some designated
non-members would condition those non-members' outcomes. And the
acceptance set must be fixed before the salts are examined. The second
hypothesis is the one the source manuscript's adaptive variant gives up.

\begin{remark}[Adaptive thresholds trade recall, not space]
\label{rem:adaptive-threshold}
An adaptive threshold\index{threshold!adaptive} takes any salt, computes
the member residues $h(a, b)$, and sets $t$ to their $p$-th smallest
value $T$, so that at least $p$ members are accepted and every salt
``succeeds.'' Nothing is gained at an equal guarantee. In the continuous
limit $T/N$ follows a Beta$(p, n - p + 1)$ law, so the mean recall is
$p/n$ and the mean false-positive rate is $p/(n + 1)$; their difference,
$p / (n(n + 1)) \le 1/(n+1)$, shows an almost non-discriminating rule, and
asking for zero false negatives ($p = n$) gives mean false-positive rate
$n/(n+1)$. If instead an FPR cap $\fprate = (t^{*} + 1)/N$ and a minimum
recall $p$ are both imposed, the event $T \le t^{*}$ is the same event as
``at least $p$ members fall at or below $t^{*}$,'' so the adaptive
construction succeeds with exactly the fixed-threshold probability and
the cost is ordinary seed search \parencite{lehmann-et-al-2025}. The
random threshold also breaks independence: distinct non-members are
independent given $T$ but have unconditional covariance $\Var{(T +
1)/N} > 0$, which is $3/64$ at $N = 2$, $n = 2$, $p = 1$. Finally, the
exact discrete law of $T$ for independent uniform residues is the
binomial tail
\[
  \Prob{T \le j} \;=\; \sum_{a = p}^{n} \binom{n}{a}
  \Bigl( \tfrac{j + 1}{N} \Bigr)^{a}
  \Bigl( 1 - \tfrac{j + 1}{N} \Bigr)^{n - a},
\]
not an order-statistic law for distinct residues sampled without
replacement\index{order statistic}. The two differ already at $N = n =
2$, $p = 1$: the minimum of two independent uniform bits is $0$ with
probability $3/4$, whereas the without-replacement formula, which an
earlier version of the source manuscript printed as exact, gives
$1$\index{counterexample!discrete order statistic}. The continuous Beta
limit is unaffected.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{The perfect hash filter}

For the perfect hash filter of \Cref{def:phf}, let $x \notin A$\index{fingerprint} and let
the probability be over the fingerprint hash $g$, with $\phi$ fixed by
the build. Two cases arise.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{(a) $\phi(x) = \phi(a^{*})$ for some $a^{*} \in A$.}
This is the only case for a minimal perfect hash, whose range is exactly
$[n]$. The query returns $\top$ iff $g(x) = g(a^{*})$, and since $x \neq
a^{*}$, uniformity of $g$ on pairs gives probability $2^{-r}$.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{(b) $\phi(x)$ falls outside $\phi(A)$.} This can happen
only for a non-minimal perfect hash with spare slots. A slot holding a
sentinel that matches no fingerprint contributes nothing; a slot holding
an arbitrary value independent of $g(x)$ contributes at most $2^{-r}$.

\begin{proposition}[Perfect hash filter false-positive rate]
\label{prop:phf-fpr}
If $g$ is drawn from a family with $\Prob{g(x) = g(y)} \le 2^{-r}$ for
every pair $x \neq y$, then for every $x \notin A$ the perfect hash
filter of \Cref{def:phf} satisfies
\[
  \Prob{\textsc{Query}(x) = \top} \;\le\; 2^{-r}
  \;=\; 2^{-\lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil} \;\le\; \fprate,
\]
with equality to $2^{-r}$ for a minimal perfect hash and a fingerprint
family that is uniform on pairs.
\end{proposition}

The ceiling in $r$ over-achieves the target by up to a factor of two: at
$\fprate = 0.01$, $r = 7$ and the realized rate is $2^{-7} \approx
0.0078$, about $22\%$ below target, the same rounding that costs $0.36$
bits per element on the space side. The BHF has no such rounding, because
its acceptance set has density exactly $(t + 1)/N$.

\subsection*{The uniformity hypothesis}

Both propositions rest on hash uniformity, and both fail against an
adversary who knows the hash: a query chosen so that $h(x, b^{*}) \le t$,
or so that $g(x) = g(a^{*})$, is a false positive with probability one.
For the perfect hash filter the requirement is only pairwise collision
probability, which the universal hashing of
\textcite{carter-wegman-1979} supplies: drawing $g$ from a universal
family at build time bounds every pairwise collision probability by the
reciprocal of the codomain size. The BHF's analysis uses the
random-oracle model\index{random oracle}, a stronger idealization, because the build examines
exponentially many salts and the argument needs the hash values at all of
them to behave independently; a real hash reduced modulo a modulus that
is not a power of two is also only approximately uniform without
rejection sampling. Salting with a build-time secret denies an adversary
advance knowledge of the hash. \Cref{ch:not-bernoulli} returns to
adaptive and adversarial queries, where these averages stop describing
the system.
