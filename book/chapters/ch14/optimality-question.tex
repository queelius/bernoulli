\section{The Optimality Question}
\label{sec:optimality-question}

Chapter~8 closed Part~II with a contrast: a lower bound that no
FNR-zero approximate set can undercut, and an upper bound that the
standard Bloom filter pays $44\%$ above the lower bound at every
target error rate. The contrast was diagnostic, not constructive.
\Cref{thm:entropy-floor} certified that some representation could in
principle reach $-\log_2 \fprate$ bits per element, but it did not
exhibit one. Chapter~5's Bloom filter sat above the floor by a fixed
multiplicative factor and was the only concrete FNR-zero scheme on the
table. The reader who finished chapter~8 was left with the question
the present chapter answers: is the floor attainable by a real
construction, or is the gap an unavoidable price for working in finite
bit arrays?

\subsection*{The gap, recalled}

\Cref{thm:entropy-floor} of \Cref{sec:entropy-floor} states that any
FNR-zero approximate-set representation with false-positive rate at
most $\fprate$ requires at least
\[
  L / n \;\ge\; -\log_2 \fprate \;+\; o(1)
\]
bits per element in the asymptotic regime $N \gg n$ and $\fprate$
fixed. The bound is a counting argument: $2^L$ possible bit strings
must collectively cover $\binom{N}{n}$ possible target sets, and each
representation can describe at most $\binom{|A_R|}{n}$ true sets
inside its accepted set $A_R$. The pigeonhole step forces $L$ to
grow linearly in $n \log_2(1/\fprate)$.

\Cref{prop:bloom-overhead} of \Cref{sec:bloom-gap} computes the
standard Bloom filter's bit budget from \Cref{def:bloom-filter}'s
construction: at the optimal hash count $k^* = (m/n)\ln 2$, the bit
budget per element is
\[
  m / n \;=\; \log_2(1/\fprate) \cdot \log_2 e,
\]
or $\log_2 e \approx 1.4427$ times the floor. The overhead is a
single constant independent of $\fprate$. It comes from a structural
choice in the Bloom design: $k$ independent hashes write into a flat
bit array, and the analysis maximizes per-bit entropy by tuning the
array to half occupancy. Half-occupancy is information-theoretically
the wrong target. The right target is to spend bits on the
discrimination the structure is supposed to perform, not on the
encoding's internal balance.

\subsection*{The open question}

The gap between the floor and the Bloom budget is structural, but
the structure is one specific design among many. \Cref{sec:bloom-gap}
ended by isolating the three Bloom design choices, $k$ independent
hashes, a flat shared bit array, and a conjunction query, and by
remarking that abandoning at least one of the three is necessary to
close the gap. The remark left the constructive question open: which
choice should one abandon, and what replacement makes the construction
work?

A constructive answer needs four properties. First, FNR-zero by
construction, so the new scheme stays on the same axis of the perfect
filter line as the Bloom filter. Second, an FPR provably bounded by
the target $\fprate$. Third, a per-element bit budget matching the
$-\log_2 \fprate$ floor up to a sublinear correction. Fourth, a query
algorithm that runs in time comparable to Bloom's, ideally constant
per query. The first two are correctness; the third is the optimality
the chapter promises; the fourth is what makes the construction
deployable rather than merely an existence proof. The question is
not whether some encoding meets all four (the floor theorem implies
that one does asymptotically), but whether an explicit construction
does, and what it looks like.

\subsection*{The answer, in sketch}

The Bernoulli Hash Function (BHF) closes the gap by abandoning the
Bloom filter's flat shared bit array. In its place the BHF uses two
ingredients. The first is a \emph{perfect hash function} $\phi : A
\to [n]$ that bijects the designated set $A$ onto the index range
$[n] = \{1, \ldots, n\}$. The second is a \emph{fingerprint table}
$F : [n] \to \{0, 1\}^r$ that stores a short fingerprint
$F[\phi(a)] = g(a)$ at the slot reserved for each $a \in A$, where
$g : \Universe \to \{0, 1\}^r$ is a secondary hash function and
$r = \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil$ is the fingerprint width.

A membership query for $x$ proceeds in two steps. Compute $\phi(x) \in
[n]$, the slot the perfect hash assigns to $x$. Read the fingerprint
$F[\phi(x)]$ stored there, and compare it to the fingerprint $g(x)$
the query computes from $x$. If the two fingerprints match, return
$\top$; otherwise return $\bot$. The intuition for why this works
runs as follows. For $x \in A$, the perfect hash sends $x$ to the
slot reserved for $x$ during construction, and the stored fingerprint
is $g(x)$ by definition; the comparison succeeds and the query returns
$\top$. No false negatives are possible. For $x \notin A$, the
perfect hash sends $x$ to some slot, and the stored fingerprint at
that slot is $g(a)$ for whatever $a \in A$ the perfect hash assigned
there. The probability that $g(a) = g(x)$ is $2^{-r}$, since $g$ is
a uniform hash onto $\{0, 1\}^r$. With $r = \lceil -\log_2 \fprate
\rceil$, the false-positive probability is at most $\fprate$ by
construction.

The space cost is the sum of two terms: $n \cdot r$ bits for the
fingerprint table and $O(n \log \log \log n / \log n)$ bits for a
modern perfect-hash representation. The fingerprint term dominates;
the perfect-hash overhead is sublinear in $n r$. The total bit
budget per element is
\[
  r \;+\; O(\log \log \log n / \log n)
  \;\longrightarrow\; \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil
  \;=\; -\log_2 \fprate \;+\; O(1),
\]
which matches the floor up to the integer rounding of $r$. The gap
the standard Bloom filter pays is gone. The construction trades
Bloom's flat shared bit array for a perfect-hash-addressed
fingerprint table, and the per-element cost drops from $\log_2 e
\cdot \log_2(1/\fprate)$ to $\log_2(1/\fprate)$ plus integer rounding.
That is the answer in sketch; the rest of the chapter fills in the
details.

\subsection*{Chapter roadmap}

\Cref{sec:bhf-construction} gives the construction in full: the
perfect hash, the fingerprint table, the insert and query algorithms,
and the worked example at $n = 1000, \fprate = 0.01$. The
construction is short. \Cref{sec:space-analysis} computes the bit
budget: $n r$ for the fingerprints, sublinear correction for the
perfect hash, and a comparison table against the floor and the Bloom
filter at $n = 10^6$ and three target $\fprate$ values. The headline
theorem of the chapter, \Cref{thm:bhf-optimality}, sits there.
\Cref{sec:fpr-analysis} proves the FPR bound: the fingerprint
comparison succeeds with probability $2^{-r}$ for any $x \notin A$,
provided $g$ is uniform on $\{0, 1\}^r$. The argument is short and
the hash-function assumption is the only nontrivial input.
\Cref{sec:construction-time} addresses the operational side: the
perfect hash takes $O(n)$ expected time to build by modern
algorithms; the fingerprint table takes one pass over $A$; the query
is $O(1)$ in the bit count. The trade-off table contrasts BHF against
Bloom on three axes: space, construction time, and insertion
support. The BHF wins on space, loses on dynamic insertions,
matches on query time. \Cref{sec:ch14-bridge} hands off to
chapter~15, which constructs the FPR-zero counterpart on the other
half of the perfect filter line.
