\section{Bridge}
\label{sec:ch14-bridge}

The chapter answered the optimality question of chapter~8 twice. The
Bernoulli hash function (\Cref{def:bhf}) attains the entropy floor of
\Cref{thm:entropy-floor} to leading order, with no integer rounding
(\Cref{thm:bhf-optimality}) and an exact false-positive rate
(\Cref{prop:bhf-fpr}), and pays for it with a build exponential in the
set size. The perfect hash filter (\Cref{def:phf}) builds in expected
linear time and pays an additive addressing cost of at least $\log_2 e$
bits per element (\Cref{prop:phf-space}), which turns the Bloom filter's
multiplicative overhead (\Cref{prop:bloom-overhead}) into an additive
one and wins on space at tight targets; retrieval-based filters close
most of the remaining gap. None of these constructions originates here,
and \Cref{sec:bib-notes-ch14} names their sources.

All of them sit on the FNR-zero arm of the perfect-filter line\index{perfect filter line}
(\Cref{def:perfect-filter-line}): they never miss a member and
occasionally accept a stranger. The other arm, $\fprate = 0$ with a
controlled $\fnrate$, is the subject of chapter~15, and the two arms are
not symmetric in cost. A false positive is a coincidence,\index{false-positive rate} a non-member's
hash falling into the acceptance set or its fingerprint matching the one
stored in its slot, and about $\log_2(1/\fprate)$ bits per element make
the coincidence as rare as desired. A guarantee of no false positives is
not a coincidence made rare but a certificate enforced against every
non-member at once, and certifying a designated subset exactly is the
expensive direction: over the whole universe it costs on the order of
$\log_2(|\Universe|/n)$ bits per element, the price of a dictionary.
Applications split along the same line. FNR-zero structures serve where
a miss is unacceptable, a cache that must not lose a stored key or a
routing layer that must not drop a known address; FPR-zero structures
serve where a spurious acceptance is unacceptable, a precision-oriented
index or an authorization filter.

Chapter~15 reaches the FPR-zero arm by reusing the perfect hash filter's
skeleton, a minimal perfect hash addressing a per-slot table built in
one pass, with a rank in each slot instead of a fingerprint. It is not a
mechanical swap of error modes. Queried over the whole universe, the rank
table behaves exactly like a fingerprint table, with no false negatives
and false positives at $2^{-r}$; the false-positive rate becomes zero
only in a restricted query model in which non-members cannot present a
valid rank (ranked search is the standard instance), and a controlled
false-negative rate is then bought by relaxing the perfect hash to a
$k$-perfect one. \Cref{ch:perfect-hash-filters} develops the
construction and its application.
