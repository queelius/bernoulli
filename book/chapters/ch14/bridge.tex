\section{Bridge}
\label{sec:ch14-bridge}

The chapter has assembled the constructive answer to the entropy
floor of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor}. The Bernoulli Hash Function of
\Cref{def:bhf} reaches the floor up to integer rounding plus a
sublinear correction (\Cref{thm:bhf-optimality}), meets the
designer's FPR target under a standard uniformity assumption on the
fingerprint hash (\Cref{prop:bhf-fpr}), and pays for the
improvement in static-set restrictions and an $O(n)$ build cost
(\Cref{sec:construction-time}). The chapter's headline result is the
$1.44\times$ factor that the standard Bloom filter spent over the
floor (\Cref{prop:bloom-overhead}) collapsing to a constant-bit
additive overhead. Two threads remain to tie before chapter~15
opens: the antecedents the construction draws on, and the dual
construction that sits opposite the BHF on the perfect-filter line
of chapter~6.

\subsection*{What comes next: the bibliographic landscape}

The construction did not arrive from a vacuum. The perfect-hash
literature is several decades old and supports a family of
constructions whose space and time bounds the chapter has cited.
The fingerprint table draws on the same universal-hashing
machinery that the Bloom filter uses, with the structural
difference being where the hash is applied. The proposition
\Cref{prop:bhf-fpr} is recent in the form the chapter states it,
but its components (perfect-hash addressing, fingerprint comparison,
universal-hashing assumption on the fingerprint hash) each have
their own histories. \Cref{sec:bib-notes-ch14} names the primary
sources: the Towell manuscript that develops the BHF construction
in its full form; the Carter-Wegman universal-hashing paper that
underwrites the fingerprint analysis; the Belazzougui-Botelho-
Dietzfelbinger construction that achieves the asymptotic
perfect-hash bound the chapter quoted; and the cuckoo-filter and
quotient-filter constructions that occupy related design spaces.
The bibliographic notes section is the place to consult for the
chapter's external dependencies; the chapter's prose deliberately
keeps the citations sparse so the argument can move at its own
pace.

\subsection*{What comes next: the other side of the perfect-filter line}

The perfect-filter line of \Cref{def:perfect-filter-line} runs
between two extremes: the FNR-zero membership data structures (the
Bloom filter and its descendants, which the present chapter has
extended to the BHF as the space-optimal representative) and the
FPR-zero ranked-search data structures (the perfect hash filters
and their descendants, which chapter~15 introduces). The two
extremes share a structural skeleton (both use a perfect hash and
a fingerprint table) but trade their error modes: the BHF accepts a
small false-positive rate to guarantee no false negatives; the
BPHF (Bernoulli Perfect Hash Filter), the chapter~15 counterpart,
accepts a small false-negative rate to guarantee no false positives.
The dual is exact: the BPHF for a designated set is the BHF for
the complement, modulo the practical detail that the complement is
usually infinite. The two halves of the perfect-filter line are
therefore not independent constructions but two instantiations of
the same architectural template, separated by the choice of which
error mode to zero out.

The applications differ accordingly. The BHF is the right choice
for membership filters where false negatives are unacceptable (a
cache that must not miss a stored element; a routing layer that
must not drop a known address; a security check that must not
admit a denied entity). The BPHF is the right choice for ranked
search and approximate set representation where false positives
are unacceptable (a precision-oriented search index that must not
return non-matching results; an authorization filter that must not
grant access to unauthorized identifiers; a deduplication layer
that must not declare two distinct items the same). The
applications correspond to the two halves of the perfect-filter
line of \Cref{sec:perfect-filter-line}, and the chapter has covered
the FNR-zero half; chapter~15 will cover the FPR-zero half.

The construction of the BPHF will mirror the BHF closely. The
ingredients are still a perfect hash and a fingerprint table; the
build pass and query algorithm are nearly identical; the space
bound is the same $\lceil -\log_2 \fnrate \rceil$ bits per element
(with $\fnrate$ replacing $\fprate$ in the role of the target
error); the trade-offs against the analogous streaming structures
(the FPR-zero analogues of the Bloom filter, which include the
quotient filter and the rank-and-select-based structures) are
analogous. The chapter~15 narrative will not need to redevelop the
machinery; it will reuse the chapter~14 derivation with the
error-mode swap. The reader who has internalized the BHF
construction will find chapter~15 a short read; the new content is
the application setting, not the data structure.

\subsection*{What this chapter has achieved}

The chapter set out to answer the optimality question from
chapter~8: can the entropy floor be reached by a real construction,
or is the $\log_2 e$ Bloom-filter overhead an unavoidable structural
cost? The answer is yes, the floor is reachable, and the BHF
reaches it. The construction is short (two ingredients, two
algorithms), the analysis is short (one optimality theorem, one
FPR proposition), and the price is operationally manageable
(static set, $O(n)$ build, $O(1)$ queries). The chapter closes
Part~IV's opening question and opens its second question, the
dual application on the FPR-zero side of the perfect-filter line.
The bibliographic notes of \Cref{sec:bib-notes-ch14} attach the
chapter's argument to the published literature it draws on, and
chapter~15 picks up the thread on the other side of the line.
