\section{Construction Time and Trade-offs}
\label{sec:construction-time}

The two constructions of this chapter differ less in what they store than
in how they find it. The BHF searches; the perfect hash filter
addresses. This section prices the search, prices the addressing, and
offers a decision rule that includes the Bloom filter and the retrieval
filters of \Cref{sec:space-analysis}.

\subsection*{The BHF build is exponential}

The build of \Cref{alg:bhf-insert-query} examines an expected $1/s =
\fprate^{-n}$ salts (\eqref{eq:bhf-success}), each rejected after an
expected at most $1/(1 - \fprate)$ hash evaluations, so its expected work
is about $\fprate^{-n}/(1 - \fprate)$ hash evaluations. In bits, the
search costs $2^{\ell^{*}}$ trials for an $\ell^{*}$-bit representation:
the space optimality of \Cref{thm:bhf-optimality} and the exponential
build are the same fact read in two directions. At $\fprate = 1/2$ a set
of $64$ members needs about $1.8 \times 10^{19}$ salts; at $\fprate =
0.01$ a set of $10$ members already needs $10^{20}$. The BHF is a
construction for small sets.

That is how seed search is used in practice.\index{seed search} Split $A$ by a first-level
hash into buckets of expected size $\beta$ and search one salt per
bucket. The work becomes about $(n/\beta) \fprate^{-\beta}$, and the
space becomes the sum of the per-bucket salt lengths, $n
\log_2(1/\fprate)$ in expectation, plus the constant slack of
\Cref{thm:bhf-optimality} for each bucket and the cost of recording where
each variable-length salt begins. The per-element overhead therefore
falls like $1/\beta$ while the work grows like $\fprate^{-\beta}$, and
how cheaply the salts can be encoded jointly is a research question in
its own right. Minimal perfect hashing uses exactly this design,
searching small buckets for seeds that are injective rather than
accepting \parencite{esposito-graf-vigna-2020,lehmann-sanders-walzer-2024},
and \textcite{lehmann-et-al-2025} combine the search with the encoding of
the seeds to reduce that overhead. The book does not develop a bucketed
BHF; it uses the single-salt BHF as the construction that shows what the
floor costs.

\subsection*{The perfect hash filter build is linear}

The perfect hash filter replaces the search over all of $A$ with an
expected-linear minimal perfect hash construction followed by one pass
writing fingerprints. Linear-time perfect hashing goes back to\index{perfect hash function}\index{minimal perfect hash function}
\textcite{fredman-komlos-szemeredi-1984}, whose scheme stores $O(n)$
words; space-efficient minimal perfect hash functions with expected
linear construction include the hypergraph-peeling construction of
\textcite{botelho-pagh-ziviani-2007}, hash-displace-and-compress
\parencite{belazzougui-2009}, and BBHash \parencite{limasset-et-al-2017},
at roughly $2$ to $3$ bits per key; RecSplit and ShockHash approach
$\log_2 e$ by spending more build time on bucketed seed search. The
fingerprint pass evaluates $g$ once per member and writes distinct slots,
so it parallelizes trivially. Building costs a few hash evaluations per
member with a constant expected number of retries, against the Bloom
filter's $k$ hash evaluations and bit writes per member with none.

\subsection*{Query time}

The BHF's query is one hash evaluation and one comparison. The perfect
hash filter's query evaluates the perfect hash (a constant number of
hashes and memory probes, the constant depending on the construction),
one fingerprint hash, one table read, and one $r$-bit comparison. The
Bloom filter's query costs $k$ hash evaluations\index{Bloom filter} and $k$ reads, with $k$
at the optimum equal to
\[
  k^{*} \;=\; (m/n) \ln 2 \;=\; \log_2(1/\fprate),
\]
since $m/n = \log_2(1/\fprate) \cdot \log_2 e$ (\Cref{prop:bloom-overhead})
and $\log_2 e \cdot \ln 2 = 1$; at $\fprate = 0.01$, $k^{*} \approx 6.6$,
and at $\fprate = 10^{-6}$, $k^{*} \approx 19.9$. Both static structures
answer in a number of memory probes that does not grow with
$\log_2(1/\fprate)$, which is often a more consequential advantage than
the bits.

\subsection*{Trade-offs}

All three structures are FNR-zero. The BHF and the perfect hash filter
are static: inserting or deleting a member means rebuilding, while the
Bloom filter inserts in $O(k)$ and supports no deletion (clearing shared
bits would create false negatives). \Cref{tab:bhf-bloom-tradeoffs}
summarizes.

\begin{remark}[Static filters do not merge by bit-OR]
\label{rem:bhf-nonmergeable}
Two Bloom filters on $A_1$ and $A_2$ with the same parameters merge into a
filter on $A_1 \cup A_2$ by bitwise OR, because bit $i$ means the same
thing in both. Neither static construction has that property. Two perfect
hash filters address their tables through unrelated bijections, so slot
$i$ holds fingerprints of unrelated members, and two BHF salts are
unrelated strings; the union must be rebuilt from the members, at the
cost of a fresh build. The data-dependent representation that removes
the Bloom filter's overhead is exactly what forbids the cheap merge. The
union can still be \emph{queried} without rebuilding, as the OR of the
two verdicts, at the composed rate of \Cref{ch:set-composition}.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Decision rule}

Three questions settle the choice: is the set static, is space binding,
and how tight is the target?

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Static set, space binding.} Use a retrieval-based filter\index{retrieval}
(xor, binary fuse, ribbon) or a perfect hash filter. The perfect hash
filter beats the Bloom filter only when $(\log_2 e - 1) \log_2(1/\fprate)$
exceeds its addressing cost, about $\fprate < 2^{-7}$ with BBHash
addressing (\Cref{prop:phf-space}); the retrieval filters approach the
floor more closely at every target.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Dynamic set.} Use a Bloom filter, or a cuckoo or quotient
filter\index{cuckoo filter}\index{quotient filter} \parencite{fan-cuckoo-2014,bender-quotient-2012}, which store
fingerprints with an additive overhead of a few bits per element and
support insertion and deletion. Pagh, Pagh, and Rao give a dynamic
structure within a $1 + o(1)$ factor of the floor plus $O(n)$ bits
\parencite{pagh-pagh-rao-2005}.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Large static base, small dynamic delta.} Keep a static
filter over the base and a small Bloom filter over the delta, report
$\top$ if either does, and rebuild the static part when the delta grows
past a fixed fraction of the base.

The BHF itself is not on the list. It is the construction that attains
the floor exactly and the reference against which the others are
measured; at deployment scale it appears only as the per-bucket
primitive of seed-search constructions.
