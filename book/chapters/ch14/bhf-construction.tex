\section{The BHF Construction}
\label{sec:bhf-construction}

\Cref{sec:optimality-question} sketched the answer in two ingredients
and two algorithms. This section states the construction in full: a
definition, pseudocode, the structural argument for $\fnrate = 0$, a
preview of the false-positive analysis that \Cref{sec:fpr-analysis}
completes, and a worked example placing the BHF's bit budget beside the
Bloom filter's at the same target.

\subsection*{The two ingredients}

Fix a finite universe $\Universe$ and a designated set $A \subseteq
\Universe$ with $|A| = n$. The construction depends on two hash
functions of opposite character.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{(i) The perfect hash $\phi$.} A \emph{perfect hash}
\[
  \phi \;:\; A \;\longrightarrow\; [n], \qquad [n] = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\},
\]
is injective on $A$, hence a bijection between $A$ and the addressing
range. It is not a hash function in the usual sense of mapping a large
universe uniformly into a small range; it is data-dependent,
constructed at build time from $A$ so that no two elements of $A$
collide. Its construction cost and storage are computed in
\Cref{sec:space-analysis} and \Cref{sec:construction-time}; for now
$\phi$ is a black-box bijection $A \to [n]$, extended by some rule to
the whole universe (the extension does not affect the FPR bound).

The departure from the Bloom design is exactly this collision-freedom.
An ordinary hash collides at the rate the range-to-universe ratio
allows, and the Bloom filter accommodates collisions by spreading each
insertion across $k$ independent hashes, the $k$-way conjunction at
query time absorbing the per-hash noise. The BHF instead commits to $A$
at build time and constructs a hash bespoke to $A$, collision-free by
design. With no per-hash noise to absorb, a single fingerprint
comparison replaces the $k$-way conjunction. Building $\phi$ is the
workload the BHF moves from query time, where Bloom pays for it in
extra hash evaluations and bits, to build time, where it is paid once
and amortized.

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{(ii) The fingerprint table $F$ and hash $g$.} The
\emph{fingerprint table}
\[
  F \;:\; [n] \;\longrightarrow\; \{0, 1\}^r
\]
holds $n$ slots of $r$ bits each, with fingerprint width
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bhf-r}
  r \;=\; \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil,
\end{equation}
the smallest integer for which $2^{-r} \le \fprate$. It is populated by
a \emph{fingerprint hash} $g : \Universe \to \{0, 1\}^r$ assumed
uniform on its codomain, instantiated in practice as the low $r$ bits
of SHA-256$(x)$, a truncated MurmurHash3, or any family known to behave
uniformly on the application's inputs. Uniformity of $g$ is the only
hash-function assumption the FPR analysis of \Cref{sec:fpr-analysis}
needs.

The two hashes play complementary roles. $\phi$ never collides on $A$
and addresses the table; $g$ collides at the controlled rate $2^{-r}$
and supplies the fingerprint that discriminates members from
non-members. The width $r$ is the only place $\fprate$ enters the
construction: tightening the target from $\fprate$ to $\fprate^2$ adds
one bit to $r$ and doubles the table, the relation the entropy floor
predicts.

\subsection*{Construction and query}

Given $A$, $\phi$, and $g$, the table is built in one pass over $A$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bhf-insert}
  \text{for each } a \in A: \quad F[\phi(a)] \;\coloneqq\; g(a).
\end{equation}
Bijectivity of $\phi$ on $A$ writes each slot exactly once, so the
table is fully populated and records $g(a)$ at the slot $\phi(a)$
reserved for each $a$.

\begin{definition}[Bernoulli Hash Function]
\label{def:bhf}
The \emph{Bernoulli Hash Function} (BHF) for a designated set $A$ with
parameters $\phi$, $g$, and $r = \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil$ is the
pair $(\phi, F)$ together with the membership query
\[
  \textsc{Query}(x) \;=\; \top \;\text{ iff }\; F[\phi(x)] = g(x).
\]
After the construction pass \eqref{eq:bhf-insert}, $x \mapsto
\textsc{Query}(x)$ is the membership report of the approximate set
$\ASet{A}$.
\end{definition}

The data structure stores the perfect hash and the table; the query
reads one slot and compares two $r$-bit strings. There is no flat bit
array, no $k$-way conjunction, no occupancy parameter. Where the Bloom
filter spreads information about $A$ thinly across a shared array, the
BHF concentrates it: each element owns one slot holding the fingerprint
that distinguishes it from non-members. That concentration is what
closes the gap. In the Bloom filter, per-bit entropy is the quantity to
optimize, and its maximum at half occupancy costs $\log_2 e$ times the
floor; in the BHF the bit budget is set directly by the width $r$,
which equals the floor's $-\log_2 \fprate$ up to integer rounding, and
the per-bit optimization simply does not arise.

\Cref{alg:bhf-insert-query} writes the construction and query in
pseudocode.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
  \caption{BHF construction and query}
  \label{alg:bhf-insert-query}
  \SetKwInOut{Input}{input}
  \SetKwInOut{Output}{output}
  \SetKwProg{func}{procedure}{}{}
  \SetKwProg{fn}{function}{}{}

  \func{\textsc{Build}($A$)}{
    construct perfect hash $\phi : A \to [n]$\;
    allocate fingerprint table $F$ of size $n$, each slot $r$ bits\;
    \For{each $a \in A$}{
      $F[\phi(a)] \gets g(a)$\;
    }
    \Return $(\phi, F)$\;
  }
  \BlankLine
  \fn{\textsc{Query}($x$)}{
    $i \gets \phi(x)$\;
    \lIf{$F[i] = g(x)$}{\Return $\top$}
    \Return $\bot$\;
  }
\end{algorithm}

\textsc{Build} runs in one pass over $A$ plus the cost of constructing
$\phi$; \textsc{Query} runs in time dominated by two hash evaluations
and an $r$-bit comparison, constant in $n$. Against the Bloom
pseudocode of \Cref{alg:bloom-insert-query}, whose \textsc{Query}
evaluates $k$ hashes and reads $k$ bits, the BHF replaces $k$ hash
evaluations with $2$ and the $k$-way conjunction with a single equality
test, a query-time factor of $k/2 \approx (m/n)\ln 2 / 2 \approx 0.347
(m/n)$ at the Bloom optimum. The price is that the construction is
static (no in-place insertions) and the perfect hash takes $O(n)$ to
build; \Cref{sec:construction-time} treats the trade-off.

\subsection*{The false-negative rate is exactly zero}

The argument mirrors the Bloom case and is shorter. Suppose $x \in A$.
The build pass \eqref{eq:bhf-insert} set $F[\phi(x)] = g(x)$, and the
table is never modified afterward (the BHF is static), so $F[\phi(x)] =
g(x)$ at every query time. \textsc{Query}$(x)$ reads slot $\phi(x)$,
finds $g(x)$, recomputes $g(x)$, and returns $\top$. The argument rests
on three facts: the build pass wrote $g(\cdot)$ into slot $\phi(\cdot)$,
the table is immutable, and $\phi$ is a function, so the query-time slot
$\phi(x)$ equals the build-time slot. Hence
\[
  \fnrate \;=\; \Prob{\textsc{Query}(x) = \bot \Given x \in A} \;=\; 0
\]
for every $x \in A$ and every parameter setting. The BHF is one-sided,
on the same axis of the perfect-filter line as the Bloom filter; its
only failure mode is the false positive.

\subsection*{False-positive rate, preview}

For $x \notin A$, the slot $\phi(x)$ equals $\phi(a^*)$ for the unique
$a^* \in A$ mapped there, so the query reads $F[\phi(x)] = g(a^*)$ and
compares it to $g(x)$. By uniformity of $g$, the strings $g(a^*)$ and
$g(x)$ are independent uniform on $\{0, 1\}^r$, agreeing with
probability
\[
  \fprate_{\text{BHF}} \;\le\; 2^{-r}
  \;=\; 2^{-\lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil} \;\le\; \fprate.
\]
\Cref{sec:fpr-analysis} develops the argument, including the role of
the perfect-hash extension to $\Universe \setminus A$; the bound above
suffices for the comparison below.

\subsection*{A worked example}

Fix $n = 1000$ and target $\fprate = 0.01$. The fingerprint width is
\[
  r \;=\; \lceil -\log_2 0.01 \rceil \;=\; \lceil 6.644 \rceil \;=\; 7
  \text{ bits},
\]
since $2^{-7} \approx 0.0078 \le 0.01 \le 2^{-6} \approx 0.0156$. The
table occupies $n r = 7000$ bits ($875$ bytes); adding a modern
perfect-hash representation, which contributes a few bits per element
at $n = 1000$ (\Cref{sec:space-analysis}), brings the BHF total to
roughly $9000$ bits. The Bloom filter at the same target is sized by
\Cref{prop:bloom-overhead} at
\[
  m \;=\; n \cdot \log_2(1/\fprate) \cdot \log_2 e
  \;=\; 1000 \cdot 6.644 \cdot 1.4427
  \;\approx\; 9586 \text{ bits}.
\]
At $n = 1000$ the two occupy similar space, the perfect-hash correction
nearly cancelling the fingerprint saving. The picture changes at scale:
the perfect-hash term per element shrinks below one bit, leaving the
BHF at $r = 7$ bits against the Bloom filter's $9.59$, the full
$2.6$-bit advantage the floor allows. Against the floor itself,
$-\log_2 0.01 = 6.644$, the BHF's $7$ bits exceed it by the
integer-rounding gap of $0.356$ bits, while the Bloom filter sits
$\approx 2.95$ bits above. \Cref{sec:space-analysis} works the
comparison at $n = 10^6$ and tabulates it across target rates.
