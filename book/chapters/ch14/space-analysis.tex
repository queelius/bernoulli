\section{Space Analysis}
\label{sec:space-analysis}

The BHF stores two objects, the perfect hash $\phi$ and the fingerprint
table $F$, and the total bit budget is their sum. The fingerprint table
is the dominant term and matches the floor of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor}
up to the integer rounding of $r$; the perfect-hash representation is a
sublinear correction that vanishes asymptotically. Together they give
the chapter's headline: the BHF reaches the floor up to one bit of
rounding plus an $o(1)$ term, and the Bloom filter's $\log_2 e$
multiplicative overhead is gone.

\subsection*{Fingerprint storage}

The table $F : [n] \to \{0, 1\}^r$ holds $n$ slots of $r$ bits, so
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bhf-fingerprint-bits}
  L_{F} \;=\; n \cdot r \;=\; n \cdot \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil
\end{equation}
bits, with $r$ from \eqref{eq:bhf-r}. The dependence on the target rate
is logarithmic, each base-$10$ digit of precision costing about $3.32$
bits per element, and the dependence on set size is linear. There is no
padding and no per-slot overhead beyond the fingerprint: the table is a
flat array addressed by $\phi$, the densest representation of one
uniform fingerprint per element. The Bloom filter, by contrast, spreads
each insertion across $k$ bits and pays the half-occupancy load factor
that the optimal-$k$ analysis selects; the BHF avoids both penalties by
resolving collisions at build time.

The integer rounding in $r$ contributes between $0$ and $1$ bits per
element over the real-valued floor $-\log_2 \fprate$, since $\lceil x
\rceil - x \in [0, 1)$, averaging $0.5$ bits over a logarithmically
uniform $\fprate$. This additive, bounded overhead is the structural
contrast with the Bloom filter's $\log_2(1/\fprate)\cdot\log_2 e$ budget
(\Cref{prop:bloom-overhead}), whose overhead is multiplicative and
grows with $\log_2(1/\fprate)$. As $\fprate \to 0$ the BHF budget
approaches the floor; the Bloom budget stays $44\%$ above it.

\subsection*{Perfect-hash storage}

The perfect hash $\phi$ is itself a stored structure, since queries
need it to address slots. Its size depends on the construction. The BDZ
family of Belazzougui, Botelho, and Dietzfelbinger (\S14.7) achieves
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bdz-bound}
  L_{\phi} \;=\; O\bigl( n \log \log \log n / \log n \bigr)
\end{equation}
bits, which is $o(n)$: the per-element overhead $L_{\phi}/n = O(\log
\log \log n / \log n)$ tends to zero. Earlier constructions
(Fredman-Komlos-Szemeredi, Hagerup-Tholey, the displacement-based
families) reach at most $O(n)$ bits with leading constants of $2$ to
$4$ bits per element. At realistic scales ($n$ between $10^3$ and
$10^9$) a BDZ-style construction occupies on the order of $2$ to $3$
bits per element, and the present accounting takes \eqref{eq:bdz-bound}
as the bound with the constant treated as a small additive correction.

At $n = 10^6$ the asymptotic form evaluates to roughly $0.2$ to $0.5$
bits per element in practical implementations, against a fingerprint
term of $r$ bits ranging from $7$ at $\fprate = 10^{-2}$ to $20$ at
$\fprate = 10^{-6}$; the perfect-hash overhead is a few percent of the
fingerprint cost and shrinks at larger $n$, below $0.1$ bits per
element by $n = 10^9$. At deployment scale it is a rounding correction
to the fingerprint term, not a separate concern.

\subsection*{Total bits per element}

Combining the two terms,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bhf-total-bits}
  L_{\text{BHF}} \;=\; L_F + L_\phi
  \;=\; n \cdot \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil
  \;+\; O\bigl( n \log \log \log n / \log n \bigr),
\end{equation}
and dividing by $n$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bhf-per-element}
  L_{\text{BHF}} / n
  \;=\; \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil
  \;+\; O(\log \log \log n / \log n).
\end{equation}
The first term lies between $-\log_2 \fprate$ and $-\log_2 \fprate + 1$;
the second is $o(1)$.

\begin{theorem}[BHF optimality]
\label{thm:bhf-optimality}
The Bernoulli Hash Function of \Cref{def:bhf} on a designated set $A$
with $|A| = n$ and target $\fprate \in (0, 1)$ uses
\[
  L_{\text{BHF}} / n
  \;=\; \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil
  \;+\; o(1)
  \;=\; -\log_2 \fprate \;+\; O(1)
\]
bits per element, achieving the entropy floor of
\Cref{thm:entropy-floor} up to one bit of integer rounding plus a
sublinear correction. The multiplicative overhead $\log_2 e \approx
1.4427$ of the standard Bloom filter (\Cref{prop:bloom-overhead}) is
absent: the BHF's overhead is additive and bounded by a constant.
\end{theorem}

The theorem is the constructive complement to \Cref{thm:entropy-floor}.
The floor said no FNR-zero representation uses fewer than $-\log_2
\fprate$ bits per element asymptotically; the present theorem exhibits
one using no more than that plus an additive constant. Together they
pin the per-element cost of a sufficient FNR-zero construction to
$[-\log_2 \fprate + o(1),\, -\log_2 \fprate + 1 + o(1)]$, with the BHF
at the top of the interval and the slack the unavoidable cost of a
whole number of fingerprint bits.

The optimality can be sharpened from ``matches the floor'' to ``optimal
within its structural class.''

\begin{remark}[Optimality among perfect-hash-addressed structures]
\label{rem:bhf-class-optimal}
Consider any FNR-zero structure of the form $(\phi, F)$ with $\phi : A
\to [n]$ a perfect hash and $F : [n] \to \{0, 1\}^r$ a fingerprint
table. For a non-member $x$ mapped to slot $i$, the verdict is $\top$
iff $F[i]$ matches the $r$-bit string the query derives from $x$; under
any non-adversarial query distribution with a uniform fingerprint
derivation, the match probability is exactly $2^{-r}$, so $\fprate \ge
2^{-r}$. With the floor's $r \ge -\log_2 \fprate$, the achievable
$(r, \fprate)$ region is $\fprate \ge 2^{-r}$ and $r \ge \lceil -\log_2
\fprate \rceil$. The BHF sits at the corner, $r = \lceil -\log_2 \fprate
\rceil$ and $\fprate = 2^{-r} \le \fprate$: no perfect-hash-addressed
FNR-zero structure meets the same target with fewer bits per element.
\end{remark}

The Bloom filter pays a multiplicative $\log_2 e \approx 1.44$ at every

about $1.1$ megabytes.

\subsection*{The \texorpdfstring{$\lceil \cdot \rceil$}{ceiling} rounding}

The budget is $\lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil$ rather than $-\log_2
\fprate$ because $r$ is an integer; there is no fractional bit in a
table addressed by the perfect hash. The rounding is not wasted
silently: at integer $r$ the achieved FPR is $2^{-r}$, at most the
target but generally strictly below it. Writing $-\log_2 \fprate = k +
\delta$ with $k$ integer and $\delta \in [0, 1)$, the width is $r = k +
1$ when $\delta > 0$, and the achieved FPR is $2^{-r} = \fprate /
2^{1-\delta}$, smaller than $\fprate$ by a factor between $1$ (as
$\delta \to 1$) and $2$ (as $\delta \to 0^+$). The BHF over-achieves
the target by the rounding slack, on average about half.

The slack can in principle be recovered: arithmetic or block coding
across $k$ elements drives the per-element cost toward $-\log_2
\fprate$ without integer rounding, at the cost of query time (a
fractional-bit fingerprint cannot be compared at machine-word speed)
and implementation complexity. In practice the $\le 1$ bit of waste is
accepted and the BHF is deployed with integer $r$. The construction is
asymptotically optimal up to that rounding, which is small and
well-understood, and on those terms the BHF is the constructive answer
to the chapter-8 floor question.
