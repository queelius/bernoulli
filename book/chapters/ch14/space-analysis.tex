\section{Space Analysis}
\label{sec:space-analysis}

The BHF's representation is its salt plus the two parameters $N$ and
$t$. This section computes the salt's expected length exactly and shows
that it meets the floor of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor} to within an\index{space lower bound!approximate membership}
additive constant over the whole set, not per element. It then states
the perfect hash filter, the practical structure that replaces search by
addressing, and accounts for its budget honestly: fingerprints at the
floor up to rounding, plus a perfect hash that cannot cost less than
$\log_2 e$ bits per element.

\subsection*{The expected salt length}

The build scans salts in the order $b_1, b_2, \ldots$ and stops at the
first valid one, $b_Q$, whose length is $L = \lfloor \log_2 Q \rfloor$.
Since $L \ge \ell$ exactly when $Q \ge 2^{\ell}$, and $\Prob{Q \ge q} =
(1 - s)^{q - 1}$ for the geometric index,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bhf-salt-series}
  \Expect{L}
  \;=\; \sum_{\ell \ge 1} \Prob{Q \ge 2^{\ell}}
  \;=\; \sum_{\ell \ge 1} (1 - s)^{2^{\ell} - 1},
\end{equation}
with $s = \fprate^{n}$ from \eqref{eq:bhf-success}.

\begin{theorem}[Expected size of the BHF]
\label{thm:bhf-optimality}
Let the BHF of \Cref{def:bhf} be built for $|A| = n$ at rate $\fprate =
(t + 1)/N$, under the random-oracle model. Its expected salt length
satisfies
\[
  n \log_2(1/\fprate) \;-\; 1 \;-\; \log_2 e
  \;\le\; \Expect{L} \;\le\;
  n \log_2(1/\fprate).
\]
With $O(\log(nN))$ further bits to record $N$, $t$, and the salt's
length, the expected representation is $n \log_2(1/\fprate) +
O(\log(nN))$ bits, that is $-\log_2 \fprate + o(1)$ bits per element for
fixed $\fprate$, matching the floor of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor} to
leading order.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\ell^{*} = \log_2(1/s) = n \log_2(1/\fprate)$. For the upper
bound, $\lfloor \log_2 Q \rfloor \le \log_2 Q$, and Jensen's inequality
for the concave logarithm gives
$\Expect{L} \le \log_2 \Expect{Q} = \ell^{*}$.
For the lower bound, $\Prob{Q \le a} \le a s$ for every $a \ge 0$ by the
union bound over the first $\lfloor a \rfloor$ salts, so
\[
  \Expect{(\ell^{*} - \log_2 Q)_{+}}
  \;=\; \int_0^{\infty} \Prob{Q < 2^{\ell^{*} - u}}\, du
  \;\le\; \int_0^{\infty} 2^{-u}\, du
  \;=\; \log_2 e,
\]
hence $\Expect{\log_2 Q} \ge \ell^{*} - \log_2 e$, and $\lfloor \log_2 Q
\rfloor \ge \log_2 Q - 1$ gives the stated bound. The parameters and the
length field take $O(\log N + \log \ell^{*})$ bits, and $\ell^{*} = O(n
\log N)$.
\end{proof}

The slack is a constant for the whole set. At $s = 2^{-16}$, the worked
example of \Cref{sec:bhf-construction}, the series
\eqref{eq:bhf-salt-series} evaluates to $14.67$, which is $1.33$ bits
below $\ell^{*} = 16$; at $s = 2^{-20}$ it is $18.67$, again $1.33$
below. Nothing in the construction allocates bits per element, so the
fractional part of $\log_2(1/\fprate)$ is paid exactly: at $\fprate =
0.01$ the BHF's expected cost is at most $6.644$ bits per element plus the
$O(\log n)/n$ parameter term, not the $7$ that an integer-width
fingerprint would take. The equality-predicate variant
\parencite{towell-bernoulli-hash-function} has success probability
$\fprate^{n-1}$ and stores its target value, $\log_2(1/\fprate)$ bits, so
its total is the same.

None of this is new as a statement about seed search.\index{seed search} The expected index
of the first of a sequence of independent trials, each succeeding with
probability $s$, has a binary length of $\log_2(1/s) + O(1)$; this is the
seed-index scale on which the bucketed seed searches of modern minimal
perfect hashing operate
\parencite{esposito-graf-vigna-2020,lehmann-sanders-walzer-2024}, and
\textcite[Sec.~1.1]{lehmann-et-al-2025} analyze exactly the case of one
seed required to satisfy independent Bernoulli constraints: near-optimal
space and exponential construction. The BHF is that baseline, written in
the vocabulary of this book. Its value here is as the construction that
shows the floor of \Cref{thm:entropy-floor} is the right target, with
the build cost made explicit rather than hidden.

\subsection*{The perfect hash filter}

Polynomial build time comes from addressing instead of search. A
\emph{minimal perfect hash function}\index{minimal perfect hash function}
$\phi : \Universe \to [n]$ is injective on $A$ and maps it onto $[n] =
\{1, \ldots, n\}$; it is data-dependent, constructed at build time from
$A$, and defined (arbitrarily) on non-members. Pair it with a
\emph{fingerprint}\index{fingerprint} hash $g : \Universe \to \{0,
1\}^{r}$, uniform on its codomain, of width
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bhf-r}
  r \;=\; \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil,
\end{equation}
the least integer with $2^{-r} \le \fprate$, and fill a table $F : [n]
\to \{0, 1\}^r$ in one pass:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bhf-insert}
  \text{for each } a \in A: \quad F[\phi(a)] \;\coloneqq\; g(a).
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Perfect hash filter]
\label{def:phf}
The \emph{perfect hash filter} (PHF)\index{perfect hash filter} for $A$ with parameters $\phi$, $g$,
and $r$ is the pair $(\phi, F)$ built by \eqref{eq:bhf-insert}, with
membership query $\textsc{Query}(x) = \top$ iff $F[\phi(x)] = g(x)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
  \caption{Perfect hash filter construction and query}
  \label{alg:phf-build-query}
  \SetKwProg{func}{procedure}{}{}
  \SetKwProg{fn}{function}{}{}

  \func{\textsc{Build}($A$)}{
    construct a minimal perfect hash $\phi : A \to [n]$\;
    allocate $F$ with $n$ slots of $r$ bits\;
    \lFor{each $a \in A$}{$F[\phi(a)] \gets g(a)$}
    \Return $(\phi, F)$\;
  }
  \BlankLine
  \fn{\textsc{Query}($x$)}{
    \lIf{$F[\phi(x)] = g(x)$}{\Return $\top$}
    \Return $\bot$\;
  }
\end{algorithm}

A member reads back the fingerprint it wrote, so $\fnrate = 0$; a
non-member reads some member's fingerprint and matches it with
probability $2^{-r} \le \fprate$ (\Cref{prop:phf-fpr}). The structure is
the standard static approximate-membership construction: the pairing of
a minimal perfect hash with fingerprints of
$\lceil \log_2(1/\fprate) \rceil$ bits, and its overhead of about $\log_2 e$ bits per element, are
described by \textcite[Sec.~1]{dietzfelbinger-pagh-2008}, who remark
that the origin of the idea is unknown to them, and the program's
manuscript on the construction \parencite{towell-bernoulli-phf} restates
it as a Bernoulli set. Chapter~15 builds on it.

\begin{proposition}[Perfect hash filter space]
\label{prop:phf-space}
The perfect hash filter of \Cref{def:phf} uses $n \lceil -\log_2 \fprate
\rceil + L_{\phi}$ bits, where $L_{\phi}$ is the size of the minimal
perfect hash. For any minimal perfect hash function on a universe much\index{space lower bound!minimal perfect hashing}
larger than $n$, $L_{\phi} \ge n \log_2 e - O(\log n)$
\parencite{fredman-komlos-1984}, so
\[
  L_{\text{PHF}} / n
  \;\ge\; \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil + \log_2 e - o(1)
  \;\ge\; -\log_2 \fprate + 1.4427 - o(1).
\]
\end{proposition}

The proposition is a lower bound on this design, not on approximate
membership: the overhead is the cost of naming each member's slot, which
the floor does not require. It is \emph{additive}, where the Bloom
filter's $\log_2(1/\fprate) \cdot \log_2 e$ (\Cref{prop:bloom-overhead})\index{Bloom filter!space overhead}
exceeds the floor by a \emph{multiplicative} factor, so the perfect hash
filter wins once $\fprate$ is small enough and loses at loose targets.
Writing the addressing cost as $c$ bits per element and ignoring the
rounding of $r$, it beats the Bloom filter exactly when $(\log_2 e - 1)
\log_2(1/\fprate) > c$, that is when $\log_2(1/\fprate) > c / 0.4427$.
At the idealized $c = \log_2 e$ the break-even is near $\fprate =
2^{-3.26}$. Real minimal perfect hashes cost more: from about $1.5$ bits
per key for the seed-search constructions RecSplit and ShockHash, which
spend build time to approach $\log_2 e$
\parencite{esposito-graf-vigna-2020,lehmann-sanders-walzer-2024}, through
about $2$ for hash-displace-and-compress \parencite{belazzougui-2009}, to
about $3$ for BBHash \parencite{limasset-et-al-2017}. The companion
notebook (\texttt{bernoulli-py/\allowbreak notebooks/\allowbreak
ch14-bhf.ipynb}) measures $c = 3.23$ bits per key for its BBHash-backed
filter at $n = 1000$, which puts the break-even at $\log_2(1/\fprate)
\approx 7.3$: at $\fprate = 2^{-7}$ the Bloom filter is smaller ($10.10$
bits per key against $10.23$), and from $\fprate = 2^{-10}$ the perfect
hash filter is ($13.23$ against $14.43$). The perfect hash filter does
not dominate the Bloom filter on space; it dominates at tight targets,
by a margin that grows linearly in $\log_2(1/\fprate)$.

\begin{remark}[Optimality within the fingerprint-table class]
\label{rem:bhf-class-optimal}
Among FNR-zero structures that address an $r$-bit fingerprint table
through a perfect hash, the width $r = \lceil -\log_2 \fprate \rceil$ is
the least that meets the target: a non-member mapped to slot $i$ matches
the stored $r$-bit fingerprint with probability exactly $2^{-r}$ under a
uniform fingerprint derivation, so $\fprate \ge 2^{-r}$ forces $r \ge
-\log_2 \fprate$. Within the class the only slack is the integer rounding
and the addressing; neither is forced on approximate membership in
general.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Closing the gap at linear build time}

The addressing cost disappears if the structure need not say
\emph{where} each member's fingerprint is, only \emph{what} the
fingerprint of each member is. A
\emph{retrieval}\index{retrieval} structure (a static function) stores,
for each $a \in A$, an $r$-bit value $v(a)$ retrievable as $V(a)$, and
returns arbitrary values on non-members; storing $v = g$ and accepting $x$
when $V(x) = g(x)$ gives an FNR-zero filter with false-positive rate
$2^{-r}$ for non-members whose retrieved value is independent of $g(x)$.
\textcite{dietzfelbinger-pagh-2008} show that retrieval, and hence
approximate membership, is achievable within a factor $1 + \delta$ of
$n r$ bits for any fixed $\delta > 0$, with efficient construction and
constant-time queries; the Bloomier filter of
\textcite{chazelle-et-al-2004}\index{Bloomier filter} is the earlier retrieval-based design.
Practical descendants include the xor filter\index{xor filter}
\parencite{graf-lemire-2020}, at about $1.23\, n r$ bits, the binary fuse
filter\index{binary fuse filter} \parencite{graf-lemire-2022}, closer still, and ribbon
\parencite{dietzfelbinger-et-al-2026-ribbon}\index{ribbon filter}. Their false-positive rates
need the same care as any filter's: homogeneous ribbon, for instance, has
exact rate $p + (1 - p) 2^{-r}$, where $p$ is the probability of a
row-span event, so it is not the ideal $2^{-r}$ of independent
fingerprints. These, not the perfect
hash filter, are the practical answer to the floor; the integer rounding
of $r$ remains unless fingerprints are coded jointly.

perfect hash filter is built in linear time and sits a constant above

per element.
