\section*{Bibliographic Notes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Bibliographic Notes}
\label{sec:bib-notes-ch14}

\paragraph{The BHF construction as a manuscript.}
The construction of \Cref{def:bhf} and the optimality result of
\Cref{thm:bhf-optimality} are drawn from
\textcite{towell-bernoulli-hash-function}, ``The Bernoulli Hash
Function: Optimal Bernoulli Sets and Maps,'' the manuscript that
introduced the BHF as the constructive complement to the entropy
floor of chapter~8. The manuscript treats the BHF in greater
generality than the chapter allows, extending the membership
construction of \Cref{sec:bhf-construction} to approximate maps
(the value-bearing analogue, where each slot stores not a
fingerprint but a key-value pair) and to the perfect-hash filter
of chapter~15. The space and FPR analyses of
\Cref{sec:space-analysis} and \Cref{sec:fpr-analysis} are
specializations of the manuscript's more general framework to the
set-membership case. A reader who wants the algebraic
generalization, with the BHF as a particular instance of the
``optimal Bernoulli map'' construction, should treat the manuscript
as the natural next reading.

\paragraph{The universal-hashing foundation.}
The fingerprint hash $g$ that appears in \Cref{prop:bhf-fpr}
inherits its uniformity guarantee from the universal hashing
literature, originating with \textcite{carter-wegman-1979}'s
``Universal Classes of Hash Functions.'' Carter and Wegman
constructed the first hash-function family with provable pairwise
independence (now called $2$-universal), establishing that
randomization at the hash-function level suffices to bound
collision probabilities to $1/|\text{codomain}|$ in the average
case over the family's random selection. The chapter's
fingerprint-hash analysis selects $g$ from such a family at build
time and treats the selection as fixed for the data structure's
lifetime; the collision probability bound then applies pointwise
over query points. Subsequent refinements (Wegman-Carter 1981 on
$k$-universal families, Siegel 1989 on faster construction) are
not needed for the BHF analysis, which requires only pairwise
independence. The Carter-Wegman paper remains the standard
reference for the result the BHF analysis depends on.

\paragraph{The modern perfect-hash construction.}
The sublinear perfect-hash bound that
\Cref{sec:space-analysis} cited and the linear-expected-time
construction algorithms that \Cref{sec:construction-time} sketched
both trace to a long line of work culminating in
\textcite{belazzougui-2009}'s ``Hash, Displace, and Compress''
algorithm (the BDZ scheme in the data-structures literature). BDZ
achieves $O(n)$ expected construction time with output bits in the
range $O(n \log \log \log n / \log n)$ at concrete constants below
$3$ bits per element for $n$ in the millions. The algorithm
combines hash-and-displace placement (a fingerprint-table analogue
that resolves collisions by displacement vectors) with output
compression (encoding the displacement values entropy-efficiently).
The chapter quoted the BDZ asymptotic bound as a black-box
subroutine; the BDZ paper is the place to read the algorithm in
full and to understand the constants the BHF inherits at finite
$n$. Earlier perfect-hash constructions (Fredman-Komlos-Szemeredi
1984 for the classical $O(n \log n)$-bit bound; Hagerup-Tholey 2001
for the first $O(n)$-bit construction; the MWHC and BBHash
displacement-based families for the implementation lineage) are
the historical antecedents that BDZ improves on.

\paragraph{Comparison data structures: cuckoo and quotient filters.}
Two contemporary data structures occupy design spaces adjacent to
the BHF and deserve mention as comparison points.
\textcite{fan-cuckoo-2014}'s cuckoo filter is a streaming-capable
alternative to the Bloom filter that uses cuckoo hashing to store
fingerprints in a cache-friendly layout. The cuckoo filter
achieves slightly better space than the standard Bloom filter
(approximately $1.05$ to $1.3$ times the entropy floor depending
on load factor, versus the Bloom filter's $1.44$) while supporting
insertions and deletions in amortized $O(1)$ time. It does not
reach the BHF's $1.00$ asymptotic ratio but recovers about half
the Bloom filter's overhead while remaining streamable, making it
a competitive choice when the static-set restriction is
unacceptable. \textcite{bender-quotient-2012}'s quotient filter is
a related design that stores fingerprints in a quotient-style
addressing scheme allowing cache-efficient access and insertion.
The quotient filter operates on similar space-time trade-offs to
the cuckoo filter and offers similar streaming properties. Neither
construction matches the BHF's asymptotic optimality, but both
narrow the gap that the standard Bloom filter pays and extend the
streaming use case beyond what the BHF supports. A reader designing
a deployment that needs both space efficiency and streaming
updates should evaluate cuckoo and quotient filters as the
practical middle ground between the Bloom filter and the BHF.
