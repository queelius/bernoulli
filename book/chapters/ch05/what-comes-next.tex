\section{What Comes Next}
\label{sec:ch5-what-comes-next}

The chapter has built one abstraction and worked one example.
\Cref{sec:from-atom-to-set} lifted the noisy bit of Part~I to a function
$\ASet{A} : \Universe \to \Bernoulli{\BoolType}$, the approximate set.
\Cref{sec:bloom-from-scratch} built the Bloom filter as the canonical
realization of that abstraction and pinned down its rates.
\Cref{sec:predicate-algebra} placed the abstraction inside chapter~4's
formal machinery: every query is a $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance,
every $n$-query workload is a Kronecker product of per-block channels,
and the per-set rates $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ are the only parameters that
survive Axiom~2. \Cref{sec:one-sided-two-sided} separated the shapes an
approximate set can take and pointed at the perfect filter line. What
remains to do in Part~II is the algebra and the bounds that the
abstraction underwrites. Four chapters follow, each picking up one
strand from the present chapter and carrying it to its proper
conclusion.

\subsection*{Chapter 6: the algebra of sets}

\Cref{ch:set-composition} takes up set operations. Approximate sets
have unions, intersections, complements, and differences; each
operation has rates that follow from the rates of its inputs. The
calculation is not new machinery; it is the composition theorem of
\Cref{sec:composition-theorem} applied to the per-element predicates
described in \Cref{sec:predicate-algebra}. The union of two Bloom
filters by bit-OR gives an FPR of
$1 - (1 - \fprate_1)(1 - \fprate_2)$, the intersection gives a cleaner
$\fprate_1 \fprate_2$, and the complement swaps the two coordinate
axes. The chapter then asks which subfamilies of approximate sets are
closed under these operations. The FNR-zero family of
\Cref{sec:one-sided-two-sided} is closed under union and intersection
(one-sidedness is preserved in the same direction), but the complement
maps it to the FPR-zero family on the opposite axis. The perfect filter
line is a subalgebra under some operations and not others, and the
asymmetry foreshadows chapter~15's constructive treatment of the
FPR-zero side.

\subsection*{Chapter 7: classification measures}

\Cref{ch:classification-measures} measures quality. The two rates
$(\fprate, \fnrate)$ are a complete intrinsic description, but
practitioners often want derived quantities: positive predictive value,
negative predictive value, recall, F1. Each is a function of the two
rates and the workload's prevalence of in-set elements, and each
depends on the workload in a way the rates themselves do not. A Bloom
filter with $\fprate = 0.01$ achieves a PPV near $0.99$ on a workload
of mostly true positives and a PPV near $0.5$ on a workload of mostly
out-of-set queries. The chapter develops the closed forms, derives
sampling distributions and confidence intervals for the estimators,
and retires the deferral \Cref{sec:sampling-rates} flagged: the Wald
interval was a placeholder, and the Wilson, Clopper-Pearson, and
Jeffreys alternatives that handle the boundary properly are stated and
applied. The chapter that began with a sawtooth in chapter~1's
notebook ends here with the proper estimator vocabulary in place.

\subsection*{Chapter 8: information-theoretic bounds}

\Cref{ch:information-bounds} bounds the space. Any FNR-zero
approximate set that achieves false-positive rate $\fprate$ must
consume at least $-\log_2 \fprate$ bits per stored element. The proof
runs through Shannon entropy and the source-coding theorem; the
statement is sharp. The standard Bloom filter of
\Cref{sec:bloom-from-scratch} achieves $\log_2 e \cdot \log_2(1/\fprate)
\approx 1.44 \log_2(1/\fprate)$ bits per element under
\Cref{thm:bloom-optimal-k}, which is roughly $44\%$ above the floor.
That $44\%$ overhead is exactly the gap \Cref{sec:one-sided-two-sided}
named in its bit-budget comparison. The chapter's job is to make the
gap precise. The construction that closes it is chapter~14's, not
chapter~8's.

\subsection*{Chapter 14: the construction that closes the gap}

\Cref{ch:optimal-bernoulli-sets} is the constructive payoff of the
bound. The Bernoulli Hash Function (BHF) is an approximate set whose
bit-per-item cost achieves the entropy lower bound up to lower-order
terms. The $44\%$ overhead the standard Bloom filter pays is the right
kind of unfinished business: a gap that is real, that is calculable,
and that admits a closed-form remedy. The BHF construction is one of
the climactic results of the book; chapters 5 through 8 build the
language in which the result can be both stated and appreciated, and
chapter~14 then delivers it.

\Cref{ch:perfect-hash-filters} is the FPR-zero counterpart on the
other half of the perfect filter line. It takes the chapter~6 algebraic
constraint seriously and constructs a representation that achieves
$\fprate = 0$ on a designated set, paying the dual price the asymmetry
of \Cref{sec:one-sided-two-sided} predicts.

\subsection*{Closing}

The bibliographic notes of \Cref{sec:bloom-from-scratch}'s parent
chapter, drafted next, name the antecedents of what we have done:
Bloom's $1970$ paper, the survey literature that grew up around it,
and the Bernoulli-set papers that recast the data structure as an
instance of a predicate algebra. The four chapters previewed above
develop that algebra into a working theory of approximate-set
operations, classification measures, information-theoretic bounds, and
space-optimal constructions. Each picks up one of the strands the
present chapter has set down; together they make the approximate set a
first-class object of study.
