\section{One-Sided vs Two-Sided Approximate Sets}
\label{sec:one-sided-two-sided}

The Bloom filter has one error rate exactly zero, by construction rather
than tuning. That is one shape an approximate set can take; others force
the false-positive rate to zero on a designated set, or allow both rates to
be positive. The shapes are not interchangeable, and the distinction
organizes the constructions of \Cref{ch:set-composition},
\Cref{ch:optimal-bernoulli-sets}, and \Cref{ch:perfect-hash-filters}.

\begin{definition}[One-sided and two-sided approximate sets]
\label{def:one-sided}
An approximate set $\ASet{A}$ with rates $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ is
\emph{one-sided in FNR} (FNR-zero) if $\fnrate = 0 < \fprate$; \emph{one-sided
in FPR} (FPR-zero) if $\fprate = 0 < \fnrate$; and \emph{two-sided} if both
rates are positive. The Bloom filter (\Cref{def:bloom-filter}) is FNR-zero;
the perfect hash filter of \Cref{ch:perfect-hash-filters} is FPR-zero on its
design domain.
\end{definition}

The name tracks the rate that is zero. A one-sided set sits on a coordinate
axis of the rate square $[0,1]^2$; a two-sided set in its interior; the
origin $\fprate = \fnrate = 0$ is the exact set, excluded as not
approximate. The Z-channel of \Cref{def:z-channel} is the per-query analog,
and the one-sided property is its per-set lift.

\subsection*{Why FNR-zero is the natural default}

The Bloom filter's FNR-zero property came for free from monotonicity: the
insert primitive only sets bits, so a query for an inserted item always
reads ones. Any structure on a monotone insert-only primitive shares it,
which is why summaries over append-only logs and hash-keyed sketches are
FNR-zero for the same structural reason. Forcing the opposite asymmetry is
harder. Making $\fprate = 0$ on a designated set requires the construction
to certify membership, which a stored copy of the set would do, at the cost
of the space an approximate set exists to save. The constructive path must
be cleverer: \Cref{ch:perfect-hash-filters} computes a perfect hash for a
fixed $A$ and stores one fingerprint per element, so a query for $x \in A$
lands on its fingerprint and is certain while a query for $x \notin A$ may
still collide. The set must be known up front and updates are expensive;
those costs buy certainty in the positive direction.

\subsection*{Safety versus space}

One-sidedness is valuable because one direction of the answer is exact: a
Bloom filter's $\bot$ is certain (the element is definitely absent), so a
cache can skip a lookup or a spell-checker can pass a known word without
follow-up. An FPR-zero filter has the dual guarantee, a certain $\top$.
Two-sided sets give up the certain direction to recover space, as a
\emph{counting} Bloom filter does: it replaces bits with small counters to
support deletion, and counter saturation, where an overflowed counter is
later decremented to zero, introduces a positive $\fnrate$ along with a
roughly fourfold space cost.

The size of the trade is calculable. By \Cref{eq:bloom-optimal-k} a Bloom
filter at $\fprate = 0.01$, $\fnrate = 0$ needs $m \approx 1.44\,n
\log_2(1/\fprate) \approx 9.6\,n$ bits.
\Cref{ch:information-bounds} shows the floor for any FNR-zero set at the
same $\fprate$ is $n\log_2(1/\fprate) \approx 6.64\,n$ bits, so the Bloom
filter pays a fixed $1.44\times$ overhead, the standard literature's
$\log_2 e$ constant, which \Cref{ch:optimal-bernoulli-sets} closes. A
two-sided set is not bound by the one-sided floor at $\fprate$, because it
can split its error budget across both directions, but it spends the
certain $\bot$ that the extra bits bought.

\subsection*{The perfect filter line}

The rate plane $(\fprate, \fnrate) \in [0,1]^2$ parameterizes every
approximate set. The one-sided sets occupy the two axes, $\{\fnrate = 0\}$
and $\{\fprate = 0\}$; we call their union the \emph{perfect filter line},
each axis a one-dimensional locus with one rate-degree of freedom. Two-sided
sets fill the interior; the exact set is the origin.

The line is an algebraic constraint, not a passive label.
\Cref{ch:set-composition} derives the union, intersection, and complement of
Bernoulli sets and shows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the union of two FNR-zero sets is FNR-zero;
  \item the intersection of two FNR-zero sets is FNR-zero;
  \item the complement swaps the axes, sending FNR-zero to FPR-zero.
\end{itemize}
The FPR-zero side is not closed under the same operations: the union of two
FPR-zero sets is FPR-zero only when both are exact on the same designated
set, a fragile condition. The asymmetry parallels the construction-side one,
FNR-zero the natural default, FPR-zero requiring care.
\Cref{sec:bridge-to-sets} already previewed the union-as-bit-OR identity for
FNR-zero filters as a special case of the composition theorem;
\Cref{ch:set-composition} generalizes it.
\Cref{ch:optimal-bernoulli-sets} and \Cref{ch:perfect-hash-filters} then
take up the two halves of the line: the FNR-zero family and its entropy
floor, and the FPR-zero construction that pays the dual price.
