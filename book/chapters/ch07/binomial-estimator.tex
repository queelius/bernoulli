\section{The Binomial Estimator: Properties}
\label{sec:binomial-estimator}

The estimator $\hat{\fprate} = \mathrm{FP}/n_0$ that
\Cref{sec:sampling-rates} reported without proof is unbiased for $\fprate$,
has variance $\fprate(1 - \fprate)/n_0$, equals the maximum-likelihood
estimator, and attains the Cram\'er--Rao lower bound. Five short propositions
settle these claims. Of the $n$ trials, the $n_0$ with truth $\bot$ are the
only ones a false positive can come from; for each, let $X_i = 1$ if the noisy
bit reports $\top$. Axiom~1 makes the $X_i$ i.i.d.\ $\mathrm{Bernoulli}
(\fprate)$, so $\mathrm{FP} = \sum_{i=1}^{n_0} X_i \sim
\mathrm{Binomial}(n_0, \fprate)$ and $\hat{\fprate} = \mathrm{FP}/n_0$. The
arguments for $\hat{\fnrate} = \mathrm{FN}/n_1$ are identical under
$\fprate \to \fnrate$, $n_0 \to n_1$.

\begin{proposition}[Unbiasedness]
\label{prop:binomial-unbiased}
$\Expect{\hat{\fprate}} = \fprate$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By linearity, $\Expect{\mathrm{FP}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n_0} \Expect{X_i} = n_0
\fprate$, and dividing by the constant $n_0$ gives $\Expect{\hat{\fprate}} =
\fprate$.
\end{proof}

The bias is zero at every $\fprate$ and every $n_0 \ge 1$, not merely in the
limit.

\begin{proposition}[Variance]
\label{prop:binomial-variance}
$\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\fprate}) = \fprate(1 - \fprate)/n_0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each $X_i$ has $\mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \fprate - \fprate^2 = \fprate(1 -
\fprate)$; independence makes $\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{FP}) = n_0 \fprate(1 -
\fprate)$, and the scaling rule $\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{FP}/n_0) =
\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{FP})/n_0^2$ gives the result.
\end{proof}

The standard error shrinks as $1/\sqrt{n_0}$, and the numerator $\fprate(1 -
\fprate)$ peaks at $\fprate = 1/2$ and vanishes at the boundaries: the rate is
hardest to pin down when the classifier is maximally noisy, the fact that
returns in \Cref{sec:confidence-intervals} as the reason Wald intervals deform
near $0$ and $1$.

\begin{proposition}[The MLE is the empirical estimator]
\label{prop:binomial-mle}
The maximum-likelihood estimator of $\fprate$ from $\mathrm{FP} \sim
\mathrm{Binomial}(n_0, \fprate)$ is $\hat{\fprate}_{\mathrm{MLE}} =
\mathrm{FP}/n_0 = \hat{\fprate}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The log-likelihood $\log L(\fprate) = \mathrm{const} + \mathrm{FP} \log
\fprate + (n_0 - \mathrm{FP}) \log(1 - \fprate)$ has derivative
$\mathrm{FP}/\fprate - (n_0 - \mathrm{FP})/(1 - \fprate)$, whose zero solves
$\mathrm{FP}(1 - \fprate) = (n_0 - \mathrm{FP})\fprate$, i.e.\ $\fprate =
\mathrm{FP}/n_0$. The second derivative $-\mathrm{FP}/\fprate^2 - (n_0 -
\mathrm{FP})/(1 - \fprate)^2$ is negative on $(0,1)$, so the critical point is
the unique interior maximum; the boundary counts $\mathrm{FP} \in \{0, n_0\}$
give $\hat\fprate_{\mathrm{MLE}} \in \{0, 1\}$, again $\mathrm{FP}/n_0$.
\end{proof}

The empirical estimator is what likelihood theory asks for, not merely a
convenient choice. It also equals the first-moment method-of-moments estimator
(equate $\Expect{\mathrm{FP}} = n_0 \fprate$ to the observed count). The
coincidence is special to one-parameter exponential families like the
binomial, whose variance is fixed by the mean; in a two-parameter model such
as $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ the first moment is one equation in two unknowns, the
moment hierarchy must reach the second moment to identify both parameters, and
maximum likelihood and the method of moments generally diverge.

\begin{proposition}[Fisher information and the Cram\'er--Rao bound]
\label{prop:fisher-information}
The Fisher information is $I(\fprate) = n_0 / [\fprate(1 - \fprate)]$, so every
unbiased estimator $T$ of $\fprate$ satisfies $\mathrm{Var}(T) \ge 1/I(\fprate)
= \fprate(1 - \fprate)/n_0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The second derivative from \Cref{prop:binomial-mle}, in expectation under
$\mathrm{FP} \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_0, \fprate)$ with $\Expect{\mathrm{FP}} =
n_0 \fprate$, gives
\[
  I(\fprate) = -\Expect{\frac{\partial^2 \log L}{\partial \fprate^2}}
  = \frac{n_0}{\fprate} + \frac{n_0}{1 - \fprate}
  = \frac{n_0}{\fprate(1 - \fprate)}.
\]
The binomial family is regular (fixed support, twice-differentiable
log-likelihood on $(0,1)$), so the Cram\'er--Rao inequality applies and
$\mathrm{Var}(T) \ge 1/I(\fprate)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Efficiency]
\label{prop:binomial-efficiency}
$\hat{\fprate} = \mathrm{FP}/n_0$ attains the Cram\'er--Rao bound and is an
efficient unbiased estimator of $\fprate$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\Cref{prop:binomial-variance} gives $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\fprate}) = \fprate(1 -
\fprate)/n_0$, which equals the bound $1/I(\fprate)$ of
\Cref{prop:fisher-information}.
\end{proof}

No unbiased estimator of $\fprate$ does better in mean-squared error within
the binomial model: a candidate reporting a lower variance is biased, trading
bias for variance. What remains is to turn the point estimate into a
confidence statement that holds at every $n_0$, not only asymptotically, which
\Cref{sec:confidence-intervals} takes up.
