\section*{Bibliographic Notes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Bibliographic Notes}
\label{sec:bib-notes-ch7}

\paragraph{The classification-measures manuscript.}
The treatment of PPV, NPV, recall, and F1 as random variables that are
functions of $(\fprate, \fnrate, \pi)$, together with the delta-method
derivations of their variances and Wald-style confidence intervals,
follows \textcite{towell-bernoulli-classification-measures}. That
manuscript is the primary source for \Cref{sec:derived-measures}: the
PPV and NPV expressions, the prevalence-dependence diagnostic, the
bivariate delta-method computation that yields \Cref{prop:ppv-variance},
and the $\Fone$ derivation are all taken from it. The chapter's
contribution beyond the manuscript is the pedagogical scaffolding ---
the explicit retirement of the four items deferred in
\Cref{sec:sampling-rates}, the worked Bloom filter scenario of
\Cref{sec:bloom-diagnostics}, and the connection between the
manuscript's machinery and the Part~II approximate-set framework via
the predicate $\ASet{A} : \Universe \to \Bernoulli{\BoolType}$.

\paragraph{Wilson's score interval.}
The interval of \Cref{def:wilson-ci} is due to \textcite{wilson-1927},
who derived it by inverting a different statistic from the one that
gives the Wald form: instead of fixing $\fprate = \hat\fprate$ in the
variance and asking which true values are consistent with the observed
deviation, Wilson keeps $\fprate$ as a free parameter inside the
variance and solves the resulting quadratic in $\fprate$. The
construction is a special case of test inversion (set-of-parameter
values not rejected by a level-$\alpha$ test), but applied to the
score statistic rather than the Wald statistic. The 1927 paper frames
the result in the language of the ``law of succession'' and was
written before the modern frequentist-CI vocabulary had stabilized;
later texts standardized the procedure and gave it the name now in
common use. Wilson's interval is the default modern recommendation
for the binomial proportion when $n_0$ is small or the rate is near a
boundary, and it is the recommendation this chapter inherits.

\paragraph{The Clopper-Pearson exact interval.}
The construction of \Cref{def:clopper-pearson-ci} is due to
\textcite{clopper-pearson-1934}, whose paper introduces the
test-inversion idea in its cleanest form. For each candidate
$\fprate$, run a two-sided binomial test at level $\alpha$; the
interval is the set of $\fprate$ values that the test fails to
reject. The construction is sometimes called the exact interval
because it uses the exact binomial CDF rather than a Normal
approximation, but as the chapter notes, the discreteness of the
binomial forces the coverage strictly above $1 - \alpha$ at most
values of $\fprate$. The over-coverage is the price of the guarantee
and is precisely what one wants in safety-critical applications.
The Jeffreys interval of \Cref{def:jeffreys-ci} predates the modern
Bayesian terminology under which it is now derived; its calibration
property (matching frequentist coverage to higher order than Wald)
is a result in higher-order asymptotic theory and is treated in
standard texts on asymptotic inference.

\paragraph{The delta method.}
The asymptotic-Normality result for smooth functions of an
asymptotically Normal estimator (\Cref{thm:delta-method}) is a
classical consequence of the first-order Taylor expansion and
Slutsky's lemma. A proof in the form most directly usable for the
chapter's PPV and $\Fone$ derivations appears in
\textcite{casella-berger}, Chapter~5, alongside the univariate and
multivariate versions, the regularity conditions, and worked examples.
The chapter cites that text for the proof and applies the result
without re-deriving it. Readers who want a more thorough development
of asymptotic theory, including the higher-order corrections that
explain why Wilson and Jeffreys outperform Wald in the small-$n_0$
regime, should consult a standard graduate text on large-sample
inference.

\paragraph{Diagnostic testing and the prevalence question.}
The prevalence-dependence of PPV and NPV at fixed $(\fprate, \fnrate)$
--- the central caveat of \Cref{sec:derived-measures} --- is the same
phenomenon that drives the medical diagnostic-testing literature
referenced in \Cref{sec:bib-notes-ch1}: the bilingual conversation
between epidemiology, signal detection, and information retrieval over
which marginal to condition on and which derived rate gets the
spotlight. A test with high sensitivity and high specificity can still
have low PPV when the disease is rare, because PPV is the
classifier-plus-workload combination and not the classifier alone.
Chapter~1's bibliographic notes (\Cref{sec:bib-notes-ch1}) record the
contingency-table tradition that this chapter inherits; chapter~7's
contribution is to make the dependence explicit in formula and to give
the reader a proper CI for any of the derived quantities at any
specified prevalence.
