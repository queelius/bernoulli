\section{Derived Measures}
\label{sec:derived-measures}

The fourth deferred item is the derived measures. Precision, recall, $\Fone$,
and the two predictive values appear throughout binary classification and
information retrieval, and \Cref{sec:quantifying-noise} noted that precision
depends on a quantity the noisy bit does not control: the workload's
prevalence of $\top$. This section makes that precise, writing each measure as
a function of $(\fprate, \fnrate, \pi)$ and propagating the binomial
confidence intervals through the delta method.

\subsection*{Definitions}

Apply a noisy bit with rates $\fprate, \fnrate$ to a workload of $n$ inputs
whose truth is i.i.d.\ $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi)$ with $\pi = \Prob{X = \top}$.
The expected contingency-table counts are
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    \Expect{\TP} &= n\pi(1 - \fnrate), & \qquad \Expect{\FN} &= n\pi\fnrate, \\
    \Expect{\FP} &= n(1 - \pi)\fprate, & \qquad \Expect{\TN} &= n(1 - \pi)(1 - \fprate),
  \end{aligned}
\]
the true-$\top$ counts summing to $n\pi$, the true-$\bot$ counts to $n(1 -
\pi)$. Recall\index{recall} is the row-conditional rate $\Recall = \tprate =
1 - \fnrate$, with $\pi$ cancelling:
recall is a property of the noisy bit alone. The predictive values and $\Fone$
are column quantities, and $\pi$ does not cancel.

\begin{definition}[Positive predictive value]
\label{def:ppv}
\index{positive predictive value}\index{precision}At workload prevalence $\pi$,
\[
  \PPV = \Precision = \frac{\pi(1 - \fnrate)}{\pi(1 - \fnrate) + (1 - \pi)\fprate},
\]
the ratio of $\Expect{\TP}$ to the $\top$-output column total $\Expect{\TP + \FP}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Negative predictive value]
\label{def:npv}
\index{negative predictive value}At workload prevalence $\pi$,
\[
  \NPV = \frac{(1 - \pi)(1 - \fprate)}{(1 - \pi)(1 - \fprate) + \pi\fnrate},
\]
the mirror of PPV on the $\bot$-output column.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\Fone$-score]
\label{def:f1}
\index{F1 score@$F_1$ score}$\Fone$ is the harmonic mean of precision and recall,
\[
  \Fone = \frac{2\Precision \cdot \Recall}{\Precision + \Recall}
        = \frac{2\pi(1 - \fnrate)}{\pi(1 - \fnrate) + \pi + (1 - \pi)\fprate},
\]
the second form using $\pi(1 - \fnrate) + \pi\fnrate = \pi$.
\end{definition}

\noindent $\Fone$ weights precision and recall equally; the weighted family
$F_\beta = (1 + \beta^2)\Precision\Recall/(\beta^2\Precision + \Recall)$
tilts toward recall for $\beta > 1$ and precision for $\beta < 1$, recovering
$\Fone$ at $\beta = 1$. Replacing the parameters by their estimates turns each
formula into a point estimator.

\subsection*{Prevalence dependence}

Read PPV as a function of $\pi$ at fixed rates. At $\pi = 0$ the numerator
vanishes, so $\PPV(0) = 0$ when $\fprate > 0$: a workload with no genuine
positives reports only false positives. At $\pi = 1$ the denominator collapses
to the numerator and $\PPV(1) = 1$. Between, PPV rises monotonically, steeply
near $\pi = 0$ when $\fprate$ is appreciable; NPV runs the mirror story,
$\NPV(0) = 1$, $\NPV(1) = 0$. The framing is the point. The pair $(\fprate,
\fnrate)$ is intrinsic to the classifier; precision is a joint property of
classifier and workload, and a vendor who reports only precision is reporting
what their evaluation workload happened to look like; reading a high
$\tprate$ and $\tnrate$ as a high PPV on a low-prevalence workload is the
base-rate fallacy\index{base-rate fallacy}. The book uses $(\fprate,
\fnrate)$ as the primary description for exactly this reason.

Not every scalar summary depends on the workload. Youden's index
\parencite{youden-1950},
\[
  J \;=\; \tprate + \tnrate - 1 \;=\; 1 - \fprate - \fnrate,
\]
uses only the two intrinsic rates\index{Youden's J@Youden's $J$}. It is zero
exactly when the report is independent of the truth ($1 - \fnrate =
\fprate$), and it is the height of the operating point above the chance
diagonal of the receiver-operating-characteristic plane. Accuracy\index{accuracy},
$\pi(1 - \fnrate) + (1 - \pi)(1 - \fprate)$, is by contrast a workload
quantity, a prevalence-weighted average of $\tprate$ and $\tnrate$.

\subsection*{Estimation and the delta method}

The estimator $\widehat{\PPV} = \TP/(\TP + \FP)$ is a ratio of two independent
binomial counts. Conditioning on the row totals $n_\top$ and $n_\bot$,
\[
  \TP \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_\top, \tprate),
  \qquad
  \FP \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_\bot, \fprate),
\]
independent by Axiom~1. A ratio's expectation is not the ratio of
expectations, so $\widehat{\PPV}$ is biased (small when both means are large,
and consistent as $n \to \infty$), and its variance is read off by the delta
method rather than directly.

\begin{theorem}[Delta method]
\label{thm:delta-method}
\index{delta method}Let $g$ be differentiable at $\theta$ with $g'(\theta)
\neq 0$. If $\sqrt{n}\,(\hat\theta - \theta)$ converges in distribution to
$\mathrm{Normal}(0, \sigma^2(\theta))$, then
\[
  \sqrt{n}\,\bigl(g(\hat\theta) - g(\theta)\bigr) \xrightarrow{d}
  \mathrm{Normal}\bigl(0, [g'(\theta)]^2\sigma^2(\theta)\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\noindent This is the first-order Taylor expansion $g(\hat\theta) \approx
g(\theta) + g'(\theta)(\hat\theta - \theta)$ with Slutsky's lemma; a proof is
in \textcite[Ch.~5]{casella-berger}. The multivariate form replaces
$g'(\theta)$ by the gradient and $\sigma^2$ by a covariance matrix, giving the
quadratic form $\nabla g^\top \Sigma\, \nabla g$.

\begin{proposition}[Delta-method variance of $\widehat{\PPV}$]
\label{prop:ppv-variance}
With $\bar t = n_\top\tprate$, $\bar f = n_\bot\fprate$, $\sigma_t^2 =
n_\top\tprate(1 - \tprate)$, $\sigma_f^2 = n_\bot\fprate(1 - \fprate)$,
\[
  \Var[\widehat{\PPV}] \approx
  \frac{\bar f^{\,2}\sigma_t^2 + \bar t^{\,2}\sigma_f^2}{(\bar t + \bar f)^4}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $g(t, f) = t/(t + f)$, the gradient is $\nabla g = (t + f)^{-2}(f, -t)$.
The covariance of $(\TP, \FP)$ is $\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_t^2, \sigma_f^2)$ by
independence, and the quadratic form $\nabla g^\top \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_t^2,
\sigma_f^2)\nabla g$ at the mean $(\bar t, \bar f)$ gives the stated formula.
\end{proof}

A Wald-style PPV interval is $\widehat{\PPV} \pm z_{\alpha/2}
\sqrt{\widehat{\Var[\widehat{\PPV}]}}$, which inherits the boundary and skew
failures of the Wald rate interval; the remedy is a Wilson-style score
interval on the column-conditional binomial $\TP \mid \TP + \FP \sim
\mathrm{Binomial}(\TP + \FP, \PPV)$, which stays inside $[0,1]$ by
construction, or a bootstrap over the contingency table.

\subsection*{$\Fone$ via the delta method}

Writing $\Fone = 2\TP/(\TP + \FP + n_\top)$ (using $\FN = n_\top - \TP$) and
applying \Cref{thm:delta-method} to $h(t, f) = 2t/(t + f + n_\top)$ gives
\[
  \Var[\Fone] \approx
  \frac{4(\bar f + n_\top)^2\sigma_t^2 + 4\bar t^{\,2}\sigma_f^2}{(\bar t + \bar f + n_\top)^4},
\]
differing from the PPV variance only by the $n_\top$ added to the
$\TP$-gradient component.

\begin{example}[$\Fone$ with delta-method CI]
\label{ex:f1-delta}
Take $\fprate = 0.05$, $\fnrate = 0.10$, $\pi = 0.1$, $n = 1000$, so
$n_\top = 100$, $n_\bot = 900$. Then $\Recall = 0.90$ and
\[
  \Precision = \frac{0.1 \cdot 0.9}{0.1 \cdot 0.9 + 0.9 \cdot 0.05}
  = \frac{0.09}{0.135} = \frac{2}{3},
\]
and
\[
  \Fone = \frac{2 \cdot (2/3) \cdot 0.9}{2/3 + 0.9} \approx 0.766.
\]
With $\bar t = 90$, $\bar f = 45$, $\sigma_t^2 = 100 \cdot 0.9 \cdot 0.1 = 9$,
$\sigma_f^2 = 900 \cdot 0.05 \cdot 0.95 = 42.75$, and $s = 90 + 45 + 100 =
235$,
\[
  \Var[\Fone] \approx
  \frac{4 \cdot 145^2 \cdot 9 + 4 \cdot 90^2 \cdot 42.75}{235^4}
  = \frac{756900 + 1385100}{3{,}049{,}800{,}625}
  \approx 7.02 \times 10^{-4},
\]
a standard deviation of $0.0265$, so the $95\%$ interval is $0.766 \pm 1.96
\cdot 0.0265 \approx [0.714, 0.818]$. The interval is narrower than the
analogous PPV interval because the $n_\top$ in the $\Fone$ denominator damps
the response to a change in either count.
\end{example}

\subsection*{Bloom filter PPV under workload}

The prevalence dependence is sharp enough to drive a concrete case. Take a
Bloom filter (\Cref{def:bloom-filter}, \Cref{sec:bloom-from-scratch}) with
$\fnrate = 0$ and $\fprate = 0.01$, deployed on two workloads. At prevalence
$\pi = 0.01$,
\[
  \PPV = \frac{0.01 \cdot 1}{0.01 \cdot 1 + 0.99 \cdot 0.01} = \frac{0.01}{0.0199}
  \approx 0.503,
\]
so half of all positive reports are false: the filter is right on every
$\top$ (by $\fnrate = 0$) but wrong on $1\%$ of the much larger $\bot$
population, and the two roughly match at the column total. At $\pi = 0.5$,
\[
  \PPV = \frac{0.5 \cdot 1}{0.5 \cdot 1 + 0.5 \cdot 0.01} = \frac{0.5}{0.505}
  \approx 0.990.
\]
Same filter, same $(\fprate, \fnrate)$, PPV moving by nearly a factor of two
across workloads. ``The Bloom filter has precision $0.99$'' is uninformative
without the workload; the $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ claim is workload-free.
\Cref{sec:bloom-diagnostics} runs the inverse problem, recovering a PPV
interval from an observed false-positive count at known prevalence.
