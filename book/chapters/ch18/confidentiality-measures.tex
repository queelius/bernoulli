\section{Confidentiality Measures}
\label{sec:confidentiality-measures}

The cipher-map framing of \S\ref{sec:bloom-based} fixed an error
profile $(\fprate, 0)$ for both Bloom-filter-based constructions,
and chapter~17 \S\ref{sec:maximizing-confidentiality} already worked
out the formal definition of confidentiality at fixed error rates
and workload prevalence. This section reads that derivation in the
encrypted-search setting: every measure chapter~7 derived for the
noisy-bit posterior has a leakage interpretation, and the design
problem of trading rate-budget for confidentiality is solved by
choosing $\fprate$ to put the system at the right point on the
chapter~7 curve.

\subsection*{The server's leakage model}

A deployed Bloom-based encrypted-search system gives the server
access to two streams of data. The first is structural: the server
holds the encrypted filter store at all times, and an adversary who
compromises the server can inspect $\mathsf{Enc}(B_i)$ for every $i$
in the per-document construction (or $\mathsf{Enc}(B_w)$ for every
$w$ in the inverted-index construction). The second is operational:
during normal service the server sees query tokens $\tau_w$ arriving
from clients and the corresponding match decisions (which filter
matched which token) as the server resolves each query.

The structural stream is, by construction, opaque: each
$\mathsf{Enc}(B_i)$ is the output of a semantically secure
encryption scheme, and reading bits off the ciphertext reveals
nothing about the underlying plaintext keywords beyond what the
encryption scheme leaks (which, for a well-designed scheme, is
exactly the size of the filter). The operational stream is where the
real analysis lives. Each match decision is a yes/no answer to the
question ``does query $\tau_w$ correspond to a keyword that appears
in document $d_i$?''. The server learns the answer to that question
exactly, because the server is the party that computed it. The
plaintext keyword $w$ remains hidden behind $\tau_w$, and the
plaintext document content remains hidden inside $\mathsf{Enc}(B_i)$,
but the bare fact of the match (and its absence) is what the server
returns to the client and therefore what the server unavoidably
knows.

The leakage analysis is the analysis of what the server can infer
about plaintext keywords and document contents from a stream of
match decisions. Under the Bernoulli model of the cipher-map error,
each match decision is a noisy bit: it is $1$ when the keyword truly
belongs to the document (because the per-document filter inherits
$\fnrate = 0$), and it is $1$ with probability $\fprate$ when the
keyword does not belong (because that is the Bloom filter's residual
false-positive rate). The chapter~7 measures over noisy bits are
therefore the right measures for the server's posterior.

\subsection*{PPV as the confidentiality measure}

Fix a query token $\tau_w$ and a document $d_i$ in whose encrypted
filter the server's match test succeeded. The server's posterior
probability that the underlying keyword $w$ truly appears in $d_i$
is, by Bayes' rule,
\[
  \Pr[\,w \in d_i \,\mid\, \tau_w \text{ matched } \mathsf{Enc}(B_i)\,]
  \;=\; \frac{\pi (1 - \fnrate)}
              {\pi (1 - \fnrate) + (1 - \pi)\,\fprate},
\]
where $\pi$ is the prior probability that $w \in d_i$ (the workload
prevalence: the share of queried keywords that truly match the
queried document at all). The right-hand side is exactly the
positive predictive value of \Cref{def:ppv}, computed at the
cipher-map's error rates and the workload's prevalence. The
chapter~7 formula and the cipher-map's posterior are not similar
quantities: they are the same quantity, derived twice.

Following chapter~17 \S\ref{sec:maximizing-confidentiality}, the
confidentiality of a match decision is the complement:
\[
  C \;=\; 1 - \PPV
  \;=\; \frac{(1 - \pi)\,\fprate}
              {\pi (1 - \fnrate) + (1 - \pi)\,\fprate}.
\]
$C$ is high when most matches are spurious (the server's evidence
points unreliably at the plaintext); $C$ is low when most matches
are genuine (the server's evidence points reliably at the plaintext).
At $\fnrate = 0$, which both Bloom constructions satisfy, the
formula simplifies to
\[
  C \;=\; \frac{(1 - \pi)\,\fprate}
              {\pi + (1 - \pi)\,\fprate}.
\]
This is the single most useful equation in the chapter. It says
that, on the perfect-filter line, confidentiality is a monotone
function of the rate-prevalence product: as $(1 - \pi)\fprate$ grows
relative to $\pi$, the share of match decisions that protect the
plaintext rises toward $1$. As $(1 - \pi)\fprate$ shrinks toward $0$,
the share collapses to zero and the server's matches identify the
plaintext with near-certainty.

\subsection*{NPV and F1 round out the picture}

PPV measures the confidentiality of a positive match decision: a
$\top$-report that the server returns and an adversary then reads.
The companion measure for the $\bot$-reports is negative predictive
value (\Cref{def:npv}),
\[
  \NPV \;=\; \frac{(1 - \pi)(1 - \fprate)}
                   {(1 - \pi)(1 - \fprate) + \pi\,\fnrate},
\]
which at $\fnrate = 0$ collapses to $\NPV = 1$: a Bloom-based
encrypted search reports $\bot$ only when the keyword really is
absent (the filter's union analysis from chapter~6 \S6.1 guaranteed
this), so a $\bot$-report is fully reliable and an adversary who
reads one learns the plaintext with certainty in that direction. The
$\fnrate = 0$ regime concentrates all confidentiality on the
$\top$-side; the system has nothing to hide on the $\bot$-side
because there is no ambiguity there.

The combined leakage of a balanced workload is the $\Fone$-score of
\Cref{def:f1}: the harmonic mean of precision (which is PPV) and
recall (which at $\fnrate = 0$ is $1$). At $\fnrate = 0$ the
$\Fone$-score reduces to $2\PPV/(1 + \PPV)$, monotone in PPV.
$\Fone$ is the standard summary when the workload prevalence $\pi$
is not known in advance, and the deployed encrypted-search system
faces a mix of $\top$- and $\bot$-decisions whose proportions vary
across keywords. The combined leakage budget of the workload is
bounded by $\Fone$ in the same way the per-decision leakage of a
positive match is bounded by PPV.

The chapter~7 \S\ref{sec:derived-measures} apparatus thus carries
over without modification. Every derived measure chapter~7 defined
for the noisy bit has a confidentiality reading in the
encrypted-search setting: the precision is the positive-match
leakage, the recall is the corpus-coverage of the response, and
$\Fone$ is the balanced leakage budget. The Bernoulli framework
gives the encrypted-search designer the same toolkit it gives the
classifier designer.

\subsection*{Multi-query leakage}

A single query leaks a bounded amount of information about a single
$(w, d_i)$ pair. A long sequence of queries leaks information about
the whole corpus, and the accumulation analysis is governed by the
chapter~4 \S\ref{sec:kronecker} Kronecker structure that chapter~17
\S\ref{sec:trapdoor-paradigm} already used for trapdoor cipher maps.

\begin{proposition}[Multi-query leakage scaling]
\label{prop:multi-query-leakage}
Suppose a client issues $m$ independent queries against a
Bloom-filter-based encrypted-search system with per-query error
profile $(\fprate, 0)$ and workload prevalence $\pi$. Under the
chapter~4 independence axiom, the server's posterior factorizes as
the $m$-fold Kronecker product of single-query posteriors. The
total raw leakage (mutual information between the query stream and
the plaintext) grows linearly in $m$:
\[
  I_m \;=\; m \cdot I_1,
\]
where $I_1$ is the per-query mutual information. The
\emph{confidentiality} of the worst plaintext bit, measured as the
number of bits of uncertainty about a single $(w, d_i)$ pair after
$m$ queries, shrinks as
\[
  C_m \;=\; \Theta(m^{-1/2})
\]
in bits, by standard concentration around the posterior mean.
\end{proposition}

The two scalings are not contradictory; they read the same
phenomenon from opposite sides. The first scaling says the server's
total information about the corpus grows linearly with the query
budget. The second says the confidentiality of any single plaintext
fact (measured in bits of uncertainty the server has about it)
decays as the square root of the query budget. The asymmetry comes
from the structure of the posterior: the central limit theorem
sharpens the server's estimate of the posterior mean at rate
$m^{-1/2}$, and the confidentiality bits are the (negative)
logarithm of the posterior gap.

Two operational implications follow. First, a system designed for a
fixed query budget $m$ can be analyzed by treating $m$ as a
parameter and reading off the corresponding $C_m$. Second, a system
designed for an unbounded budget cannot have non-trivial
confidentiality in the long run: either the trapdoor key has to be
rotated periodically (the rotation resets the accumulated leakage),
or the system has to be designed for a regime where the server's
asymptotic posterior is itself acceptable. Both options are real
deployment choices, and the framework makes the trade-off explicit
rather than hiding it behind a security definition.

\subsection*{A worked example}

Take the per-document Bloom construction of
\Cref{def:per-document-bloom} at $\fprate = 0.01$, with workload
prevalence $\pi = 0.1$ (one queried keyword in ten actually appears
in the queried document, a typical figure for keyword-based search
on a topical corpus). The PPV formula at $\fnrate = 0$ gives
\[
  \PPV
  \;=\; \frac{0.1}{0.1 + 0.9 \cdot 0.01}
  \;=\; \frac{0.1}{0.109}
  \;\approx\; 0.917.
\]
Confidentiality at this operating point is $C \approx 0.083$: when
the server reports a match, an adversary reading the report has
about a $91.7\%$ posterior on the underlying keyword truly being
present in the document, and only an $8.3\%$ residual uncertainty.
The system is correctness-tuned and privacy-leaky.

Now move the cipher map to $\fprate = 0.3$, a much looser filter
that returns many spurious matches per query. The PPV becomes
\[
  \PPV
  \;=\; \frac{0.1}{0.1 + 0.9 \cdot 0.3}
  \;=\; \frac{0.1}{0.37}
  \;\approx\; 0.270.
\]
Confidentiality jumps to $C \approx 0.730$: the server's matches are
now mostly false positives, an adversary reading a match has only a
$27\%$ posterior on the underlying keyword being present, and the
system protects the plaintext at the cost of a much noisier response
to the client. The client now has to do substantially more
post-filtering work to recover the true match set, but in exchange
the server's matches reveal much less about the plaintext.

The two operating points span the trade-off curve the framework
exposes. Every choice of $\fprate$ at fixed $\pi$ sits on the curve
$C = (1 - \pi)\fprate / [\pi + (1 - \pi)\fprate]$, and the designer
picks the point. There is no free lunch: PPV and confidentiality
sum to $1$, and the framework's space, accuracy, and
confidentiality are coupled through chapter~8's entropy bound and
chapter~7's posterior in a way that prevents the designer from
improving any one of the three without paying somewhere else. What
the framework provides is the equation that prices the trade-off.

For intermediate operating points, the curve passes through
$\fprate = 0.05$ at $\PPV \approx 0.690$ ($C \approx 0.310$),
through $\fprate = 0.1$ at $\PPV \approx 0.526$ ($C \approx 0.474$),
and through $\fprate = 0.2$ at $\PPV \approx 0.357$ ($C \approx
0.643$). The numerical pattern is consistent with the geometric
reading of the chapter~17 perfect-filter line: moving away from
$\fprate = 0$ in single percentage-point steps buys confidentiality
in roughly equal increments early, and in diminishing increments as
the system approaches the trivial $\fprate = 1$ endpoint. The
designer's job is to read the workload's $\pi$ from operational
data, pick a target confidentiality, and solve the curve in
closed form for the corresponding $\fprate^*$. The closed-form
solution is direct: rearranging $C = (1 - \pi)\fprate^* /
[\pi + (1 - \pi)\fprate^*]$ for $\fprate^*$ gives $\fprate^* =
\pi C / [(1 - \pi)(1 - C)]$, so a deployment that requires $C =
0.9$ at $\pi = 0.1$ needs $\fprate^* = 0.1 \cdot 0.9 / (0.9 \cdot
0.1) = 1$, which is degenerate. The same target at $\pi = 0.01$
needs $\fprate^* \approx 0.091$, a much friendlier operating
point. Lower-prevalence workloads buy confidentiality more cheaply,
which matches the chapter~17 intuition that confidentiality is
abundant in the regime where the prior already concentrates the
plaintext mass on $\bot$. The next section addresses the harder
version of the problem: when the workload is adversarial and $\pi$
is itself a quantity to be estimated under uncertainty.
