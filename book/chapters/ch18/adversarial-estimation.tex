\section{Estimation under Adversarial Queries}
\label{sec:adversarial-estimation}

\Cref{sec:confidentiality-measures} priced confidentiality as a
function of three parameters: the cipher map's $\fprate$ and
$\fnrate$, and the workload prevalence $\pi$. The first two are
design choices fixed at deployment time; the third is a property of
how the system is used in the wild. A defender who runs the system
in production and wants to know its actual leakage has to estimate
$\pi$ (and through it, the PPV and the confidentiality margin) from
live query traffic. The estimation problem looks elementary at first:
each query either matches a document or it does not, the matches are
Bernoulli, and the chapter~7 binomial machinery should price the
estimate with a confidence interval to taste.

The complication is the source of the queries. A benign workload
sampled iid from a fixed distribution would be the textbook setting
of \Cref{sec:binomial-estimator}, and the chapter~7 Wilson and
Clopper-Pearson intervals of \Cref{sec:confidence-intervals} would
deliver a defensible CI in one line. An adversarial workload, in
which the attacker chooses each query to maximize leakage and
adapts to the system's responses, is not iid. It is not even
stationary. Each query the attacker issues is informed by every
prior response, and the per-query match probability the defender
observes is a function of the attacker's strategy, not a fixed
parameter. Standard iid estimation gives a confidence interval, but
the interval is for the wrong quantity: it bounds the empirical
match rate the defender has just measured, not the worst-case rate
the attacker can sustain.

\subsection*{Why iid estimation fails on adversarial traffic}

A binomial estimator $\hat\pi = (\text{matches})/(\text{queries})$
gives a consistent estimate of $\pi$ under the iid assumption: each
query is a Bernoulli trial with fixed success probability $\pi$, and
the central limit theorem delivers a normal approximation with width
$O(n^{-1/2})$. The Wilson and Clopper-Pearson CIs of
\Cref{sec:confidence-intervals} turn the estimate into a defensible
interval at any specified confidence level $1 - \delta$.

Under an adversarial workload the iid assumption breaks at the
first step. The attacker is free to query a high-prevalence keyword
on every trial, or to alternate between high- and low-prevalence
keywords in whatever pattern best exploits the server's response
structure. The empirical match rate the defender measures is the
average over the attacker's chosen mix; the iid CI bounds that
average; the CI does not bound the leakage of any single keyword
the attacker is actually interested in.

The chapter~16 boundary section (\Cref{sec:adversarial-inputs})
catalogued this failure mode in the abstract. The encrypted-search
defender needs the same warning read constructively: the iid CI
is not just imprecise on adversarial traffic, it is estimating the
wrong quantity, and substituting it into the confidentiality
formula returns a number the defender should not act on.

\subsection*{Multi-armed bandit framing}

The right framing borrows from the bandit literature. Treat each
distinct query (or, more carefully, each distinct keyword the
attacker might query) as an arm in a multi-armed bandit. Each pull
of an arm is one query against the deployed system, and the reward
is the leakage that query produces (in the framework, the per-query
confidentiality drop $1 - \PPV$ for the matched keyword). The
defender's goal is not to maximize reward, as in a standard bandit
problem, but to bound the worst arm's reward: the defender wants a
high-confidence statement that no keyword in the vocabulary
exceeds a target leakage budget, with confidence $1 - \delta$ over
all arms simultaneously.

The upper confidence bound (UCB) family of bandit algorithms maps
directly onto this problem. The defender maintains a per-arm
estimate $\hat\pi_w$ and a per-arm uncertainty bound, and the UCB
quantity $\hat\pi_w + \sqrt{2 \log(1/\delta)/n_w}$ is a
high-confidence upper bound on the true per-arm rate. The bound is
\emph{adversarially valid}: it holds simultaneously over all arms
and over all attacker strategies, because the UCB formula does not
assume the attacker's arm-selection rule is iid. The defender takes
the maximum of the per-arm UCBs as the worst-case bound and feeds
that into the confidentiality formula.

Thompson sampling is the Bayesian counterpart. Each arm carries a
$\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_w, \beta_w)$ posterior over its match rate;
the defender samples one rate per arm from the corresponding
posteriors and reports the maximum as the worst-case estimate. The
two algorithms agree on the qualitative claim: the worst arm is
what the defender has to bound.

\subsection*{CI construction under adversarial sampling}

The chapter~7 Wilson and Clopper-Pearson CIs adapt to the bandit
setting by a union bound over arms. Suppose the vocabulary has $K$
arms and the defender wants confidence $1 - \delta$ that no arm's
true rate exceeds the reported bound. Computing each per-arm CI at
confidence $1 - \delta/K$ and taking the maximum gives a valid
joint bound at $1 - \delta$, by the standard Bonferroni union
inequality. The price of the union bound is a $\log K$ factor in
the per-arm CI width: at vocabulary size $K = 10^5$ and joint
confidence $1 - \delta = 0.95$, the per-arm CI is tightened by
roughly $\log(2 \times 10^6) \approx 14.5$ standard deviations
relative to the $\delta = 0.05$ marginal interval.

A tighter alternative is what \textcite{towell-es-conf-mab}
developed for the encrypted-search setting specifically. The
construction observes that the defender is not estimating $K$
independent quantities but a single quantity (the worst-case
confidentiality) defined as the maximum of $K$ rate parameters,
and applies an order-statistic-aware bound that is tighter than
the naive union bound when the rates are concentrated. The
construction is a moving-average bootstrap: the defender resamples
the empirical match trace with a moving window and reports the
worst-window rate as the bound, with the bootstrap quantile
playing the role of the CI's tail probability. The bound is
narrower than the union-bound CI at the same joint confidence
when the per-arm rates are similar, and it degenerates to the
union-bound CI when one arm dominates the rest.

The operational reading is straightforward. The defender chooses
the CI construction based on what is known about the attacker's
strategy. Under a worst-case assumption (any keyword in the
vocabulary could be the target), the union-bound CI is correct.
Under a working assumption that the attacker concentrates on a
small number of keywords (a common case when the attacker is
exploiting a specific user's interest), the bootstrap CI is
tighter and the defender can report a sharper bound.

\subsection*{Connection to chapter~11 Chernoff and chapter~16
adversarial inputs}

The chapter~11 multiplicative Chernoff bound of
\Cref{thm:chernoff} sharpened the iid CI to exponential decay in
the deviation from the true rate. It is the right bound for an
iid workload; it is the wrong bound for an adversarial workload
exactly because its derivation invokes the iid moment generating
function, which depends on the attacker's arm-selection rule being
state-independent. The MAB UCB bound replaces the iid MGF with an
adversarial concentration argument that survives state-dependent
arm selection. The two bounds agree numerically on iid traffic
(the UCB constant $\sqrt{2 \log(1/\delta)}$ matches the Chernoff
exponent for moderate $\delta$) and diverge on adversarial
traffic, where Chernoff under-bounds the true rate and UCB
over-bounds it correctly.

The chapter~16 \Cref{sec:adversarial-inputs} discussion of where
the framework breaks under adversarial inputs reads here as a
positive recipe: the framework still applies, but the estimator
that turns observations into rate estimates has to be chosen for
the actual input model. The Bernoulli model itself is correct
(the per-query match indicator is still Bernoulli, conditionally
on the queried keyword); what changes is the estimator that
aggregates the per-query indicators into a confidentiality
bound.

\subsection*{A worked example}

Consider a defender monitoring a deployed encrypted-search system
at 100 queries per minute. Over one hour the system processes
$n = 6000$ queries. The defender observes 540 matches across the
hour, for an overall empirical match rate $\hat\pi = 0.09$. The
defender wants to report a $95\%$ upper bound on the
confidentiality leakage at this operating point.

\emph{Naive iid Chernoff approach.} Treat the 6000 queries as iid
Bernoulli with success probability $\pi$, and apply the chapter~11
Chernoff bound at $\delta = 0.05$. The Wilson CI at the same
confidence level gives roughly $\hat\pi \pm 1.96 \cdot
\sqrt{0.09 \cdot 0.91 / 6000} \approx 0.09 \pm 0.0072$, i.e.
$\pi \in [0.083, 0.097]$ at $95\%$ confidence. Substituting the
upper-end $\pi^{\mathrm{iid}}_{\max} = 0.097$ into the
confidentiality formula (with $\fprate = 0.01, \fnrate = 0$) gives
$\PPV \approx 0.915$, $C \approx 0.085$. The defender concludes
that confidentiality is at least $C \approx 0.085$ at the operating
point.

\emph{MAB UCB approach.} Suppose the vocabulary has $K = 1000$
keywords actively queried over the hour. The union-bounded per-arm
CI at joint confidence $1 - \delta = 0.95$ uses
$\delta_{\mathrm{arm}} = 0.05/1000 = 5 \times 10^{-5}$. Each arm
sees on average $n_w = 6$ queries, with substantial variance: some
arms see hundreds of queries, others see only one. For an arm with
$n_w = 6$ and $\hat\pi_w = 0.5$ (3 matches out of 6), the
Clopper-Pearson upper bound at $\delta_{\mathrm{arm}} = 5 \times
10^{-5}$ is roughly $\pi_w^{\mathrm{UCB}} \approx 0.95$, because
the per-arm sample size is small and the joint-confidence
correction further inflates the bound. Taking the maximum across
arms, the defender's bound on the worst-case per-keyword rate is
$\pi^{\mathrm{MAB}}_{\max} \approx 0.95$, substituting which gives
$\PPV \approx 0.999$ and $C \approx 0.001$.

The two bounds differ by nearly two orders of magnitude. The iid
bound reports $C \ge 0.085$; the MAB UCB bound reports $C \ge
0.001$. Both bounds are correct statements about their respective
quantities, and only the MAB bound answers the question the
defender actually has: how leaky is the worst-case keyword the
attacker might target?

The closure is the same warning the chapter has been issuing
throughout. A framework guarantee is only as strong as the
estimator that produced its parameters. The Bernoulli machinery is
not at fault; the iid estimator misapplied to adversarial data is.
