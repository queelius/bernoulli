\section{Closing Part III}
\label{sec:closing-part3}

Three parts of the book are now on the table. Part~I built the atom:
a noisy bit, $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$, characterized by a $2 \times 2$
channel matrix and the two rates $(\fprate, \fnrate)$. Part~II
specialized the atom one direction, to predicates on a universe, and
worked out the algebra of approximate sets: union, intersection, and
complement under the rate calculations of chapter~6, the
classification measures of chapter~7, the information-theoretic floor
of chapter~8. Part~III specialized the atom a second direction, to
maps and types: \Cref{ch:approximate-functions} lifted the binary
channel to $k \times k$ channels for finite codomains;
\Cref{ch:sketches-estimators} did the same for the noisy counters and
cardinality estimators of streaming algorithms; chapter~11 did the
same for randomized deciders; \Cref{ch:approximate-relations} did the
same for predicates on pairs; and the present chapter unified the
four into one type-driven algebra.

Each part lifts the previous via a more general type structure. Part~I
fixes the codomain to $\BoolType$ and looks at the atom. Part~II
fixes the codomain to $\BoolType$ and looks at predicates on a
universe, that is, functions $T \to \BoolType$. Part~III releases
both the codomain and the domain to range over arbitrary algebraic
types built from $\BoolType$ and the four type constructors. The
codomain widens from $\BoolType$ to $\mathbb{N}$, to $[0, 1]$, to
sums, to lists, to products of these, and the domain widens from a
single universe to products, sums, lists, and function spaces. At each
widening the rate calculations of the earlier parts carry through,
specialized by the new type's structure. The two rates of Part~I
become the channel matrix of Part~III; the union and intersection of
Part~II become the rows of the table in \Cref{sec:composition-functor}.

The full type system that emerged is the one promised in chapter~1.
Part~I's $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ is the base case. Chapter~9 made the
first inductive step: any finite type $T$ admits a $\Bernoulli{T}$
characterized by a $|T| \times |T|$ channel matrix. The remaining
chapters of Part~III worked out four type constructors that close
this $\Bernoulli{-}$ operation under composition. Products go to
Kronecker products of channels; sums go to direct sums; lists go to
per-position lifts; functions go to per-input lifts. Chapter~13
states these four rules as one structure-preserving map and closes
the algebra: every approximate structure built from $\BoolType$ and
the four constructors is one application of $\Bernoulli{-}$, with
composition determined by the type's structural decomposition.

Three further parts remain. Part~IV gives the constructive payoff
that Part~II's lower bound left open.
\Cref{ch:optimal-bernoulli-sets} constructs the Bernoulli Hash
Function, an approximate set that achieves the chapter~8 entropy
floor asymptotically; the construction abandons the shared bit array
of the standard Bloom design in favor of a perfect-hash-based
fingerprint encoding. \Cref{ch:perfect-hash-filters} constructs the
other one-sided axis, the FPR-zero family of perfect hash filters,
which give exact membership at the cost of a controlled
false-negative rate. Together the two chapters answer the
constructive question that Part~II's floor poses: which structures
achieve the lower bound, and how?

Part~V draws the boundary and turns the algebra to cryptographic use.
\Cref{ch:not-bernoulli} catalogues the cases in which the
independent-error model breaks down: correlated errors across
queries, rates that drift in time, adversarial query distributions
that defeat the uniform-sampling assumption underlying FPR
estimation. Chapters~17 and~18 then read the algebra in the opposite
direction: cipher maps and encrypted search use the controlled error
of a Bernoulli map as a cryptographic resource rather than a
deployment constraint. The salt of a Bernoulli hash function plays
the role of a key, the acceptance predicate plays the role of a
cipher map, and the chapter~8 entropy floor governs both the space
cost and the security budget.

With Part~III closed the algebra is in place. The next two parts
build on it.
