\section*{Bibliographic Notes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Bibliographic Notes}
\label{sec:bib-notes-ch13}

\paragraph{The type-driven algebra as a manuscript.}
The four-row table of \Cref{sec:composition-functor} and the
type-by-type lift of \Cref{sec:bernoulli-of-composite} are drawn from
\textcite{towell-bernoulli-data-type}, ``Random approximate values
over algebraic types,'' the manuscript that worked out the algebraic
data type of $\Bernoulli{T}$ and the four composition rules over
products, sums, lists, and functions. The manuscript treats
$\Bernoulli{-}$ as a type-level operation with its own algebra, gives
the channel-matrix factorizations explicitly, and develops the
function-row monad structure at greater length than the chapter
allows. A reader who wants the type-algebra treatment as primary,
with the channel-matrix derivations of chapters~4 and~9 as derived
consequences of the type structure, should treat the manuscript as
the next reading. The book takes the opposite route, starting with
the channel matrix and reading the type algebra off it; the two
presentations agree on the four rows.

\paragraph{Algebraic data types in programming-language theory.}
The four-constructor view of types as products, sums, lists, and
functions is the standard algebraic-data-type framing from
programming-language theory. It is the type system of ML, Haskell,
and the functional descendants of Hindley-Milner; the chapter assumes
the reader has seen pairs, tagged unions, and parametrically
polymorphic lists before. \textcite{hudak-1989}'s survey
``Conception, Evolution, and Application of Functional Programming
Languages'' is a readable history of how this type system grew out of
the lambda calculus and Church's simply typed terms, and it
introduces the algebraic-data-type idiom to readers coming from
imperative or object-oriented backgrounds. The book uses the type
constructors without their language-implementation baggage; readers
who want to see them as features of a working language should read
the Haskell or OCaml documentation alongside the chapter.

\paragraph{The categorical reading.}
The structure-preserving language of \Cref{sec:composition-functor}
is informal category theory. The formal name for a map between two
collections of objects-with-operations that preserves the operations
is a \emph{functor}, and the four-row table is the statement that
$\Bernoulli{-}$ is a functor from the category of algebraic types
(with type constructors) to a category of noisy channels (with
Kronecker product, direct sum, list, and exponentiation). The
function-row identity $\Bernoulli{T \to U} \cong T \to \Bernoulli{U}$
is the statement that $\Bernoulli{-}$ is, more specifically, a monad
in the categorical sense; the chapter mentioned this in passing
without checking the monad axioms.
\textcite{maclane-categories}'s ``Categories for the Working
Mathematician'' is the standard reference for the categorical
machinery: functors in chapter~I, natural transformations in
chapter~II, monads in chapter~VI. \textcite{wadler-1992}'s ``The
Essence of Functional Programming'' translates the categorical monad
into the programming-language idiom and is the reference for the
$T \to \Bernoulli{U}$ reading we used.

\paragraph{Where to go for more.}
The chapter's algebra is one application of the algebraic-data-type
machinery; the same machinery applies to many other lifts. Probability
monads in the categorical sense were studied by Giry and Lawvere in
the 1980s and have re-emerged in the functional-programming
literature as ``probabilistic programming'' DSLs. The Bernoulli
lift is one such monad, with the specific property that its
channel-level representation is a finite stochastic matrix and its
composition rules are computable in closed form. A reader who wants
the broader categorical picture of probability and approximation
should follow the references in
\textcite{towell-bernoulli-data-type}; the book stops at the
four-row table because that is the level the practitioner needs.
