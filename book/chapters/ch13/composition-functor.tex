\section{The Composition Functor}
\label{sec:composition-functor}

§13.2 catalogued the lift $\Bernoulli{-}$ on each of the four type
constructors. Four rules, one per constructor. This section observes
that the four rules belong together: they are the four cases of one
underlying lift. Naming and using that lift is the chapter's
headline payoff.

\subsection*{A structure-preserving map}

Pick any operation on types: product, sum, list, function. The lift
$\Bernoulli{-}$ takes that operation on the type level to an
analogous operation on the noisy-channel level. For products,
\Cref{thm:bernoulli-product} said $\Bernoulli{T \times U} \cong
\Bernoulli{T} \otimes \Bernoulli{U}$: the product on types becomes a
Kronecker product on channels. For sums, $\Bernoulli{T + U} \cong
\Bernoulli{T} \oplus \Bernoulli{U}$: the sum on types becomes a
direct sum on channels. For lists and functions, the pattern repeats
with each constructor's natural channel-level operation.

A map between two settings that takes operations to corresponding
operations is what we call a \emph{structure-preserving map}.
$\Bernoulli{-}$ is such a map from the world of algebraic types to
the world of noisy channels: it takes each type constructor to a
specific channel-level operation, and it does so consistently across
the four constructors. The formal categorical name for this kind of
map is ``functor,'' a term §13.5 returns to. The prose name is
enough for the chapter.

\subsection*{The isomorphism table}

The four cases collected into one table:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Type level & Channel level & Reading \\
\hline
$T \times U$ & $\Bernoulli{T} \otimes \Bernoulli{U}$ & Kronecker product \\
$T + U$ & $\Bernoulli{T} \oplus \Bernoulli{U}$ & direct sum (block-diagonal) \\
$L T$ & $L \Bernoulli{T}$ (modulo length) & per-position lift \\
$T \to U$ & $T \to \Bernoulli{U}$ & per-input lift \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each row is an isomorphism: the type on the left and the channel on
the right specify the same $\Bernoulli{-}$ instance up to relabeling
of indices. The four rows together state that $\Bernoulli{-}$
commutes with the four type constructors.

The function row $\Bernoulli{T \to U} \cong T \to \Bernoulli{U}$ is
worth pausing on. It says the noisy version of a function is the
same as a function returning noisy values. Programmers will recognize
this as the signature of a ``monad'' in the type-theoretic sense:
a wrapper type $M$ together with operations that let computations
return $M$-values be sequenced. $\Bernoulli{-}$ qualifies as a monad
in this sense, with $T \to \Bernoulli{U}$ as the basic
noise-introducing function shape. We mention this here for readers
fluent in programming-language type theory; the formal monad axioms
are checked in exercises.

\subsection*{Why the table matters operationally}

Without the table, every new approximate structure required its own
composition derivation. Bloom filters got chapter~6; sketches got
chapter~10; relations got chapter~12. Each chapter rederived a
composition rule that was, in retrospect, an instance of the same
underlying structure-preserving map.

With the table, composition of $\Bernoulli{-}$ instances is
mechanical. Given a composite type $T$ built from the four
constructors, the channel-level structure of $\Bernoulli{T}$ is
determined by decomposing $T$ into constructors and applying the
appropriate row of the table at each level. No new derivation is
needed. The four rows handle every composite type the four
constructors generate.

The practical consequence is that adding a new approximate data
structure to the catalogue is a matter of identifying its type, not
of redoing the algebra. A new sketch that estimates entries of a
matrix? That is $\Bernoulli{T \times U \to \mathbb{R}}$: a product of
two index types feeding into a real-valued function. The composition
rule for this sketch is determined by the table, not by a fresh
derivation. A new probabilistic data structure for sparse vectors?
That is $\Bernoulli{T \to \mathbb{R}}$ with $T$ a sparse index set:
the function-row rule applies, and the per-input noise propagates
through any downstream computation.

This is what we mean by an algebra of approximate types. The book's
chapters did the work of deriving each instance because the unified
view was easier to motivate after the instances were on the table.
Now that the table is in place, every future approximate structure
inherits its composition rules.

\subsection*{Examples from prior chapters as instances}

The structures we have built so far each instantiate the table.

\textbf{Chapter~6 set composition.} An approximate set is a noisy
predicate $\Bernoulli{T \to \BoolType}$. The function row of the
table gives the composition rule: a noisy predicate is one
$\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ per input. Composing two such predicates by
AND or OR or XOR is then the chapter~3 noisy-bit composition applied
pointwise across inputs. The chapter~6 results that took several
sections to derive are a special case of the function row,
specialized to codomain $\BoolType$ and read pointwise.

\textbf{Chapter~10 CountMin.} A CountMin sketch is a
$\Bernoulli{\mathbb{N}}$ per key, hence a function
$T \to \Bernoulli{\mathbb{N}}$ where $T$ is the universe of keys. By
the function row of the table this is the same as
$\Bernoulli{T \to \mathbb{N}}$. CountMin's well-known property
``estimates are additive across independent sketches'' is the
$\mathbb{N}$-arithmetic analogue of the product-row Kronecker
factorization applied to the codomain. The chapter~10 derivation of
CountMin's composition rules is the function-and-product-row
specialization at $T \to \mathbb{N}$.

\textbf{Chapter~12 approximate relations.} A relation $R \subseteq
T \times U$ is a function $T \times U \to \BoolType$. An approximate
relation is $\Bernoulli{T \times U \to \BoolType}$. The function row
gives the per-input lift; the product row inside the domain factors
the index space as $T \times U$. The join-rate calculations of §12.2
combine the predicate-noise of two relations via the function-row
rule and propagate the result across the product-row domain
structure. Both rows of the table contribute, in the natural order
their composite type calls for them.

\textbf{Chapter~9 n-ary channels.} The n-ary channel $(X, Y, Q)$ is
the building block: a noisy function $X \to Y$ on finite $X$ and $Y$.
Every other example in the list is built on top of this one. The
$X \to Y$ shape is the function row of the table; the finiteness of
$X$ and $Y$ keeps the parameter count manageable. Sets, sketches, and
relations are all special cases of this structure, distinguished by
their choice of codomain and by what additional structure the domain
carries.

\subsection*{The Bernoulli framework as one thing}

The lesson of the chapter is that there is one underlying
mathematical object across the book. The atomic case is the noisy
bit of Part~I, $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$. Every other approximate
structure is a $\Bernoulli{T}$ for some algebraic type $T$ built from
$\BoolType$ and the four type constructors. The composition rules
that organized the earlier chapters are all special cases of the
four-row table above.

The book's title was ``an algebra of approximate computation.'' That
title is now justified. The algebra is the algebra of the type
constructors; the lift $\Bernoulli{-}$ is the algebra's action on the
approximate-computation side. Every approximate calculation in the
preceding twelve chapters and every approximate calculation in the
five chapters ahead can be read as an instance of one type-driven
algebra.

The remaining chapters of the book do not introduce new structural
machinery. They specialize the algebra to specific constructive
problems: chapter~14 builds Bloom filters that achieve the entropy
floor of chapter~8; chapter~15 does the same for perfect-hash
filters; chapter~16 catalogues the cases where the algebra's
independence assumptions break down; chapters~17 and~18 turn the
algebra to cryptographic uses. Each of these chapters is a
specialized application of one of the rows of the table.
