\section{The Asymmetric Binary Channel}
\label{sec:asymmetric-channel}

\subsection*{Lifting the symmetry constraint}

The Binary Symmetric Channel of \Cref{sec:bsc} is a useful starting
point, but its defining property---equal error rates for both
inputs---is rarely satisfied in practice. A spam filter flags
legitimate mail as spam far more often than it lets genuine spam
through. A Bloom filter returns false positives but never false
negatives. A medical screening test is deliberately tuned to
minimize one kind of error at the cost of the other.

These systems are not defective BSCs; they are different channels
altogether. When we allow the false-positive rate $\fprate$ and the
false-negative rate $\fnrate$ to be independent parameters, we obtain
the \emph{asymmetric binary channel}: the same two-element input and
output domains, the same row-stochastic channel matrix $Q$, but no
constraint tying $\fprate$ to $\fnrate$.

\begin{definition}[General asymmetric binary channel]
\label{def:asymmetric-channel}
An \emph{asymmetric binary channel} with false-positive rate $\fprate
\in [0,1)$ and false-negative rate $\fnrate \in [0,1)$ is a random
map $\phi : \{\top, \bot\} \to \{\top, \bot\}$ satisfying
\begin{align*}
  \Pr\{\phi(\top) \neq \top\} &= \fnrate, \\
  \Pr\{\phi(\bot) \neq \bot\} &= \fprate.
\end{align*}
The channel transition matrix is
\[
  Q
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \tprate  & \fnrate \\
    \fprate  & \tnrate
  \end{pmatrix}
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \fnrate & \fnrate \\
    \fprate     & 1 - \fprate
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
where row 1 corresponds to input block $B_1 = \{\top\}$ (positives),
row 2 to block $B_2 = \{\bot\}$ (negatives), and columns to output
labels $\top$ (left) and $\bot$ (right), following the convention
established in \Cref{sec:bsc}.
\end{definition}

Two special cases are immediately visible in the matrix. Setting
$\fnrate = \fprate$ recovers the BSC of \Cref{def:bsc}: the two rows
become identical up to a reflection, and the single crossover
parameter $\fprate$ is enough to describe the channel. Setting
$\fnrate = 0$ makes row 1 become $(1, 0)$: a positive input is
always reported correctly. This is the \emph{Z-channel}, the subject
of the next subsection. The name of each special case and its
connection to a familiar system is spelled out in full in
\Cref{sec:model-order}.

\subsection*{The Z-channel: one-sided error}

The most widely encountered asymmetric channel in computer science is
the \emph{Z-channel}. In the variant used here, the transmission
$\top \to \top$ is perfect, and the only error is $\bot \to \top$: a
negative input incorrectly reported as positive.\footnote{Information-theory
texts often define the Z-channel with the opposite asymmetry---false
negatives only, no false positives ($\omega > 0$, $\varepsilon = 0$),
so that $\bot \to \bot$ is perfect and the only error is $\top \to
\bot$. Both variants are equivalent up to relabeling of inputs and
outputs. The convention here aligns with the Bloom filter and other
positive approximate sets, where the false-positive direction is the
natural error mode.}

\begin{definition}[Z-channel]
\label{def:z-channel}
A \emph{Z-channel} with false-positive rate $\fprate \in (0,1)$ is
an asymmetric binary channel with $\fnrate = 0$. Its channel matrix is
\[
  Q_Z
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1       & 0 \\
    \fprate & 1 - \fprate
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
\end{definition}

The Z-channel has a sharp asymmetry: false negatives are impossible
($\fnrate = 0$), while false positives occur with probability
$\fprate$. This makes an output of $\bot$ fully reliable---it cannot
be a mistake---while an output of $\top$ is uncertain.

\paragraph{Bloom filters as Z-channels.}
The Bloom filter is the canonical Z-channel instance from data
structures. A Bloom filter answers membership queries for a set $S$:
given an element $x$, it returns either \emph{present} ($\top$) or
\emph{absent} ($\bot$). The filter is constructed so that
\[
  x \in S \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{Bloom returns } \top \quad
  \text{(no false negatives)},
\]
while a non-member $x \notin S$ may still trigger $\top$ with some
probability $\fprate$ (a false positive). In the channel-matrix
language, the Bloom filter is exactly a Z-channel: $\fnrate = 0$ by
construction, $\fprate$ controlled by the filter's bit-width and hash
count. When the Bloom filter returns absent ($\bot$), the answer is
certain; when it returns present ($\top$), the answer is probabilistic.
This semantic asymmetry---reliable ``no'', uncertain ``yes''---is the
hallmark of all Z-channel systems.

\subsection*{The biased coin, revisited}

\Cref{sec:three-faces} introduced the biased coin as one framing of
the noisy bit. In \Cref{sec:bsc} we identified it as a BSC: a coin
with the same flip probability for heads and tails. But a coin can be
\emph{doubly biased}: the probability of landing heads on a flip from
heads may differ from the probability of landing tails on a flip from
tails. Such a coin is not a BSC; it is an asymmetric binary channel.

Concretely: let $\top$ represent heads and $\bot$ represent tails.
Suppose a flipped-heads coin shows tails with probability $\fnrate$,
and a flipped-tails coin shows heads with probability $\fprate$, where
$\fnrate \neq \fprate$. The channel matrix for this coin is exactly
$Q$ from \Cref{def:asymmetric-channel}. The BSC of \Cref{sec:bsc}
was the special case $\fnrate = \fprate$, where the double bias
collapses to a single bias. The asymmetric channel is the general case
that was always there, waiting for $\fnrate$ and $\fprate$ to differ.

Physical examples are easy to construct. A coin that spins on a
surface comes to rest heads or tails with probabilities that depend on
its initial face. A weighted die, approximated as a two-sided object,
has different effective flip rates for each face. A market that can
only move a small amount in one direction (a price floor or ceiling)
is an asymmetric channel on the direction of movement. In every case,
the natural description is an asymmetric binary channel with two
independent parameters $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$.

\subsection*{Medical tests as asymmetric channels}

Medical screening tests illustrate the asymmetric channel in a domain
where the two error types have very different consequences. A test for
a serious disease might be deliberately calibrated to err on the side
of sensitivity: it flags nearly every true case ($\fnrate$ small) at
the cost of a higher false-alarm rate ($\fprate$ larger). A
confirmatory test later in the diagnostic pipeline does the opposite:
it trades sensitivity for specificity ($\fprate$ small, $\fnrate$
possibly larger).

In both cases, the test is fully described by the matrix $Q$ from
\Cref{def:asymmetric-channel}. The \emph{screening test} has small
$\fnrate$ and larger $\fprate$; the \emph{confirmatory test} has
small $\fprate$ and larger $\fnrate$. Neither is a BSC. The
channel-matrix representation makes the trade-off visible at a glance:
the top row (positives) and bottom row (negatives) can be tuned
independently by adjusting the decision threshold.

This is precisely the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve
familiar from binary classification: as the threshold moves, $(\fprate,
\fnrate)$ traces a curve in the unit square $[0,1]^2$, and each point
on the curve is a distinct asymmetric binary channel.

\subsection*{Worked example: $\fprate = 0.05$, $\fnrate = 0.10$}

To make the asymmetric channel concrete, we work through the channel
matrix and several derived quantities for $\fprate = 0.05$ and
$\fnrate = 0.10$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The matrix.}
\[
  Q
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \fnrate & \fnrate \\
    \fprate     & 1 - \fprate
  \end{pmatrix}
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0.90 & 0.10 \\
    0.05 & 0.95
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Row sums.}
Row 1: $0.90 + 0.10 = 1.00$. Row 2: $0.05 + 0.95 = 1.00$. Both
rows sum to 1, confirming that $Q$ is row-stochastic.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Reading the matrix.}
A positive input ($\top$) is correctly reported as positive with
probability $0.90$ (true-positive rate $\tprate = 1 - \fnrate$) and
incorrectly reported as negative with probability $0.10$ (false-negative
rate $\fnrate$). A negative input ($\bot$) is incorrectly reported as
positive with probability $0.05$ (false-positive rate $\fprate$) and
correctly reported as negative with probability $0.95$ (true-negative
rate $\tnrate = 1 - \fprate$). The two error rates are distinct:
$\fnrate = 0.10 \neq 0.05 = \fprate$. This channel is not a BSC.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Error-rate asymmetry.}
The ratio of the two error rates is $\fnrate / \fprate = 0.10 /
0.05 = 2$: false negatives are twice as likely as false positives.
For the BSC with $\fprate = \fnrate = 0.075$ (the mean of the two
rates), the matrix would be
\[
  Q_{\mathrm{BSC}}
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0.925 & 0.075 \\
    0.075 & 0.925
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
which has identical off-diagonal entries. Comparing this with the
asymmetric $Q$ above makes the structural difference plain: the
off-diagonal entries $0.10$ and $0.05$ are a factor of 2 apart.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Joint probabilities under a uniform prior.}
If the input is drawn uniformly from $\{\top, \bot\}$ (prior
probability $1/2$ for each), the joint probabilities of each
input-output pair are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cc|l}
           & output $\top$ & output $\bot$ & total \\
\hline
input $\top$ & $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.90 = 0.450$ & $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.10 = 0.050$ & $0.500$ \\
input $\bot$ & $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.05 = 0.025$ & $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.95 = 0.475$ & $0.500$ \\
\hline
total        & $0.475$                             & $0.525$                             & $1.000$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Accuracy.}
Under the uniform prior, accuracy is the sum of the diagonal joint
probabilities: $0.450 + 0.475 = 0.925$. This is the average of
$\tprate = 0.90$ and $\tnrate = 0.95$: accuracy $= \frac{1}{2}(0.90
+ 0.95) = 0.925$. Compare this with the BSC above: the BSC would
achieve accuracy $0.925$ as well, but by having both error rates at
$0.075$ rather than one at $0.05$ and one at $0.10$. The asymmetric
channel and this particular BSC have the same accuracy under a uniform
prior, but their error structures are different---a point that matters
when the prior is not uniform or when the two error types carry
different costs.

\subsection*{The asymmetric channel as the general order-2 case}

The BSC has one free parameter ($\fprate$, with the constraint
$\fprate = \fnrate$ reducing the two-parameter family to one). The
asymmetric binary channel has two free parameters ($\fprate$ and
$\fnrate$, with no constraint between them). \Cref{sec:model-order}
formalizes what ``order 2'' means: the Boolean input domain $\{\top,
\bot\}$ partitions into two blocks, $B_1 = \{\top\}$ and $B_2 = \{
\bot\}$, each with its own per-block error rate. The asymmetric
channel assigns $\fnrate$ to $B_1$ and $\fprate$ to $B_2$
independently; the BSC additionally forces $\fnrate = \fprate$.

In this sense, the BSC is not a different type of channel from the
asymmetric channel: it is the \emph{symmetric special case} of the
same order-2 structure. The constraint $\fnrate = \fprate$ reduces
the number of free parameters from 2 to 1 without changing the
partition of the input domain.

The distinction between model order (the number of partition blocks)
and parameter count (how many numbers are needed to describe the
channel) is a recurring theme. \Cref{sec:model-order} makes it
precise and proves the Boolean ceiling theorem: because the Boolean
domain has exactly two elements, any binary channel has model order
at most 2. There is no room for a more fine-grained partition, no
matter how many free parameters the channel has.

\subsection*{Looking ahead}

The Z-channel and general asymmetric channel are the natural habitat
of most real-world approximate Boolean systems. In
\Cref{sec:channel-composition} we compose two binary channels---which
may each be symmetric, asymmetric, or Z-channel---via matrix
multiplication and read off the resulting channel's error rates
directly from the product. The asymmetric channel's two-parameter
freedom makes the composition richer than the BSC case, and the
matrix formalism handles both cases uniformly.
