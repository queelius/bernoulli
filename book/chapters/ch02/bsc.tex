\section{The Binary Symmetric Channel}
\label{sec:bsc}

The three framings of \Cref{sec:three-faces} share a common structure, and the
binary symmetric channel is that structure stripped to its essentials: a single
Boolean input, a single Boolean output, and one crossover probability that the
output disagrees with the input.

\begin{definition}[Binary Symmetric Channel]
\label{def:bsc}
A \emph{binary symmetric channel} (BSC) with crossover probability $\fprate \in
(0, 1)$ is a random map $\phi : \{\top, \bot\} \to \{\top, \bot\}$ with
\[
  \Pr\{\phi(x) \neq x\} \;=\; \fprate
  \quad \text{for every } x \in \{\top, \bot\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The defining property is that the crossover is the same for both inputs:
$\Pr\{\phi(\top) \neq \top\} = \Pr\{\phi(\bot) \neq \bot\} = \fprate$, so a
single parameter plays the role of both the false-positive and the
false-negative rate, $\fprate = \fnrate$. The symmetry is a genuine constraint,
and most real systems lack it: a spam filter flags legitimate mail far more
often than it leaks spam. The BSC is the clean starting point precisely because
it isolates bit-flip noise from any question of which direction is noisier; the
asymmetric generalization is \Cref{sec:asymmetric-channel}.

\subsection*{The channel matrix}

We record a binary channel as a $2 \times 2$ row-stochastic matrix $Q$, the
\emph{channel matrix}. The convention, fixed for the rest of the book: rows are
indexed by the input block, row 1 for $B_1 = \{\top\}$ (positives) and row 2
for $B_2 = \{\bot\}$ (negatives); columns by the output label, $\top$ then
$\bot$; and the $(i,j)$ entry is the probability of output $j$ given an input in
block $B_i$. The four entries name the confusion-table rates, so the general
order-2 channel and the BSC special case are
\[
  Q \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \tprate & \fnrate \\
    \fprate & \tnrate
  \end{pmatrix}
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \fnrate & \fnrate \\
    \fprate & 1 - \fprate
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  Q_{\mathrm{BSC}} \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \fprate & \fprate \\
    \fprate & 1 - \fprate
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The BSC matrix is \emph{circulant}: its two rows are permutations of each
other, the matrix expression of symmetry. At $\fprate = 0.05$, for instance, a
positive input reads back correctly with probability $0.95$ and flips with
probability $0.05$, and likewise for a negative input; under a uniform input
prior the marginal error rate is exactly $\fprate$, independent of the prior
because the channel is symmetric.

\subsection*{Capacity}

The BSC's \emph{capacity}, the maximum mutual information $I(X; Y)$ over input
priors, is
\[
  C \;=\; 1 - H_2(\fprate),
  \qquad
  H_2(\fprate) = -\fprate \log_2 \fprate - (1 - \fprate)\log_2(1 - \fprate),
\]
attained at the uniform prior $p = 1/2$. The derivation is short: $H(Y \mid X) =
H_2(\fprate)$ for every prior, while $H(Y)$ is maximized at $p = 1/2$, so
$C = \max_p H(Y) - H_2(\fprate) = 1 - H_2(\fprate)$. Capacity is one bit per use
at $\fprate = 0$ (a perfect channel) and zero at $\fprate = 1/2$, where the
output is independent of the input. The $\fprate = 1/2$ point is the
zero-information case met in \Cref{sec:quantifying-noise}; for the BSC it
coincides with the symmetric crossover, whereas for an asymmetric channel the
zero-information locus is $\fprate + \fnrate = 1$.

\subsection*{The three faces in matrix form}

The framings of \Cref{sec:three-faces} are the same matrix $Q_{\mathrm{BSC}}$
with different labels. The biased coin is a coin with one flip probability
$\fprate$ for both faces; the noisy line is Shannon's original BSC
\cite{shannon-1948}, a bit flipped independently with probability $\fprate$;
the classifier with equal false-positive and false-negative rates is a BSC,
though real classifiers rarely satisfy $\fprate = \fnrate$ and are better
modeled by the asymmetric channel of \Cref{sec:asymmetric-channel}. Composing a
BSC with itself makes matters worse, not better: two crossover-$\fprate$
channels in series give crossover $2\fprate(1 - \fprate) > \fprate$ for
$0 < \fprate < 1/2$, derived in \Cref{sec:channel-composition}.

\subsection*{Model order of the BSC}

The source literature uses two phrases for the BSC, ``first-order'' and ``the
symmetric special case of the order-2 model,'' and they refer to different
things. Model order, formalized in \Cref{sec:model-order}, counts the blocks in
the partition of the input domain that groups inputs sharing a common error
rate. For the BSC both inputs share the crossover $\fprate$, so they form a
single block and the BSC has \emph{model order 1}; the asymmetric channel, with
$\fprate \neq \fnrate$, keeps them in separate blocks at model order 2. Calling
the BSC ``the symmetric special case of the order-2 model'' refers not to its
order but to the family it is embedded in: one may always write the BSC with
the two-block discrete partition and impose $\fprate = \fnrate$, the working
frame for chapters 2 through 4. The BSC is genuinely model order 1, studied as a
constrained member of the order-2 family; \Cref{sec:model-order} separates model
order from parameter count and proves that no binary channel exceeds order 2.

Throughout, $\fprate$ is the false-positive rate, $\fnrate$ the false-negative
rate, with $\tprate = 1 - \fnrate$ and $\tnrate = 1 - \fprate$ the
complementary rates, and for the BSC the single crossover $\fprate = \fnrate$.
The source papers sometimes pair (FPR, TPR) rather than (FPR, FNR); since
$\mathrm{TPR} = 1 - \mathrm{FNR}$ the choice is cosmetic, and this book uses
$(\fprate, \fnrate)$.
