\section{Composition of Channels}
\label{sec:channel-composition}

\subsection*{Channels in series}

The channels we have studied so far---the BSC of \Cref{sec:bsc} and
the asymmetric binary channel of \Cref{sec:asymmetric-channel}---model
a single noisy step. Many real systems chain two or more such steps
together. A signal passes through a first amplifier that introduces
some noise, then through a second that adds more. A classification
pipeline applies one model to filter candidates and a second to rank
them. A Bloom filter checks membership; a downstream system then
applies its own fallible test to the reported positives.

In each case we have a \emph{serial pipeline}: an element enters the
first channel $Q_1$, its output becomes the input to the second channel
$Q_2$, and the combined system produces a final output. We want to know:
what is the channel matrix of the composed system?

\begin{definition}[Serial composition of binary channels]
\label{def:channel-composition}
Let $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ be two binary channel matrices. The \emph{serial
composition} $Q_2 \circ Q_1$ is the channel formed by applying $Q_1$
first and $Q_2$ second. Its channel matrix is the ordinary matrix
product
\[
  Q_{\mathrm{total}} \;=\; Q_2\, Q_1.
\]
The $(i,j)$-entry of $Q_{\mathrm{total}}$ is the probability of
observing final output $j$ given that the original input belongs to
block $B_i$.
\end{definition}

The right-to-left convention $Q_2 Q_1$ is standard: in matrix notation,
the \emph{rightmost} factor is applied first. The input vector (or row)
is transformed by $Q_1$, and the intermediate output is then transformed
by $Q_2$. The law of total probability is what makes the product work:

\[
  (Q_{\mathrm{total}})_{ij}
  \;=\;
  \sum_{k} (Q_1)_{ik}\,(Q_2)_{kj}.
\]

Reading this sum: $(Q_1)_{ik}$ is the probability that the first channel
maps input block $B_i$ to intermediate output $k$; $(Q_2)_{kj}$ is the
probability that the second channel maps intermediate input $k$ to final
output $j$. Summing over all intermediate values $k$ gives the total
probability that $B_i$ produces final output $j$, which is exactly the
$(i,j)$-entry of the composed channel.

\subsection*{Worked example: composing two BSCs}

The Binary Symmetric Channel of \Cref{sec:bsc} is the cleanest test
case for composition. We show that composing two BSCs always yields a
third BSC, and we derive the composed crossover probability explicitly.

Let $Q_1$ be a BSC with crossover $\fprate_1$ and $Q_2$ be a BSC with
crossover $\fprate_2$:
\[
  Q_1 \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \fprate_1 & \fprate_1 \\
    \fprate_1 & 1 - \fprate_1
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  Q_2 \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \fprate_2 & \fprate_2 \\
    \fprate_2 & 1 - \fprate_2
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]

We compute $Q_{\mathrm{total}} = Q_2 Q_1$ directly:
\begin{align*}
  Q_2 Q_1
  &=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1-\fprate_2 & \fprate_2 \\
    \fprate_2   & 1-\fprate_2
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1-\fprate_1 & \fprate_1 \\
    \fprate_1   & 1-\fprate_1
  \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
  &=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    (1-\fprate_2)(1-\fprate_1) + \fprate_2 \fprate_1
      & (1-\fprate_2)\fprate_1 + \fprate_2(1-\fprate_1) \\
    \fprate_2(1-\fprate_1) + (1-\fprate_2)\fprate_1
      & \fprate_2 \fprate_1 + (1-\fprate_2)(1-\fprate_1)
  \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align*}

Observe that the top-left and bottom-right entries are equal, and the
top-right and bottom-left entries are equal. That is precisely the
structure of a BSC matrix. Denote the off-diagonal entry as
$\fprate_{\mathrm{total}}$:
\begin{align}
  \fprate_{\mathrm{total}}
  &= (1-\fprate_2)\fprate_1 + \fprate_2(1-\fprate_1) \notag \\
  &= \fprate_1 - \fprate_1\fprate_2 + \fprate_2 - \fprate_1\fprate_2 \notag \\
  &= \fprate_1 + \fprate_2 - 2\fprate_1\fprate_2.
  \label{eq:bsc-composition}
\end{align}

So the composed channel is
\[
  Q_{\mathrm{total}}
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 - \fprate_{\mathrm{total}} & \fprate_{\mathrm{total}} \\
    \fprate_{\mathrm{total}}     & 1 - \fprate_{\mathrm{total}}
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
a BSC with crossover
\[
  \fprate_{\mathrm{total}}
  \;=\;
  \fprate_1(1-\fprate_2) + (1-\fprate_1)\fprate_2.
\]
This is the formula stated in \Cref{def:channel-composition}: two BSCs
in series produce a third BSC.

\subsection*{Reading the composition formula}

The formula $\fprate_{\mathrm{total}} = \fprate_1(1-\fprate_2) +
(1-\fprate_1)\fprate_2$ has a direct probabilistic interpretation. An
error in the composed channel occurs if and only if \emph{exactly one}
of the two channels flips its input. There are two ways this can happen:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The first channel flips (probability $\fprate_1$) and the second
    does not (probability $1 - \fprate_2$), contributing $\fprate_1(1-\fprate_2)$.
  \item The first channel does not flip (probability $1 - \fprate_1$) and
    the second does (probability $\fprate_2$), contributing $(1-\fprate_1)\fprate_2$.
\end{itemize}

If both channels flip, the two errors cancel: the input is back to its
original value. If neither flips, the input passes through unchanged.
The composed error is therefore the XOR of the two individual errors,
and the probability of the XOR of two independent Bernoulli trials with
parameters $\fprate_1$ and $\fprate_2$ is exactly
$\fprate_1(1-\fprate_2) + (1-\fprate_1)\fprate_2$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Two channels make things worse.}
For $0 < \fprate_1, \fprate_2 < 1/2$, we have
\[
  \fprate_{\mathrm{total}}
  = \fprate_1 + \fprate_2 - 2\fprate_1\fprate_2
  > \fprate_1.
\]
Adding a second channel in series strictly increases the crossover
probability (unless one of the channels is perfect, $\fprate = 0$, or
one is a maximal-error channel, $\fprate = 1$). Two imperfect BSCs in
series are noisier than either alone. The only way to reduce the
crossover in a serial pipeline is to make the intermediate channel
a \emph{corrector}, which is outside the binary-channel model we study
here.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Numerical example.}
Take $\fprate_1 = 0.05$ and $\fprate_2 = 0.10$. Then
\[
  \fprate_{\mathrm{total}}
  = 0.05 \cdot 0.90 + 0.95 \cdot 0.10
  = 0.045 + 0.095
  = 0.140.
\]

The composed channel has crossover $0.14$, strictly larger than either
$0.05$ or $0.10$. As a matrix:
\[
  Q_{\mathrm{total}}
  \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0.86 & 0.14 \\
    0.14 & 0.86
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both rows sum to 1 and the off-diagonal entries are equal---the composed
channel is indeed a BSC.

\subsection*{General asymmetric channels compose by the same rule}

The BSC is the symmetric special case. The general asymmetric binary
channel of \Cref{def:asymmetric-channel} follows the same rule: the
composed channel matrix is the matrix product $Q_2 Q_1$, and the
composed error rates follow from the law of total probability exactly
as above.

Let $Q_1$ have false-positive rate $\fprate_1$ and false-negative
rate $\fnrate_1$, and let $Q_2$ have $\fprate_2$ and $\fnrate_2$:
\[
  Q_1 =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1-\fnrate_1 & \fnrate_1 \\
    \fprate_1   & 1-\fprate_1
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  Q_2 =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1-\fnrate_2 & \fnrate_2 \\
    \fprate_2   & 1-\fprate_2
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The composed matrix $Q_2 Q_1$ yields combined rates (reading off the
$(1,2)$-entry for the false-negative rate and the $(2,1)$-entry for the
false-positive rate):
\begin{align*}
  \fnrate_{\mathrm{total}} &= (1-\fnrate_2)\fnrate_1 + \fnrate_2 \fprate_1, \\
  \fprate_{\mathrm{total}} &= (1-\fprate_2)\fprate_1 + \fprate_2(1-\fnrate_1).
\end{align*}

This is Theorem~3.7 from the companion paper \cite{towell-bernoulli-sets},
stated there in terms of the approximate-identity function composition
$\widetilde{\mathrm{id}}_{\fprate,\tprate} \circ \widetilde{\mathrm{id}}_{\fprate',\tprate'}$.
The matrix-product derivation above is the channel-matrix reformulation
of the same result.

Note that the composition of two asymmetric channels is \emph{not}
generally a BSC even when the individual channels happen to have equal
overall crossover rates: the asymmetry in $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ generally
produces different false-positive and false-negative rates in $Q_2 Q_1$.
The BSC is the special case in which the symmetry $\fprate = \fnrate$
is preserved under composition because both inputs to the formula are
symmetric, which forces $\fprate_{\mathrm{total}} = \fnrate_{\mathrm{total}}$.

\subsection*{Serial composition vs.\ parallel queries}

It is essential to distinguish the \emph{serial} composition of this
section from the \emph{parallel} query structure studied in
\Cref{sec:kronecker}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Serial composition} (this section): an element passes
    through channel $Q_1$, and the \emph{output} of $Q_1$ becomes the
    \emph{input} to channel $Q_2$. The combined system acts on a single
    element, producing a single final output. The joint channel matrix
    is the ordinary matrix product $Q_2 Q_1$, which is a $2 \times 2$
    matrix (the same size as each factor).

  \item \textbf{Parallel queries} (\Cref{sec:kronecker}): $m$
    independent queries are applied to $m$ \emph{distinct} elements,
    each through its own copy of the channel. The combined output is an
    $m$-tuple. The joint channel matrix is the Kronecker product
    $Q_1 \otimes Q_2 \otimes \cdots \otimes Q_m$, which is a
    $2^n \times 2^m$ matrix, growing \emph{exponentially} in $m$.
\end{itemize}

The two compositions are structurally different and answer different
questions. Serial composition (matrix product) asks: what is the
end-to-end error rate when one channel's output feeds another? Parallel
composition (Kronecker product) asks: what is the joint output
distribution when we query $m$ elements simultaneously?

\Cref{thm:kronecker} shows that the Kronecker product structure arises
because Axioms~1 and~2 force independence across parallel queries. The
matrix product of this section arises from the law of total probability
applied to the intermediate state in a serial pipeline. These are
complementary, not competing, structures. When both matter---for
instance, when $k$ parallel pipelines each consist of multiple stages
in series---the combined channel is a Kronecker product of composed
matrices:
\[
  \left(Q_2^{(1)} Q_1^{(1)}\right)
  \otimes
  \left(Q_2^{(2)} Q_1^{(2)}\right)
  \otimes \cdots,
\]
where each parenthesized factor is a serial composition computed by
ordinary matrix multiplication. The Kronecker product is applied
\emph{across} parallel pipelines; the ordinary product is applied
\emph{within} each pipeline.

\subsection*{Composition is associative but not commutative}

An immediate algebraic consequence of the matrix-product interpretation
is that serial channel composition is \emph{associative}: composing
three channels as $(Q_3 Q_2) Q_1$ or $Q_3 (Q_2 Q_1)$ gives the same
result, since matrix multiplication is associative. This means that
chaining any number of binary channels in series is unambiguous: the
order of application matters, but the grouping does not.

Commutativity, however, fails in general. The product $Q_2 Q_1$ need
not equal $Q_1 Q_2$: applying the first channel before the second is
not the same as applying the second before the first, unless the two
channels have some special symmetry.

For the BSC, commutativity \emph{does} hold: two BSC matrices commute
because each is a polynomial in the same circulant permutation matrix.
Concretely, if $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are both BSCs, then $Q_2 Q_1 = Q_1 Q_2$
by the symmetry of the composition formula
$\fprate_{\mathrm{total}} = \fprate_1(1-\fprate_2) +
(1-\fprate_1)\fprate_2$, which is symmetric in $\fprate_1$ and
$\fprate_2$. This symmetry is a property of the BSC's circulant
structure and is \emph{lost} in the asymmetric channel.

Exercise~2.7 constructs an explicit pair of asymmetric channels that do
not commute, illustrating that the order of channels in a serial
pipeline matters outside the BSC special case.

\subsection*{The stationary point}

For a BSC with crossover $\fprate < 1/2$, the composition formula shows
that $\fprate_{\mathrm{total}} = \fprate_1 + \fprate_2 - 2\fprate_1
\fprate_2 > \max(\fprate_1, \fprate_2)$ whenever $0 < \fprate_1,
\fprate_2 < 1/2$. As we chain more and more copies of the same BSC in
series, the crossover approaches the stationary value $1/2$ from below.
At $\fprate = 1/2$, the channel output is statistically independent of
the input: the channel transmits no information. This convergence to
the stationary point mirrors the figure in the companion paper
\cite{towell-bernoulli-sets} (Figure~3.1), which shows both TPR and FPR
converging to the stationary value $\fprate/(\fprate + \fnrate)$ under
repeated composition of the general asymmetric channel.

\subsection*{Looking ahead: two perspectives on the same idea}

Channel composition via matrix product is the channel-theoretic
perspective on a result that chapter 3 will reframe algebraically.
\Cref{sec:composition-theorem} will show that composing $k$ approximate
identity channels in series produces a composed false-positive rate
(and, symmetrically, a composed hit rate $\eta_{\mathrm{total}}$)
satisfying
\[
  \eta_{\mathrm{total}} \;=\; 1 - \prod_{i=1}^k (1 - \eta_i),
\]
where $\eta_i$ is the hit rate of the $i$-th stage. This is the same
calculation as the matrix product, viewed through the Boolean-algebra
lens: the composition theorem in chapter 3 is the algebraic restatement
of what \Cref{def:channel-composition} computes.

At the other end of the formalism, \Cref{sec:kronecker} and
\Cref{thm:kronecker} give the \emph{parallel} analog: $m$ independent
channels queried on $m$ distinct elements produce a joint confusion
matrix that is the Kronecker product $Q_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes Q_m$.
The Kronecker product is the structure forced by Axioms~1 and~2; the
matrix product of this section needs neither axiom, only the law of
total probability.

Understanding which composition is relevant---serial (matrix product)
or parallel (Kronecker product)---is one of the key skills this book
develops. The corollary \Cref{cor:kronecker-unconditional} gives the
parallel-query factorization; the formula of this section gives the
serial-pipeline composition. Both structures appear throughout Parts~II
and~III.
