\section{Composition of Channels}
\label{sec:channel-composition}

Many systems chain noisy steps: an amplifier feeds another, a filter feeds a
ranker. If an element passes through $Q_1$ and its output enters $Q_2$, the
end-to-end channel is the matrix product.

\begin{definition}[Serial composition of binary channels]
\label{def:channel-composition}
For binary channel matrices $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, the \emph{serial composition}
applying $Q_1$ then $Q_2$ has channel matrix $Q_{\mathrm{total}} = Q_2 Q_1$,
with $(Q_{\mathrm{total}})_{ij} = \sum_k (Q_1)_{ik}(Q_2)_{kj}$.
\end{definition}

The sum is the law of total probability over the intermediate value $k$:
$(Q_1)_{ik}$ carries block $B_i$ to intermediate output $k$, and $(Q_2)_{kj}$
carries $k$ to final output $j$. The right-to-left order $Q_2 Q_1$ is the usual
matrix convention, rightmost factor first.

\subsection*{Composing two BSCs}

Two BSCs in series produce a third. Multiplying circulant matrices preserves
the circulant form, and the off-diagonal entry of $Q_2 Q_1$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bsc-composition}
  \fprate_{\mathrm{total}}
  \;=\; \fprate_1(1 - \fprate_2) + (1 - \fprate_1)\fprate_2
  \;=\; \fprate_1 + \fprate_2 - 2\fprate_1\fprate_2.
\end{equation}
The composed error is the exclusive-or of the two individual flips: the input is
corrupted exactly when one channel flips and the other does not (if both flip,
the errors cancel). For $0 < \fprate_1, \fprate_2 < 1/2$ this exceeds both
operands, so two imperfect symmetric channels in series are noisier than either
alone; at $\fprate_1 = 0.05$, $\fprate_2 = 0.10$ the composed crossover is
$0.14$. Chaining copies of one BSC drives the crossover toward the stationary
$1/2$, where the channel carries no information.

\subsection*{Composing asymmetric channels}

The general rule is the same matrix product. For $Q_1, Q_2$ with rates
$(\fprate_i, \fnrate_i)$, the $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ entries of $Q_2 Q_1$ give
\[
  \fnrate_{\mathrm{total}} = (1 - \fnrate_2)\fnrate_1 + \fnrate_2 \fprate_1,
  \qquad
  \fprate_{\mathrm{total}} = (1 - \fprate_2)\fprate_1 + \fprate_2(1 - \fnrate_1),
\]
the channel-matrix form of the rate-propagation theorem of
\textcite{towell-bernoulli-sets}. Composition of asymmetric channels is not
generally symmetric: the BSC is the special case in which symmetry is preserved
because both factors are symmetric, forcing $\fprate_{\mathrm{total}} =
\fnrate_{\mathrm{total}}$.

\subsection*{Serial versus parallel}

Serial composition must not be confused with the parallel query structure of
\Cref{sec:kronecker}. Serial composition feeds one element's output into the
next channel and yields a $2 \times 2$ matrix, the ordinary product $Q_2 Q_1$.
Parallel querying applies $m$ independent channels to $m$ distinct elements and
yields a $2^m \times 2^m$ joint matrix, the Kronecker product $Q_1 \otimes
\cdots \otimes Q_m$, which \Cref{thm:kronecker} shows is forced by the
independence axioms. The two answer different questions, end-to-end error of a
pipeline versus joint distribution of simultaneous queries, and combine when
$k$ multi-stage pipelines run in parallel: the Kronecker product across
pipelines, the ordinary product within each. The serial product needs only the
law of total probability; the Kronecker product needs the axioms
(\Cref{cor:kronecker-unconditional}).

\subsection*{Algebraic properties}

Serial composition is associative, since matrix multiplication is, so a chain of
channels groups unambiguously. It is not commutative in general: $Q_2 Q_1 \neq
Q_1 Q_2$ unless the factors share a symmetry. Two BSCs do commute, because
\eqref{eq:bsc-composition} is symmetric in $\fprate_1$ and $\fprate_2$; a BSC
and an asymmetric channel generally do not, as a direct computation of $Q_2 Q_1$
and $Q_1 Q_2$ shows, so the order of stages in a mixed pipeline matters.

\Cref{sec:composition-theorem} reframes the serial result algebraically: the
hit rate of $k$ composed one-sided stages satisfies $\eta_{\mathrm{total}} = 1 -
\prod_{i=1}^k (1 - \eta_i)$, the Boolean-algebra restatement of the matrix
product computed here.
