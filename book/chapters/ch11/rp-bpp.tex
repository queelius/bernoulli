\section{RP, BPP, and Bernoulli Type}
\label{sec:rp-bpp}

The Monte Carlo regime of \Cref{def:monte-carlo} is the regime in which a
single coin sequence may produce a wrong answer with bounded probability.
Once one accepts that contract, the obvious next question is which decision
problems admit a polynomial-time algorithm of that shape, and how the
admissible shapes carve up by where the error is allowed to land. The
answer is the family of randomized complexity classes RP, coRP, ZPP, and
BPP. This section states each one in its standard form, then reads it back
through the Part~II vocabulary: a language is in RP exactly when its
decision algorithm sits on the FNR-zero axis of the perfect filter line of
\Cref{def:perfect-filter-line}; coRP is the FPR-zero axis; ZPP is the
origin; BPP is the interior of the rate plane below a fixed bound. The
correspondence is not an analogy. The complexity-class definitions and the
chapter~6 perfect-filter-line classification are the same axes seen from
two angles.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{RP: one-sided error in the false-positive direction}

\begin{definition}[Randomized Polynomial Time, RP]
\label{def:rp}
A language $L \subseteq \{0,1\}^*$ is in the class \emph{RP} when there
exists a polynomial-time randomized algorithm $A$ and a polynomial $p$
such that on input $x \in \{0,1\}^*$, $A$ reads at most $p(|x|)$ random
bits, runs in time at most $p(|x|)$, and satisfies
\begin{align*}
  x \in L      &\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \Pr[A(x) = 1] \;\ge\; 1/2,\\
  x \notin L   &\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \Pr[A(x) = 1] \;=\; 0.
\end{align*}
The probability is over $A$'s coin flips alone; $x$ is fixed in both lines.
\end{definition}

Read the two lines through the $2 \times 2$ channel of
\Cref{def:bernoulli-bool}, with $A(x) = 1$ identified with the report
\textsc{in-$L$} and the truth value $\mathbf{1}_{x \in L}$ identified with
the predicate $P(x)$. The second line is the statement that the algorithm
never reports \textsc{in-$L$} on an input that is not in $L$:
\[
  \fprate \;=\; \Pr[A(x) = 1 \mid x \notin L] \;=\; 0.
\]
The first line is the statement that on inputs that are in $L$, the
algorithm reports \textsc{in-$L$} with probability at least $1/2$, which
is the false-negative bound
\[
  \fnrate \;=\; \Pr[A(x) = 0 \mid x \in L] \;\le\; 1/2.
\]
An RP algorithm is, channel-for-channel, a $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$
instance with $\fprate = 0$ and $\fnrate \le 1/2$. The class RP is the
complexity-class incarnation of the FNR-zero axis of the chapter~6
perfect filter line, polynomially constrained and polynomially advised.
Where chapter~5 \S5.4 named the Bloom filter as the canonical FNR-zero
approximate set, RP plays the same role for decision algorithms: a
witness seen is a witness believed, and the only thing that can go wrong
is failing to see the witness when one exists. The constant $1/2$ in the
first line is conventional; any constant in $(0, 1)$ would give the same
class by amplification, a point we return to below.

The canonical RP problem is \textsc{Non-Zero Multivariate Polynomial},
which asks, given an arithmetic circuit computing a polynomial $f$ over a
finite field, whether $f$ is not the zero polynomial. The
Schwartz-Zippel-DeMillo-Lipton lemma says that if $f$ is not zero, a
uniformly random assignment of values to its variables produces a non-zero
output with probability at least $1 - d/|F|$, where $d$ is the total
degree and $|F|$ the field size. If $f$ is the zero polynomial, every
assignment produces zero. Reporting \textsc{non-zero} when the random
assignment evaluates to non-zero is exactly the RP shape: silent on zero
polynomials (no false positives), occasionally wrong on non-zero
polynomials (false negatives at rate $\le d/|F|$).

%---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{coRP and ZPP: the mirror axis and the origin}

\begin{definition}[Co-Randomized Polynomial Time, coRP]
\label{def:corp}
A language $L$ is in \emph{coRP} when its complement $\bar L$ is in RP.
Equivalently, there exists a polynomial-time randomized algorithm $A$
with
\begin{align*}
  x \in L      &\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \Pr[A(x) = 1] \;=\; 1,\\
  x \notin L   &\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \Pr[A(x) = 1] \;\le\; 1/2.
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

The two lines are the mirror image of \Cref{def:rp}. Reading them as a
channel, $\fnrate = 0$ and $\fprate \le 1/2$. A coRP algorithm is a
$\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance on the FPR-zero axis of the perfect
filter line: an answer of \textsc{not-in-$L$} is always correct; the only
error mode is reporting \textsc{in-$L$} when the input is not in $L$.
Miller-Rabin (\Cref{thm:miller-rabin-formal}) is the canonical coRP
algorithm for the language \textsc{Composites}: the algorithm reports
\textsc{composite} only with a witness in hand, and that report is always
correct; the failure mode is reporting \textsc{prime} on a composite
input. Equivalently, the language \textsc{Primes} is in RP via the same
algorithm with output negated, and historically this was the textbook
example of a non-trivial RP language until the discovery of the
deterministic polynomial-time AKS primality test in 2002.

\begin{definition}[Zero-error Probabilistic Polynomial Time, ZPP]
\label{def:zpp}
A language $L$ is in \emph{ZPP} when $L \in \mathrm{RP} \cap \mathrm{coRP}$.
Equivalently, there exists an expected-polynomial-time Las Vegas algorithm
(\Cref{def:las-vegas}) for $L$: an algorithm that always returns the
correct answer and whose expected runtime is polynomial in $|x|$.
\end{definition}

The equivalence is the Las Vegas / Monte Carlo conversion of \S11.2.
Given an RP algorithm $A_+$ and a coRP algorithm $A_-$ for the same
language $L$, run them in parallel on input $x$ until one of them
produces a definite answer. $A_+$ produces a definite \textsc{in-$L$} on
every coin sequence that triggers its acceptance branch; $A_-$ produces a
definite \textsc{not-in-$L$} on every coin sequence that triggers its
rejection branch. On every input, at least one of the two algorithms is
allowed to be definite on a random fraction $\ge 1/2$ of its coin
sequences (the fraction depends on whether $x \in L$ or not). The
expected number of parallel rounds to obtain a definite answer is
therefore bounded by a constant, and the overall runtime is expected
polynomial. Conversely, an expected-polynomial-time Las Vegas algorithm
gives an RP algorithm by the truncation trick of \S11.2 (run for a
constant multiple of the expected time, report \textsc{not-in-$L$} on
timeout) and a coRP algorithm by the opposite truncation. ZPP is the
complexity-class incarnation of the origin of the rate plane: $\fprate =
\fnrate = 0$, exact decisions, paid for in runtime tail.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{BPP: the interior of the rate plane}

\begin{definition}[Bounded-error Probabilistic Polynomial Time, BPP]
\label{def:bpp}
A language $L$ is in \emph{BPP} when there exists a polynomial-time
randomized algorithm $A$ such that
\begin{align*}
  x \in L      &\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \Pr[A(x) = 1] \;\ge\; 2/3,\\
  x \notin L   &\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \Pr[A(x) = 1] \;\le\; 1/3.
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

The two lines are now both bounded away from $1/2$ on the right side, but
neither is forced to $0$ or $1$. Read as a channel, $\fnrate \le 1/3$ and
$\fprate \le 1/3$, so the BPP algorithm sits in the interior of the rate
plane, off both axes of the perfect filter line. The constants $2/3$ and
$1/3$ are conventional; any pair $(p, q)$ with $p > 1/2 > q$ gives the
same class by amplification (\Cref{thm:bpp-amplification} below; the
proof is deferred to \Cref{sec:sample-complexity}).

The Part~II vocabulary applies directly. At workload prevalence $\pi =
\Pr[x \in L]$ (which depends on the input distribution the algorithm is
deployed against, not on the algorithm itself), the positive predictive
value of a BPP algorithm is given by \Cref{def:ppv}:
\[
  \PPV
  \;=\; \frac{\pi (1 - \fnrate)}{\pi (1 - \fnrate) + (1 - \pi) \fprate}
  \;\ge\; \frac{\pi \cdot 2/3}{\pi \cdot 2/3 + (1 - \pi) \cdot 1/3}
  \;=\; \frac{2 \pi}{2 \pi + (1 - \pi)}
  \;=\; \frac{2 \pi}{\pi + 1}.
\]
The expression looks innocent until one substitutes a small $\pi$. At
$\pi = 0.01$ the bound gives $\PPV \ge 0.0198 / 1.01 \approx 0.0196$: the
algorithm's report of \textsc{in-$L$} is correct only about $2\%$ of the
time, despite the algorithm's $2/3$-versus-$1/3$ accuracy on each
distribution. The other $98\%$ of positive reports are false alarms. The
negative predictive value of \Cref{def:npv} similarly depends on the
workload. The two definitions are not artifacts of medical statistics;
they are the unavoidable book-keeping for any decision algorithm whose
per-class accuracy is bounded away from $1$, including a generic BPP
algorithm. A BPP algorithm with a $1/3$ error rate looks impressive in
isolation and unimpressive at low prevalence, and \Cref{def:ppv} is the
calculation that explains why.

The conventional gap $2/3$ versus $1/3$ exists for exactly this reason:
it forces a non-trivial signal in both directions, so that amplification
can drive both errors to zero rather than only one. A class defined with
the gap closed (both probabilities equal to $1/2$ on the respective
inputs) is the class PP, which is much larger than BPP and is not
believed to admit efficient amplification.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Amplification: error reduction by repetition}

The constants in the definitions of RP, coRP, and BPP look arbitrary,
because they are. Any constant strictly inside $(0, 1)$ for RP, and any
constant gap around $1/2$ for BPP, gives the same class. The reason is
amplification: $k$ independent repetitions plus the appropriate
aggregator drive the error rate to zero exponentially in $k$.

\begin{theorem}[RP amplification]
\label{thm:rp-amplification}
Let $A$ be an RP algorithm for $L$ with $\fnrate \le 1/2$ per run. Define
$A^{(k)}$ to be $k$ independent runs of $A$ on $x$, reporting
\textsc{in-$L$} when any run reports \textsc{in-$L$} and
\textsc{not-in-$L$} otherwise. Then $A^{(k)}$ is a polynomial-time RP
algorithm for $L$ with $\fnrate \le 2^{-k}$ and $\fprate = 0$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is the chapter~3 composition theorem (\Cref{thm:composition})
applied to $k$ independent draws from the same Bernoulli predicate, with
the OR aggregator. The $\fprate = 0$ property is preserved by OR (any
\textsc{in-$L$} report must be true), and the $\fnrate$ at the OR is the
product of the per-run $\fnrate$s by independence
(\Cref{ax:element-indep}). Bringing $\fnrate$ from $1/2$ to $2^{-30}$
costs a factor of $30$ in runtime; bringing it to $2^{-60}$ costs a
factor of $60$. This is the perfect-filter-line analog of the $k$-round
Miller-Rabin bound of \Cref{thm:miller-rabin-formal}, with the only
difference being that Miller-Rabin's per-round bound is $1/4$ rather than
$1/2$. coRP amplifies the same way with the AND aggregator, since coRP
is RP on the mirror axis.

\begin{theorem}[BPP amplification]
\label{thm:bpp-amplification}
Let $A$ be a BPP algorithm for $L$ with $\fnrate, \fprate \le 1/3$ per
run. Define $A^{(k)}$ to be $k$ independent runs of $A$ on $x$,
reporting \textsc{in-$L$} when at least $k/2$ runs report
\textsc{in-$L$} and \textsc{not-in-$L$} otherwise. Then $A^{(k)}$ is a
polynomial-time BPP algorithm with $\fnrate, \fprate \le \exp(-\Omega(k))$.
\end{theorem}

The BPP amplification rate is exponential in $k$, but the exponent is no
longer the per-run error rate itself; it is the Chernoff exponent for a
majority of independent Bernoulli draws. The proof rests on the Chernoff
bound (\Cref{thm:chernoff}) applied to the count of majority votes for
the wrong answer; we defer it to \Cref{sec:sample-complexity}, which
develops the Chernoff machinery in the form needed for sample-complexity
calculations across the whole chapter. The qualitative content is what
matters here: BPP error decays exponentially in the number of repetitions
even though the BPP algorithm cannot drive either error to zero on a
single coin sequence. Bringing the per-vote error from $1/3$ to $2^{-30}$
costs $O(k)$ runs with $k = \Theta(30)$, and the implicit constant is
small.

\subsection*{The $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ correspondence at scale}

The four definitions of this section read in the chapter~4 vocabulary as
four shapes in the rate plane:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
\textbf{Class} & $\fprate$ bound & $\fnrate$ bound \\
\hline
RP   & $= 0$       & $\le 1/2$ \\
coRP & $\le 1/2$   & $= 0$ \\
ZPP  & $= 0$       & $= 0$ \\
BPP  & $\le 1/3$   & $\le 1/3$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The first three rows trace out the perfect filter line of
\Cref{def:perfect-filter-line}, with RP and coRP as the two axes and ZPP
as their intersection at the origin. The fourth row, BPP, is the interior
of the rate plane below the constant $1/3$. The chapter~6 classification
of approximate sets by where they sit on the perfect filter line and the
classical taxonomy of randomized complexity classes are the same
classification applied to two different families of objects: arbitrary
$\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instances in chapter~6, polynomially constrained
decision algorithms here. The constraints differ; the geometry of the
rate plane is the same.

This is more than a slogan. The constraint that propagates the most is
amplification. A point on the FNR-zero axis can be slid arbitrarily close
to the origin by independent repetition with OR; the chapter~3
composition theorem says so and \Cref{thm:rp-amplification} is the
complexity-class-specific restatement. A point in the interior can be
slid arbitrarily close to the origin by independent repetition with
majority vote; the Chernoff bound says so and
\Cref{thm:bpp-amplification} is the complexity-class-specific
restatement. The two amplification theorems are special cases of the
same phenomenon: any $\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ instance whose per-trial
error is bounded away from $1/2$ on both inputs can be driven to
negligible error by enough independent repetitions and the right
aggregator. The aggregator depends on which axis or interior region the
instance sits in; the existence of amplification does not.

The connection runs the other way too. When chapter~12 lifts the
$\Bernoulli{\BoolType}$ framework to relations and when chapter~13
unifies all of Part~III via the algebra of approximate types, every
theorem about RP, coRP, ZPP, and BPP will lift along with it. A
randomized algorithm that decides membership in a binary relation $R
\subseteq A \times B$ with $\fprate, \fnrate$ in the BPP regime sits in
a class one might call $\mathrm{BPP}_R$, the relational analog of BPP,
and amplification will work for it for the same reason it works for the
language version. The plumbing the rest of Part~III installs is what
makes that lift mechanical rather than a theorem-by-theorem retread.
