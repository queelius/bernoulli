\section{Sample Complexity}
\label{sec:sample-complexity}

Every algorithm of this chapter has a knob $k$, the round or repetition count,
that drives a per-trial error below a target $\delta$. The sample-complexity
question is how to set it, and the answer rests on one theorem about sums of
independent Bernoulli variables.

\begin{theorem}[Multiplicative Chernoff bound]
\label{thm:chernoff}
For independent $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ with $\Prob{X_i = 1} = p$, $X = \sum_i X_i$,
$\mu = np$, and every $\epsilon \in (0, 1]$,
\[
  \Prob{X \ge (1 + \epsilon)\mu} \le \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\mu\epsilon^2}{3}\right),
  \qquad
  \Prob{X \le (1 - \epsilon)\mu} \le \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\mu\epsilon^2}{2}\right).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $t > 0$, Markov on $e^{tX}$ gives $\Prob{X \ge a} \le e^{-ta}\Expect{e^{tX}}$,
and independence factors the moment-generating function, $\Expect{e^{tX}} =
\prod_i(1 - p + pe^t) = (1 + p(e^t - 1))^n \le \exp(\mu(e^t - 1))$ by $1 + x \le
e^x$. At $a = (1 + \epsilon)\mu$ the exponent $-t(1 + \epsilon)\mu + \mu(e^t - 1)$
is minimized at $t = \ln(1 + \epsilon)$, giving $-\mu[(1 + \epsilon)\ln(1 +
\epsilon) - \epsilon]$, and $(1 + \epsilon)\ln(1 + \epsilon) - \epsilon \ge
\epsilon^2/3$ on $(0,1]$ yields the upper tail. The lower tail follows with $t <
0$ and the matching $\ge \epsilon^2/2$ estimate.
\end{proof}

The upper tail's $\mu\epsilon^2/3$ is looser than the lower tail's
$\mu\epsilon^2/2$ (deviations above the mean are harder against the $\{0,1\}$
ceiling); a two-sided union gives $\Prob{|X - \mu| \ge \epsilon\mu} \le
2\exp(-\mu\epsilon^2/3)$.

\paragraph{Miller-Rabin round count.}
Miller-Rabin sits on the FNR-zero axis ($\fnrate = 0$, $\fprate \le 1/4$), so the
Chernoff bound is not needed: a single fooled round suffices for a false
positive, and the composition theorem reduces $\fprate^{(k)}$ to the product
$\le (1/4)^k$ (\Cref{thm:miller-rabin-formal}). Driving this below $\delta$
requires $k \ge \log_4(1/\delta) = \tfrac12\log_2(1/\delta)$: about $10$ rounds
for $\delta = 10^{-6}$, $20$ for $10^{-12}$, $64$ for $2^{-128}$. Each bit of
confidence costs a constant number of rounds, not a constant factor, because the
error is one-sided and a single witness collapses the chain.

\paragraph{BPP amplification.}
The deferred proof of \Cref{thm:bpp-amplification} is a Chernoff calculation. Let
$A$ accept correctly with probability $\ge 1/2 + \epsilon_0$ ($\epsilon_0 = 1/6$
for the $2/3$-versus-$1/3$ gap of \Cref{def:bpp}), let $X_i = 1$ when run $i$ is
correct, independent by \Cref{ax:element-indep}, and let $X = \sum_{i=1}^k X_i$
with $\mu = k(1/2 + \epsilon_0)$. The majority is wrong when $X < k/2 = (1 -
\epsilon')\mu$ with $\epsilon' = \epsilon_0/(1/2 + \epsilon_0)$, so the lower tail
gives
\[
  \Prob{X < k/2} \le \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\mu(\epsilon')^2}{2}\right)
  = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{k\epsilon_0^2}{2(1/2 + \epsilon_0)}\right)
  \le \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{k\epsilon_0^2}{3}\right),
\]
the last step since $1/[2(1/2 + \epsilon_0)] \ge 1/3$ for $\epsilon_0 \le 1$.
Asking this be $\le \delta$ gives $k \ge 3\ln(1/\delta)/\epsilon_0^2$, so the
$\epsilon_0 = 1/6$ gap costs $108\ln(1/\delta)$ rounds (the constant tightens
with the sharper lower-tail exponent). The point is the shape: BPP amplification
is $O((1/\epsilon_0^2)\log(1/\delta))$, exponential decay in $k$ with the
exponent the squared margin above $1/2$. Against RP amplification's bit-per-round
($\fnrate$ from $1/2$ to $2^{-k}$ in $k$ rounds), the two-sided contract pays a
$\Theta(1/\epsilon_0^2)$ overhead per bit, the price of needing a vote rather
than a single witness.

\paragraph{Chernoff versus the variance interval.}
The Chernoff bound and the variance interval of \Cref{sec:binomial-estimator}
are two tails of the same binomial estimator $\hat p = X/n$. Chebyshev on the
variance $p(1-p)/n$ (\Cref{prop:binomial-variance}) gives $\Prob{|\hat p - p| \ge
\epsilon} \le p(1-p)/(n\epsilon^2) \le 1/(4n\epsilon^2)$, polynomial in
$n\epsilon^2$; Hoeffding, the additive cousin of \Cref{thm:chernoff}, gives
$\le 2\exp(-2n\epsilon^2)$, exponential. Chebyshev needs only the variance and
gives the chapter-7 point estimate its Cram\'er--Rao certificate; Chernoff needs
the full moment-generating function and gives sample complexity. The gap between
the polynomial $n = \Theta(1/\delta)$ and the exponential $n =
\Theta(\log(1/\delta))$ is the gap between $10^6$ samples for $\delta = 10^{-6}$
and $20$, which is why Chernoff, not Chebyshev, is the workhorse of
randomized-algorithm analysis.
