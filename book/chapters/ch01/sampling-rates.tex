\section{Sampling and Estimating Rates}
\label{sec:sampling-rates}

\Cref{sec:quantifying-noise} defined what $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$ mean
as conditional rates read off a contingency table.
\Cref{sec:boolean-universe-first-look} walked the joint structure of the
four cells of that table. Both treated the rates as known. In practice
they are not. A spam filter arrives without a label saying ``my
false-positive rate is $0.03$''; a randomized algorithm comes with a
worst-case error bound but not a tight one; a noisy channel must be
characterized from observed traffic. The honest question is: given a
finite sample of trials, how do we estimate the rates, and how
confident should we be in the estimate?

This section answers in three short pieces. Binomial counts give the
estimator. The central limit theorem gives an approximate confidence
interval. A notebook simulation lets the reader watch both work and
fail. The full theoretical treatment of the estimators (bias, variance,
efficiency, alternative interval forms) lives in chapter~7 (Part~II),
which earns the formal step after the algebra of approximate sets is
in place.

\subsection*{Binomial counts}

Run a noisy bit $n$ times. Of those $n$ trials, suppose $n_0$ have
truth $\bot$ and $n_1 = n - n_0$ have truth $\top$. The two row totals
of the contingency table of \Cref{tab:contingency} are $n_0$ and $n_1$.
For each of the $n_0$ trials with truth $\bot$, the noisy bit
independently reports $\top$ with probability $\fprate$; the count of
such reports is the false-positive count FP. Independence across the
$n_0$ trials makes the count a binomial random variable,
\[
  \textnormal{FP} \;\sim\; \mathrm{Binomial}(n_0, \fprate).
\]
The same construction on the $n_1$ trials with truth $\top$ gives
$\textnormal{FN} \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_1, \fnrate)$.

The natural empirical estimators are the row-conditional proportions
\[
  \hat{\fprate} \;=\; \frac{\textnormal{FP}}{n_0},
  \qquad
  \hat{\fnrate} \;=\; \frac{\textnormal{FN}}{n_1}.
\]
These are exactly the formulas of \Cref{sec:quantifying-noise} with
empirical counts in the numerator. The estimators have two properties
worth stating at this stage and deferring the proof for later. First,
each estimator is unbiased: $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\fprate}] = \fprate$ and
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\fnrate}] = \fnrate$. Second, the variances are
\[
  \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\fprate}) \;=\; \frac{\fprate(1 - \fprate)}{n_0},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\fnrate}) \;=\; \frac{\fnrate(1 - \fnrate)}{n_1}.
\]
Both shrink as $1/n_0$ or $1/n_1$, so the estimator improves with more
trials of the corresponding truth class. The variance numerator
$\fprate(1 - \fprate)$ peaks at $\fprate = 1/2$, where the noisy bit is
hardest to characterize, and vanishes at $\fprate \in \{0, 1\}$, where
a single trial settles the matter. Chapter~7 derives both facts.

\subsection*{A confidence interval}

For large $n_0$, the central limit theorem applies to the standardized
estimator. The result is that $\hat{\fprate}$ is approximately Normal
with mean $\fprate$ and variance $\fprate(1 - \fprate)/n_0$. Replacing
the unknown $\fprate$ in the variance with its empirical estimate gives
the standard Wald confidence interval at level $1 - \alpha$,
\[
  \hat{\fprate} \;\pm\; z_{\alpha/2}
    \sqrt{\frac{\hat{\fprate}(1 - \hat{\fprate})}{n_0}},
\]
where $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the upper-$\alpha/2$ quantile of the standard
Normal. The analogous interval for $\fnrate$ replaces $\hat{\fprate}$
with $\hat{\fnrate}$ and $n_0$ with $n_1$.

The Wald form is convenient and asymptotically correct, but it is also
known to under-cover at small $n_0$ when $\fprate$ sits near $0$ or
$1$. The interval can even extend below $0$ or above $1$, which is a
sign that the Normal approximation has broken down. Alternative
intervals (Wilson, Clopper-Pearson, Jeffreys) handle the boundary case
better. Chapter~7 develops the full theory; for now the Wald interval
is the existence statement that an interval is available.

\subsection*{The chapter 1 notebook}

The accompanying notebook
\texttt{python/notebooks/ch01-noisy-bit.ipynb} makes the abstract
statements concrete. It simulates a binary symmetric channel with a
known crossover probability and lets the reader vary the sample size
across several orders of magnitude.

The notebook does four things. First, it runs many independent
replications at a fixed true $\fprate$ for each sample size and plots
$\hat{\fprate}$ as a function of $n_0$ for $n_0$ sweeping from tens of
samples to tens of thousands. The plot shows the empirical estimator
settling toward the true rate, with a visible $1/\sqrt{n_0}$ band of
fluctuation traced by the spread of the replications. The unbiasedness
statement of the previous subsection is visible in the centering of the
band; the variance formula is visible in its width.

Second, at a single fixed $n_0$ it runs many independent replications,
constructs the Wald interval at the $95\%$ level for each, and counts
the fraction of intervals that actually contain the true $\fprate$. The
CLT promises this fraction will approach $95\%$; the reader sees it land
close to nominal at a moderate sample size.

Third, it sweeps $n_0$ across several orders of magnitude and plots the
empirical coverage at each size. For small $n_0$ the coverage falls
below $95\%$, exactly the under-coverage promised above, and the gap
closes as $n_0$ grows. The reader watches the asymptotic guarantee fail
in the regime where it is supposed to fail.

Fourth, it sweeps the true $\fprate$ from $0.5$ down to $0.01$ at a
fixed $n_0$ and shows the coverage gap widening as the rate approaches
the boundary. The pattern is a small but real warning: an estimator
that is asymptotically correct can still mislead at the sample sizes
practitioners actually use. The negative-space chapters of Part~III
return to that point with examples in which the Wald approximation
fails by enough to matter.
