\section{Three Faces of a Noisy Bit}
\label{sec:three-faces}

Three systems, drawn from three different corners of applied probability,
each produce a single Boolean output that may be wrong. The first is a
biased coin that reports an external state. The second is a one-bit
communication channel that occasionally corrupts what it transmits. The
third is a binary classifier that emits a verdict about each input it sees.
We will look at each in turn, with no formalism beyond the most informal
arithmetic. The question we want the reader to hold throughout this section
is the same in every case: what is the abstract structure that all three
share?

\subsection*{Face 1: The biased coin}
\label{subsec:biased-coin-intro}

Imagine a coin that is not quite fair, and whose bias depends on how it has
been held. When you palm it ``in'' (cupped, warm side down) and flip, it
lands heads with probability $\omega$. When you palm it ``out'' (cool side
down) and flip, it lands heads with probability $1 - \tau$. The numbers
$\omega$ and $\tau$ are properties of the coin and how it is being held.
They need not be equal, and neither needs to be close to $1/2$.

This is a contrived setup, on purpose. Read it as a thought-experiment
device that lets us write a single object that has two possible inputs (in
versus out), one Boolean output (heads versus tails), and two independent
parameters for how reliably the output tracks the input. The same flip
mechanism is at work in both cases; what changes is how the coin is being
held. In the language we are building toward, ``in'' is a positive
condition and ``out'' is a negative condition, and the coin reports each
with its own characteristic reliability. The biased coin will return as a
running example throughout Part~I, first in
\Cref{sec:asymmetric-channel} as an asymmetric channel and again as the
worked example of the formal definition in \Cref{ch:axioms-kronecker}.

\subsection*{Face 2: The binary symmetric channel}
\label{subsec:bsc-intro}

A single bit is transmitted across a noisy line: a wire, a wireless link,
a chip-to-chip trace, or any one-bit communication path subject to
disturbance. The line sends $\top$ or $\bot$ from sender to receiver, and
for each bit independently the line behaves as if a small probabilistic
adversary acts on it. With probability $\fprate$ the line flips the bit;
with probability $1 - \fprate$ it passes the bit through unchanged. The
crossover probability $\fprate$ is the same regardless of which symbol is
being transmitted: a $\top$ flips to $\bot$ with probability $\fprate$,
and a $\bot$ flips to $\top$ with the same probability $\fprate$.

This object is the binary symmetric channel, and it is the simplest model
of a noisy one-bit communication line. The symmetry assumption (one
parameter for both flip directions) is a strong one. Many real
communication systems are asymmetric: a transistor pulled to ground may
be more reliable than one held high; a photon detector may have a small
dark-count rate but never an extinguished-photon false negative. Such
systems need two parameters, one per direction, and we will treat them
in \Cref{sec:asymmetric-channel}. For now, the BSC is the cleanest
starting point because it isolates the phenomenon of bit-flip noise from
the question of whether one direction is noisier than the other. The BSC
is the most studied noisy channel in information theory, and the
chapter~2 development of it (\Cref{sec:bsc}) will give it both a formal
definition and a matrix representation.

\subsection*{Face 3: The classifier verdict}
\label{subsec:classifier-intro}

A spam filter examines each incoming message and emits a verdict: spam
($\top$) or ham ($\bot$). Some messages are genuinely spam; some are
genuinely ham; the filter does its best on each. Like any imperfect
classifier, it sometimes flags ham as spam (a false positive) and
sometimes misses spam, marking it as ham (a false negative). The rate
at which it flags ham as spam is the false-positive rate $\fprate$; the
rate at which it misses spam is the false-negative rate $\fnrate$. In
general these two numbers are not equal, and the asymmetry matters: a
spam filter that drops legitimate mail at $1\%$ is a different product
from one that lets $1\%$ of spam through.

Notice that the spam filter, unlike the BSC, needs two parameters by
design rather than by happenstance. The two error directions are
conceptually distinct: a false positive misroutes legitimate mail, and a
false negative leaks spam into the inbox. These are different harms with
different costs. A useful classifier therefore comes with two rates, not
one, and the user of the classifier wants to know both. The classifier
verdict is our most realistic noisy-bit setting, because it forces us to
take two-parameter error structure seriously from the start. The
vocabulary of false positives and false negatives is the bridge to the
contingency-table arithmetic we develop in \Cref{sec:quantifying-noise},
and the spam filter itself returns as a worked example throughout the
chapter.

\subsection*{The unifying observation}

We have three systems: a coin held in or out, a wire transmitting one
bit, and a filter emitting one verdict. They live in different worlds.
The first is physical, the second is informational, the third is
algorithmic. Yet at the level of input-output behavior they look the
same. Each takes a Boolean input ($\top$ or $\bot$, by whatever name) and
produces a Boolean output. Each output is, with some probability, the
correct one, and with the remaining probability the wrong one. The error
behavior in every case is characterized by at most two numbers: the rate
of reporting $\top$ when the truth is $\bot$, and the rate of reporting
$\bot$ when the truth is $\top$. The BSC happens to have a single
parameter because its two rates are constrained to be equal; the coin
and the spam filter need two. But in all three cases, two numbers
suffice to describe everything probabilistic about the system at the
single-query level.

We will call the abstract structure that all three share \emph{a noisy
bit}.

The naming is deliberate. It captures three commitments we will hold to
throughout Part~I. First, the object is atomic: a noisy bit has one
Boolean input slot and one Boolean output slot, and nothing larger. (We
will see in chapters 2 and 4 that everything bigger, multi-bit channels,
multi-witness algorithms, classifier ensembles, is built up from noisy
bits.) Second, the structure is purely behavioral: we say nothing about
how the noise arises, only about its rates. A biased coin, a thermal
photon detector, and a logistic-regression classifier may have totally
different mechanisms and identical noisy-bit profiles, and our framework
treats them identically. Third, the description is parsimonious: at most
two numbers per noisy bit, no more. The rest of Part~I unfolds the
algebra of how these atoms compose, decompose, and combine.

\subsection*{Where the chapter goes from here}

\Cref{sec:quantifying-noise} fixes the vocabulary by introducing the
$2 \times 2$ contingency table that gives $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$ a
precise meaning, and connects this notation to the classifier vocabulary
of precision and recall.
\Cref{sec:boolean-universe-first-look} walks the four-cell
Boolean universe (two true inputs, two observed outputs) qualitatively,
deferring the joint-probability formalism to chapter~4.
\Cref{sec:sampling-rates} asks how we estimate $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$
from data, hooking the chapter's accompanying notebook.
\Cref{sec:why-atom} closes the chapter by saying why this object earns
the title ``atom'' and previews the algebraic development of chapters 2,
3, and 4. The three faces introduced here will return at named points in
each of those chapters; by the end of Part~I, each face will have been
treated at four progressively tighter levels of formality, culminating
in a single definition that retroactively justifies everything we say
about it now.
