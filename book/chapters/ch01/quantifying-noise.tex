\section{Quantifying the Noise}
\label{sec:quantifying-noise}

The closing observation of \Cref{sec:three-faces} was that every noisy bit, biased
coin, binary symmetric channel, classifier verdict, is characterized by at most
two error rates: the rate of reporting $\top$ when the truth is $\bot$, and the
rate of reporting $\bot$ when the truth is $\top$. That phrasing is suggestive
but informal. What does ``rate'' mean precisely, what is the denominator, and how
do we measure these numbers from data? Those are the questions of this section.

The path is short. We will introduce the $2 \times 2$ contingency table that
records the outcome of $n$ observations of a noisy bit, name the four rates one
can compute from that table, and connect those rates to the precision and recall
that practitioners of binary classification already know. By the end of this
section the reader can read off the error rates of any noisy bit from a confusion
matrix, and can move freely between $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ on the one hand and
$(\textnormal{precision}, \textnormal{recall})$ on the other.

\subsection*{The contingency table}

Suppose we run a noisy bit $n$ times. On each trial we know the true input,
either $\top$ or $\bot$, and we observe an output, also either $\top$ or $\bot$.
The result of $n$ trials is a list of $n$ ordered pairs $(\text{truth},
\text{output})$, and each pair falls into exactly one of four joint states.
A pair where the truth is $\top$ and the output is $\top$ is a \emph{true
positive}. A pair where the truth is $\top$ but the output is $\bot$ is a
\emph{false negative}, the noisy bit reported a $\bot$ when it should have
reported $\top$. A pair where the truth is $\bot$ but the output is $\top$ is a
\emph{false positive}. A pair where the truth is $\bot$ and the output is $\bot$
is a \emph{true negative}. A standing source of student confusion is that
``false negative'' names the \emph{system output}, not the truth: a false
negative is a $\bot$ output produced when the truth was $\top$.

Let TP, FN, FP, and TN denote the counts of each joint state across the $n$
trials. By construction $\textnormal{TP} + \textnormal{FN} + \textnormal{FP} +
\textnormal{TN} = n$. We arrange these four counts in a $2 \times 2$ table
indexed by truth (rows) and observed output (columns):

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{l|cc|c}
\hline
              & \textbf{output $\top$} & \textbf{output $\bot$} & total \\
\hline
\textbf{truth $\top$} & TP & FN & TP + FN \\
\textbf{truth $\bot$} & FP & TN & FP + TN \\
\hline
total         & TP + FP & FN + TN & $n$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{The $2 \times 2$ contingency table for $n$ observations of a noisy bit.
Each of the $n$ trials contributes one count to exactly one cell. The row totals
record how many trials had each truth value; the column totals record how many
trials had each observed output.}
\label{tab:contingency}
\end{table}

\Cref{tab:contingency} is the empirical record of one experiment. Two
conventions matter for reading it. First, rows are conditioned on truth: the
top row holds only those trials whose true input was $\top$, and the bottom row
holds only those trials whose true input was $\bot$. Second, the diagonal cells
(TP and TN) record agreement between truth and output, and the off-diagonal
cells (FN and FP) record disagreement. A perfectly accurate noisy bit produces
all of its mass on the diagonal; a maximally noisy one produces it all off the
diagonal.

\subsection*{From counts to rates}

The two error rates of a noisy bit are conditional: each is normalized by the
row total of the truth on which it is conditioned. The
\emph{false-positive rate} is the fraction of $\bot$-inputs that produce a
$\top$-output,
\[
  \fprate \;=\; \frac{\textnormal{FP}}{\textnormal{TN} + \textnormal{FP}},
\]
and the \emph{false-negative rate} is the fraction of $\top$-inputs that produce
a $\bot$-output,
\[
  \fnrate \;=\; \frac{\textnormal{FN}}{\textnormal{TP} + \textnormal{FN}}.
\]
Each rate divides one off-diagonal cell by its row total, not by $n$. The
denominator FP + TN counts only the trials whose true input was $\bot$, and FN +
TP counts only the trials whose true input was $\top$. The conditioning matters:
it makes $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$ properties of the noisy bit itself, not of the
particular mixture of inputs we happen to have observed.

The two complementary rates are the \emph{true-negative rate}
$\tnrate \;=\; 1 - \fprate$ and the \emph{true-positive rate}
$\tprate \;=\; 1 - \fnrate$. The true-negative rate is the fraction of
$\bot$-inputs reported correctly; the true-positive rate is the fraction of
$\top$-inputs reported correctly. The true-positive rate is also called
\emph{sensitivity} in clinical and classification settings, and the
true-negative rate is called \emph{specificity}.

\subsection*{Notation sidebar}

\begin{quote}
\noindent\textbf{Rate symbols.}
The four rates used throughout this book have fixed Greek letters.
\begin{description}
  \item[$\fprate$] false-positive rate. The rate at which a $\bot$-input is reported as $\top$.
  \item[$\fnrate$] false-negative rate. The rate at which a $\top$-input is reported as $\bot$.
  \item[$\tprate$] true-positive rate, $= 1 - \fnrate$. Also called \emph{sensitivity} or \emph{recall}.
  \item[$\tnrate$] true-negative rate, $= 1 - \fprate$. Also called \emph{specificity}.
\end{description}
\noindent In the biased-coin vocabulary of \Cref{sec:three-faces}, $\tprate$
was written $\tau$ and $\fprate$ was written $\epsilon$; here we adopt names
that emphasize what each rate measures.

\noindent The two free parameters of a generic noisy bit are $\fprate$ and
$\fnrate$; the other two are derived. The binary symmetric channel of
\Cref{sec:bsc} is the constrained case in which $\fprate = \fnrate$, and one
parameter suffices. A consolidated cheat sheet, including how these symbols
connect to the five core principles of the framework, appears in
\Cref{app:five-principles}.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Cross-walk to precision and recall}

A practitioner of binary classification looks at \Cref{tab:contingency} with a
different vocabulary in mind. The standard metrics for a classifier are
\emph{precision} and \emph{recall}:
\[
  \textnormal{precision} \;=\; \frac{\textnormal{TP}}{\textnormal{TP} + \textnormal{FP}},
  \qquad
  \textnormal{recall} \;=\; \frac{\textnormal{TP}}{\textnormal{TP} + \textnormal{FN}}.
\]
Read off the table: precision divides TP by the \emph{column} total of trials
the noisy bit \emph{called $\top$}, and recall divides TP by the \emph{row}
total of trials whose true input was $\top$. Recall therefore coincides with
the true-positive rate: $\textnormal{recall} = \tprate = 1 - \fnrate$.

Precision is different. Its denominator is the column total $\textnormal{TP} +
\textnormal{FP}$, which depends not only on the noisy bit's error rates but
also on how many $\top$- and $\bot$-inputs were in the sample. Concretely, if
the sample contains $n_\top$ positives and $n_\bot$ negatives with $n_\top +
n_\bot = n$, then $\textnormal{TP} \approx n_\top \tprate$ and
$\textnormal{FP} \approx n_\bot \fprate$ (treating $\tprate$ and $\fprate$ as
the population rates, this is the expected-count decomposition; for the
finite-sample version see \Cref{sec:sampling-rates}), so
\[
  \textnormal{precision}
  \;\approx\;
  \frac{n_\top \tprate}{n_\top \tprate + n_\bot \fprate}
  \;=\;
  \frac{n_\top (1 - \fnrate)}{n_\top (1 - \fnrate) + n_\bot \fprate}.
\]
Two facts follow. First, precision is a function not only of $(\fprate, \fnrate)$
but also of the input mix $n_\top / n_\bot$, the \emph{prevalence} of positives
in the sample. Recall depends only on the noisy bit. Second, precision and recall
together determine $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$ only when the prevalence is also known.
The pair $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ is intrinsic to the noisy bit; the pair (precision,
recall) is intrinsic to the noisy bit together with a workload.

This is why the framework of this book uses $(\fprate, \fnrate)$ as the primary
description of a noisy bit, and treats precision and the related accuracy as
derived measures. The full development of these derived measures lives in the
companion paper on classification measures; here we note only that they are
expressible in terms of the four cells of \Cref{tab:contingency} and the
prevalence.

\subsection*{Worked example: a spam-filter run}

Suppose a spam filter is run on a labelled mailbox of $n = 100$ messages, of
which 40 are spam ($\top$) and 60 are ham ($\bot$). The filter's outputs
produce the contingency table

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cc|c}
\hline
              & \textbf{flagged spam} & \textbf{flagged ham} & total \\
\hline
\textbf{true spam} & 35 & 5  & 40  \\
\textbf{true ham}  & 3  & 57 & 60  \\
\hline
total         & 38 & 62 & 100 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Row totals: $35 + 5 = 40$ spam, and $3 + 57 = 60$ ham. Column totals:
$35 + 3 = 38$ flagged, and $5 + 57 = 62$ not flagged. Grand total $100$. The
table is internally consistent.

The four rates are
\[
  \fprate = \frac{3}{60} = 0.05,
  \quad
  \fnrate = \frac{5}{40} = 0.125,
  \quad
  \tnrate = \frac{57}{60} = 0.95,
  \quad
  \tprate = \frac{35}{40} = 0.875.
\]
By construction $\tnrate = 1 - \fprate$ and $\tprate = 1 - \fnrate$, and both
identities check.

The two classifier metrics are
\[
  \textnormal{precision} = \frac{35}{35 + 3} = \frac{35}{38} \approx 0.921,
  \qquad
  \textnormal{recall} = \frac{35}{35 + 5} = \frac{35}{40} = 0.875.
\]
Recall matches $\tprate$, as the cross-walk predicts. Precision lies between
$\tprate$ and $1$, reflecting that the column total 38 is smaller than the row
total 40 because the filter misses 5 spam messages. The prevalence of spam in
this sample is $40/100 = 0.4$; a sample with a different prevalence would yield a
different precision from the same $(\fprate, \fnrate)$, illustrating the point
of the previous subsection.

\subsection*{What we have, what comes next}

A noisy bit is characterized by two numbers, $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$, each a
conditional rate read off the contingency table. The complementary rates
$\tprate$ and $\tnrate$ are derived, as are the classifier metrics precision
and recall. The symbols $\fprate, \fnrate, \tprate, \tnrate$ recur throughout
the book with the meanings fixed in the notation sidebar.

\Cref{sec:boolean-universe-first-look} now walks the four-cell Boolean
universe of joint truth-output pairs whose conditional rates this section has
just defined, treating the cells qualitatively before \Cref{ch:axioms-kronecker}
assigns each cell a joint probability. \Cref{sec:sampling-rates} returns to
the present section's contingency-table arithmetic from the data-analytic side,
asking how reliably $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$ can be estimated from finite $n$.
