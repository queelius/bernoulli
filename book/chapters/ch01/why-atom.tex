\section{Why ``Atom''?}
\label{sec:why-atom}

The noisy bit has been built up carefully over four sections. We have seen
its three faces (\Cref{sec:three-faces}), named its two rates
(\Cref{sec:quantifying-noise}), walked its joint structure
(\Cref{sec:boolean-universe-first-look}), and shown how to estimate the
rates from finite samples (\Cref{sec:sampling-rates}). The opening
overview of the chapter promised something more: the noisy bit would be
the framework's \emph{atom}, not merely its first example. It is worth
pausing on what that word is doing here, because the rest of the book
takes the claim as a working hypothesis.

A noisy bit is atomic in a precise sense. It has no internal Bernoulli
structure to peel back. The input is a single Boolean value, the output
is a single Boolean value, and the entire behavior is captured by two
numbers, the false-positive rate $\fprate$ and the false-negative rate
$\fnrate$. You cannot decompose a noisy bit into smaller noisy parts,
because a smaller part would have a sub-Boolean input or output, and
there is nothing below a Boolean. The atom is the place where the
internal accounting stops and the external accounting begins. Every
larger object the book builds, from chained channels to noisy AND
gates to randomized algorithms to approximate sets to general
algebraic types, will turn out to be assembled, by some specific
composition rule, from noisy bits. The atom is the smallest object
that the framework's machinery applies to without itself invoking the
machinery.

That commitment is empty until we discharge it. The next three
chapters do the discharging in three different directions.

\subsection*{Chapters 2 through 4: the algebra of the atom}

Chapter 2 (\Cref{ch:binary-channels}) gives the noisy bit a formal
shape. The two-rate description of \Cref{sec:quantifying-noise} becomes
a $2 \times 2$ row-stochastic matrix $Q$. The binary symmetric channel,
where $\fprate = \fnrate$, is one specialization; the asymmetric binary
channel, where the two rates may differ, is the general case; the
Z-channel, where one rate is forced to zero, is a useful boundary. The
chapter introduces \emph{model order} as a way to talk about how many
distinct rates a channel actually has, and shows that the noisy bit
sits at model order two, the minimum non-trivial value. Chapter 2 also
takes the first step in composition: channels can be chained, and the
matrix product of two row-stochastic matrices is another row-stochastic
matrix, so a chain of channels is itself a channel. The atom is closed
under serial composition.

Chapter 3 (\Cref{ch:boolean-algebra}) raises the question that classical
logic forgot to ask. AND, OR, and NOT are familiar operations on
Boolean values. What do they do to noisy bits? If the two operands of
an AND each carry their own $\fprate$ and $\fnrate$, what are the
effective rates of the result? The answer is the composition theorem
(\Cref{sec:composition-theorem}): the output is itself a noisy bit,
with rates computable in closed form from the rates of the operands.
The Boolean operations stay closed when their operands stop being
exact. The chapter develops the closed-form rules and applies them to
several worked examples. The atom is closed under the operations of
Boolean algebra.

Chapter 4 (\Cref{ch:axioms-kronecker}) axiomatizes. Chapters 1 through 3
silently assume that error events at distinct inputs are independent,
and that the rates themselves do not drift across the universe of
inputs. Chapter 4 names these as Axiom 1 (\Cref{sec:axiom-1}, element-wise
independence) and Axiom 2 (\Cref{sec:axiom-2}, conditional independence
of per-block rates). The Kronecker factorization theorem
(\Cref{sec:kronecker}) shows what those two axioms purchase: the joint
distribution over a block of inputs factors as a tensor product of the
per-element channel matrices, which is what makes the per-element
description sufficient. Chapter 4 then writes the formal definition
$\Bernoulli{\BoolType} = (X, Y, Q)$
(\Cref{sec:bernoulli-bool-formal}) and shows that every concrete object
chapters 1 through 3 worked with is an instance of it. The atom is
characterized axiomatically, not just by example.

\subsection*{Miller-Rabin: a familiar algorithm in unfamiliar dress}

One example deserves its own preview because it is the first place the
framework will pay an unfamiliar dividend. The Miller-Rabin primality
test is among the most widely deployed randomized algorithms in
computer science; every cryptographic library uses it. The standard
treatment presents Miller-Rabin as a clever piece of number theory
attached to a clever piece of probability. \Cref{sec:miller-rabin}
takes a different view. One round of Miller-Rabin is exactly a noisy
bit, with $\fnrate = 0$ (a prime never fails a witness test) and
$\fprate \le 1/4$ (the strong-liar count for any composite is at most a
quarter of the witness pool). The $k$-round test is the AND of $k$
such noisy bits, and the famous bound $\fprate_k \le (1/4)^k$ is not
a separate piece of analysis but a direct application of the
composition theorem of \Cref{sec:composition-theorem}. The number
theory contributes the per-round $1/4$ bound; everything else is the
algebra of the atom. We will see the calculation in full in chapter 3.

\subsection*{Part II: sets out of atoms}

Chapter 1 has shown what a single noisy bit looks like. A natural next
question is what happens when many of them are arranged into a
structure. Part II asks the version of this question that
membership-test data structures invite. A set $S$ over a universe $U$
induces, for each element $u \in U$, a Boolean query, namely ``is $u$
in $S$?''. An approximate set is one in which each such query is a
noisy bit. Bloom filters, the canonical example, are a recipe for
producing a noisy-bit instance per universe element with one-sided
error ($\fnrate = 0$) and a tunable $\fprate$. The atom becomes a
building block, and the project of Part II is the algebra of the
resulting structures: how set operations propagate error rates, how
classification measures quantify quality, and how much space an
approximate set must consume to hit a given accuracy target. Each of
these threads is grounded in the atom but is not reducible to it; the
atom is the unit, not the whole story.

\subsection*{Part III: lifting the atom past Boolean}

The Boolean domain is convenient but not fundamental. Once the
machinery of Part II is in place, the natural question is whether the
same construction works for richer codomains. Part III replaces
$\{\top, \bot\}$ with an arbitrary algebraic type $T$. Approximate
maps, approximate relations, and approximate values of structured
types all become available. The two-element model order of chapter 2
ceases to be a ceiling. The atom of chapter 1 is the simplest
non-trivial special case of a much larger family of atoms, one for each
algebraic type, and the closures and composition rules of Part I
generalize accordingly. Where Part II takes the atom and builds a
collection out of it, Part III takes the atom and changes the alphabet
it works in.

\subsection*{Closing}

This has been a forward glance. The chapter ahead, namely
\Cref{ch:binary-channels}, picks up where \Cref{sec:sampling-rates}
left off and gives the noisy bit its first formal vocabulary. Before
that, the next and final section of this chapter, the bibliographic
notes, names the antecedents of what we have just done: Shannon's
channel framing, the contingency-table tradition from medical
statistics, the precision-and-recall vocabulary from information
retrieval, and the original Miller and Rabin papers on primality
testing. The historical record will tell us that almost every
component of chapter 1 was already in use somewhere; what is new is
the decision to treat them all as one object and to call that object
an atom.
