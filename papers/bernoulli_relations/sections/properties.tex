\section{Preservation of relational invariants}
\label{sec:invariants}
A relation may be required to satisfy reflexivity, symmetry, single-valuedness,
or transitivity in every realization. Per-tuple error rates alone do not ensure
these global properties. We first compute preservation probabilities under the
independent-tuple model, then distinguish three ways to enforce constraints:
sharing a canonical source, adding reported tuples, and deleting reported tuples.
The constructions have different rate and dependence consequences; they do not
give a classification of all possible repairs.

Throughout the preservation calculations, the universes are finite,
$\Relation{R}$ is fixed and satisfies the named property, and
$\ARelation{R}[\fprate][\tprate]$ satisfies \cref{asm:ber_trial}, with
$\tprate=1-\fnrate$. Probabilities concern the random construction, and each
constructed tuple bit is reused. The canonical construction below deliberately
replaces tuple independence by independence between equivalence classes.

\subsection{Reflexivity}
\label{sec:reflexive}
A relation on $\Set{A}$ is reflexive when it contains every diagonal pair.

\begin{theorem}[Reflexivity preservation]
\label{thm:reflexive}
For a reflexive $\Relation{R}$,
\begin{equation}
  \Prob{\ARelation{R}\text{ reflexive}}=\tprate^{\Card{\Set{A}}}.
\end{equation}
For fixed $\tprate<1$ this tends to zero as $\Card{\Set{A}}\to\infty$;
for $\tprate=1$ reflexivity is preserved surely.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The diagonal pairs are distinct true members. Reflexivity requires all their
independent acceptance trials to succeed, giving the product.
\end{proof}

When reflexivity is known in advance, answering true on the diagonal enforces it
exactly. This changes the diagonal true-positive rate to one and introduces no
false positive there. It is a rule for known required tuples, not a consequence
of taking an OR over a singleton trial.

\subsection{Symmetry}
\label{sec:symmetric}
For $a\ne b$, symmetry requires the two ordered membership bits for
$\Pair{a}{b}$ and $\Pair{b}{a}$ to agree. Diagonal pairs impose no constraint.

\begin{theorem}[Symmetry preservation]
\label{thm:symmetric_preservation}
Let $P$ and $N$ count the positive and negative unordered off-diagonal pairs of
a symmetric $\Relation{R}$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \Prob{\ARelation{R}\text{ symmetric}}
  =(\tprate^2+\fnrate^2)^P
   \bigl((1-\fprate)^2+\fprate^2\bigr)^N.
\end{equation}
For fixed $0<\tprate<1$ and $0<\fprate<1$, this tends to zero as $P+N$ grows.
Decay also follows whenever an unbounded number of pairs have agreement
probability bounded away from one.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A positive pair agrees when both trials accept or both reject, with probability
$\tprate^2+\fnrate^2$. A negative pair agrees with probability
$(1-\fprate)^2+\fprate^2$. Different unordered pairs involve disjoint trials,
so these probabilities multiply. Each base is strictly below one at an interior
rate, while at rates zero and one the corresponding trials agree surely.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Symmetric closure]
\label{thm:symmetric_closure}
Let $\Relation{R}$ be symmetric and define
\begin{equation}
  \Pair{x}{y}\in\ARelation{R}[s]
  \iff \Pair{x}{y}\in\ARelation{R}
        \text{ or }\Pair{y}{x}\in\ARelation{R}.
\end{equation}
Then $\ARelation{R}[s]$ is symmetric in every realization. Off the diagonal its
false-positive and true-positive rates are, respectively,
$1-(1-\fprate)^2$ and $1-(1-\tprate)^2$. On the diagonal its rates remain
$\fprate$ and $\tprate$, where the corresponding truth class is present.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $x\ne y$, symmetry of the exact relation makes the two trials homogeneous
in exact membership. They are independent under \cref{asm:ber_trial}, so the
OR rejects exactly when both reject. For $x=y$ there is only one trial: reading
it twice does not create an independent copy. Swapping the coordinates leaves
the OR unchanged, which proves symmetry.
\end{proof}

The repaired ordered bits within an off-diagonal pair are identical, and are
dependent whenever their common acceptance probability lies strictly between
zero and one. Independence remains between distinct unordered pairs. A later
operator must therefore use these shared source identities.

\subsection{Single-valuedness}
\label{sec:functional}
A relation $\PFun{f}\subseteq\Set{X}\times\Set{Y}$ is single-valued when each
key has at most one reported value. Having no spurious value is sufficient but
not necessary: if the true value is missed, one spurious value alone still
gives a single-valued row. Undefined keys must also be included.

\begin{theorem}[Single-valuedness preservation]
\label{thm:functional}
Let $v=\Card{\Set{Y}}\ge2$, $d=\Card{\Dom(\PFun{f})}$, and
$u=\Card{\Set{X}}-d$. Define
\begin{align}
 q_1&=(1-\fprate)^{v-1}
       +\fnrate(v-1)\fprate(1-\fprate)^{v-2},\\
 q_0&=(1-\fprate)^v+v\fprate(1-\fprate)^{v-1}.
\end{align}
Under \cref{asm:ber_trial},
\begin{equation}
 \Prob{\ARelation{f}\text{ single-valued}}=q_1^d q_0^u.
\end{equation}
A factor with no keys is an empty product, equal to one.
For $v\le1$ single-valuedness is automatic. For fixed $v\ge2$,
$0<\fprate<1$, and $\tprate>0$, both $q_1$ and $q_0$ are below one, so the
preservation probability tends to zero as the number of keys grows.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At a defined key the true-value indicator $T$ is Bernoulli with mean $\tprate$,
independent of the spurious count $K\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(v-1,\fprate)$.
The row is single-valued exactly when $T+K\le1$: either $K=0$, or $K=1$ and
$T=0$. Thus its probability is $\Prob{K=0}+\fnrate\Prob{K=1}=q_1$.
At an undefined key every value is negative, so the reported count is
$\operatorname{Binomial}(v,\fprate)$ and the probability of at most one is
$q_0$. Different rows have disjoint trials, proving the product.
\end{proof}

The probability of \emph{no spurious report} at a defined key is still
$(1-\fprate)^{v-1}$. It equals that row's single-valuedness probability when
$\fnrate=0$, but otherwise can be strictly smaller. At $\fprate=0$ deletions
of true values preserve single-valuedness surely, regardless of the universe
size.

\subsection{Transitivity}
\label{sec:transitive}
Transitivity requires every reported two-edge path to have a reported shortcut.
The premises are the \emph{reported} pairs, not merely the true pairs of the
exact relation. Triple constraints overlap, so their probabilities cannot in
general be multiplied. A disjoint subcollection nevertheless gives a simple
upper bound.

\begin{theorem}[A sufficient regime for vanishing transitivity]
\label{thm:transitive_preservation}
Suppose the fixed rates satisfy $0<\fprate,\tprate<1$. Put
$p_-=\min\{\fprate,\tprate\}$, $p_+=\max\{\fprate,\tprate\}$, and
$n=\Card{\Set{A}}$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \Prob{\ARelation{R}\text{ transitive}}
 \le \bigl(1-p_-^2(1-p_+)\bigr)^{\lfloor n/3\rfloor},
\end{equation}
which tends to zero as $n\to\infty$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose $\lfloor n/3\rfloor$ vertex-disjoint ordered triples $(a,b,c)$.
On each triple, reporting $(a,b)$ and $(b,c)$ but rejecting $(a,c)$ violates
transitivity. The three distinct tuple trials are independent, and this event
has probability at least $p_-^2(1-p_+)>0$. Events on the selected triples use
disjoint trials and are independent. Transitivity excludes every such event,
which gives the bound.
\end{proof}

This sufficient condition does not cover all boundary-rate cases. For example,
an empty exact relation with $\fprate=0$ remains empty and transitive surely.
There is no universal ordering of the four invariants by preservation
probability. Nor does the bound imply a complexity lower bound for repairing
transitivity.

\subsection{Canonical representation and specific repair operators}
\label{sec:closure_dichotomy}
Fix an equivalence on a tuple universe $\Set{U}$. Call a constraint
\emph{class-induced} when its satisfying relations are exactly the unions of
equivalence classes, called orbits here. Every orbit $O$ of an admissible exact
relation is homogeneous: either $O\subseteq\Relation{R}$ or
$O\cap\Relation{R}=\EmptySet$. Symmetry has orbits
$\{\Pair{a}{b},\Pair{b}{a}\}$ of size two off the diagonal and one on it.
For invariance under coordinate permutations, repeated coordinate values can
likewise make actual orbit sizes smaller than the maximum.

Reflexivity instead forces a specified collection of tuples to be present;
it is not, by itself, a class-induced constraint. Single-valuedness imposes a
cardinality cap on each row, not equality of all bits in an orbit. We treat
these constructions separately.

\begin{theorem}[Class-induced constraints admit an orbitwise OR-closure]
\label{thm:local_closure}
For a class-induced constraint, let $h(O)=\Card{O}$. Starting from an
independent-tuple approximation, admit all of $O$ whenever at least one of its
trials accepts. This operator satisfies the constraint in every realization
and is idempotent. Its orbit-specific rates are
\begin{equation}
 \fprate_O=1-(1-\fprate)^{h(O)},\qquad
 \tprate_O=1-(1-\tprate)^{h(O)}.
\end{equation}
If $1\le h(O)\le m$, then
$\fprate\le\fprate_O\le1-(1-\fprate)^m$ and
$\tprate\le\tprate_O\le1-(1-\tprate)^m$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
An orbit is rejected exactly when all its $h(O)$ independent, homogeneous trials
reject. Filling a reported orbit introduces no tuple in another orbit, and
every orbit is either empty or full after one application. A second application
therefore makes no change. The bounds follow from monotonicity in the exponent.
\end{proof}

One application here is a structural statement, not a constant-time algorithm:
explicit evaluation can inspect all $h(O)$ trials for a queried orbit. The
output bits within an orbit coincide, so tuple independence is lost unless the
orbit is a singleton or its output is deterministic.

\begin{theorem}[Canonical tests preserve the base marginal rates]
\label{thm:canonical_test}
For a class-induced constraint, choose a fixed representative
$\operatorname{rep}(O)$ of every orbit and answer a query $t$ with the single
frozen trial on $\operatorname{rep}(\operatorname{orbit}(t))$. The resulting
$\ARelation{R}[c]$ satisfies the constraint surely and has the unchanged
per-tuple rates $(\fprate,\tprate)$. Under \cref{asm:ber_trial}, different
orbits have independent canonical trials.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
All tuples in an orbit receive the same answer, so each realization is a union
of orbits. Homogeneity makes its representative a true member exactly when the
queried tuple is a true member, preserving the appropriate marginal rate.
Representatives of different orbits are distinct, giving independence between
orbits under the stated assumption.
\end{proof}

The invariant therefore requires no increase in the \emph{marginal} error rates.
Canonicalization can rewrite queries to an existing construction without
reducing its storage, or precede construction of an approximate set over the
quotient universe. The latter has one input key per member orbit; for a
symmetric relation with empty diagonal this is $\Card{\Relation{R}}/2$ keys.
This key-count reduction is not a claim that every implementation halves its
allocated bytes: encoding, canonicalization, and fixed overhead remain relevant.
The common orbit answer is a shared source under \cref{asm:source_model}; a
later projection can therefore correlate distinct output tuples, as shown in
\cref{thm:project_sources}.

The OR-closure has a different purpose. At a fixed raw true-positive rate it
raises recall from $\tprate$ to $1-(1-\tprate)^{h(O)}$, while increasing the
false-positive rate too. The following inversion budgets that increase.

\begin{corollary}[Rate budget and conditional space accounting]
\label{cor:invariant_space_cost}
If all orbit sizes are at most $m\ge1$, a raw false-positive rate
\begin{equation}
 \fprate=1-(1-\fprate')^{1/m},\qquad 0<\fprate'<1,
\end{equation}
ensures every negative orbit's OR-closure rate is at most $\fprate'$.
Equality holds for an orbit of size $m$. For fixed $m$, as $\fprate'\to0$,
\begin{equation}
 \fprate=\frac{\fprate'}m+O((\fprate')^2),\qquad
 \log_2\frac{\fprate'}{\fprate}=\log_2m+O(\fprate').
\end{equation}
Thus an accounting model charging $-\log_2\fprate$ bits per stored input key,
with other per-key costs held fixed, assigns an asymptotic $\log_2m$-bit
surcharge per input key to this rate change.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Substitution gives $1-(1-\fprate)^m=\fprate'$, and smaller exponents give
smaller rates. Taylor expansion at $\fprate'=0$ proves the asymptotics.
\end{proof}

The space statement is conditional accounting, not a universal memory bound
for two-sided error channels. The familiar one-sided approximate-membership
benchmark~\cite{bernoulliEntropy} has $\tprate=1$, so no recall improvement
is available there. A construction offering $\tprate<1$ requires its own
space--rate relation, including whether tightening $\fprate$ can hold
$\tprate$ fixed. Comparing total memory with the canonical construction must
also account for their different numbers of stored keys.

\paragraph{Deletion for single-valuedness.}
Keeping at most one reported value per key enforces single-valuedness.
Use a uniform tie-break, independent of the membership trials, sampled once per
row and reused. For a defined key with $v=\Card{\Set{Y}}\ge1$, the retained
true-value probability is
\begin{equation}
 \tprate\,\Expect{\frac1{1+K}}
 =\tprate\,\frac{1-(1-\fprate)^v}{v\fprate},
 \qquad K\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(v-1,\fprate),\quad\fprate>0.
\end{equation}
Indeed, conditional on reporting the true value it wins with probability
$1/(1+K)$. The expectation follows from
\[
 \Expect{1/(1+K)}=\int_0^1(1-\fprate+\fprate t)^{v-1}\,dt.
\]
At $\fprate=0$ the continuous extension is $\tprate$. This probability tends
to zero as $v\to\infty$ for fixed $\fprate>0$ and $\tprate>0$; neither its
rate nor the work to inspect a row is independent of the value-universe size.
Deleting tuples cannot increase any individual false-positive probability,
but it can discard the true value. A map representation enforces
single-valuedness by its output type instead~\cite{bernoulliMaps}.

\paragraph{Limits of the orbit construction for transitivity.}
\begin{lemma}[No equivalence induces transitivity]
\label{lem:no_quotient}
For $\Card{\Set{A}}\ge2$, no fixed equivalence on $\Set{A}\times\Set{A}$ has
the transitive relations as exactly its unions of classes.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Unions of classes of a fixed equivalence are closed under union. For distinct
$a,b$, the relations $\{\Pair{a}{b}\}$ and $\{\Pair{b}{a}\}$ are each
transitive, but their union omits $\Pair{a}{a}$ and is not transitive.
\end{proof}

This excludes the particular canonical-source construction of
\cref{thm:canonical_test} as a representation of \emph{all} transitive
relations. It does not exclude other encodings, restricted subclasses, or
repairs that add or delete tuples.

\begin{theorem}[Depth of simultaneous transitive completion on a path]
\label{thm:transitivity_cascade}
Let $\Relation{R}_0=\{\Pair{1}{2},\ldots,\Pair{k}{k+1}\}$, with $k\ge1$,
and iterate
\begin{equation}
 \Relation{R}_{j+1}=\Relation{R}_j\cup(\Relation{R}_j\circ\Relation{R}_j).
\end{equation}
The first iterate equal to the transitive closure has index
$\lceil\log_2k\rceil$. The closure contains $\binom{k+1}{2}$ pairs, adding
$\binom{k+1}{2}-k$ pairs to the path.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Inductively, $\Relation{R}_j$ contains exactly the forward pairs of distance at
most $\min\{2^j,k\}$. Composing two such paths doubles the maximum distance,
and every smaller positive distance is retained or obtained by splitting it
into two available distances. All forward pairs first appear when $2^j\ge k$.
These pairs form the full strict order on $k+1$ elements, giving the count.
\end{proof}

The iteration has no universe-independent bound on its number of rounds; for
$k\ge2$ it can add $\Theta(k^2)$ pairs. The statement concerns this
\emph{simultaneous addition rule}, not every algorithm for transitive closure
and not every repair objective. In particular, deleting every pair produces a
transitive relation while sacrificing all true answers. Meaningful claims
about repair costs must specify the allowed operations and accuracy target.

Database repair and consistent query answering already study the dependence
of repair problems on the constraint class~\cite{bertossi-2011-repairing}.
Here the narrower role of the constructions is to expose how a representation
choice changes both marginal error and shared sources. A finer marginal-rate
partition does not restore tuple independence: symmetry may have only a few
rate blocks while imposing perfect dependence within each orbit. Query-level
consequences are treated in \cref{sec:query_contracts}.

\subsection{The approximate partial function}
\label{sec:map}
For product key and value types, write
$\Set{X}=\Set{X}[1]\times\cdots\times\Set{X}[n]$ and
$\Set{Y}=\Set{Y}[1]\times\cdots\times\Set{Y}[m]$. A partial function is a
single-valued relation $\PFun{f}\subseteq\Set{X}\times\Set{Y}$; it is total
when every key has a value. Its domain is $\Proj_{\Set{X}}(\PFun{f})$ and its
image is $\Proj_{\Set{Y}}(\PFun{f})$. A partial function may be total; the
term does not require an undefined key.

\begin{definition}[Approximate partial function]
\label{def:approx_pfun}
A random approximate partial function of $\PFun{f}$ is a random relation
$\APFun{f}\subseteq\Set{X}\times\Set{Y}$ whose every realization is
single-valued, together with specified error guarantees relative to
$\PFun{f}$. For a defined key $x$, its three disjoint outcomes are returning
$\PFun{f}(x)$, returning a different value, and returning no value. For an
undefined key, the outcomes are returning a value and returning no value.
These probabilities may be specified pointwise or averaged under an explicit
key distribution.
\end{definition}

Domain false-positive and false-negative rates alone do not determine value
accuracy: returning a wrong value at every defined key can preserve the domain
exactly. A raw tuplewise approximation of a function graph need not meet this
definition because it can report multiple values. The deletion construction
above enforces the required type and gives its correct-value probability;
map constructions and composition are the subject of the companion
paper~\cite{bernoulliMaps}.
