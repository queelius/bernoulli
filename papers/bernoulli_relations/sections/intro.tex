\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Relational data is everywhere: a relation, in Codd's sense~\cite{codd1970}, is
just a subset of a product of attribute domains, and tables, indexes, joins, and
projections are the daily vocabulary of data management. Relations are also sets,
and a large body of work shows that sets can be represented far more compactly
than their exact membership tables if one is willing to tolerate a controlled
rate of error. Bloom filters~\cite{bf} and perfect hash filters~\cite{phf} answer
membership with a known false-positive rate while storing a small fraction of the
bits an exact structure would need. Reading a relation through such a structure
turns an exact relation into an \emph{approximate} one: some tuples are reported
that do not belong, and some genuine tuples are missed.

\paragraph{The gap.}
The per-structure error of an approximate set is well understood in isolation,
but a relation is rarely queried in isolation. It is selected, projected, and
joined, and the results are projected and joined again. Two questions then have
no off-the-shelf answer. First, how does the per-tuple error of the operands
propagate through the relational operators: what false-positive and
false-negative rates does a projection or a join inherit, and does the clean
element-wise independence that makes the set model tractable survive the
operation? Second, relations often carry structural invariants (a relation is
reflexive, or symmetric, or single-valued, or transitive), and approximation
tests each tuple independently, so a symmetric relation read through an
approximate set need not come back symmetric. Does an approximated invariant
survive, and at what rate? This paper answers both.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We treat the approximate relation as a Bernoulli random approximate
set~\cite{bernoulliSets} over the tuple universe, the abstract data type of
\cref{sec:approx_rel_model}, and from that footing we establish the following.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Error propagation through the relational operators}
    (\cref{sec:rel_algebra}). Selection is rate-transparent. Projection amplifies
    the false-positive rate to $1 - (1-\fprate)^{k}$ in the fan-in $k$ and raises
    the model order while preserving element-wise independence. A join does
    \emph{not} preserve element-wise independence: two output tuples that share an
    input component reuse the same operand trial and are positively correlated, so
    the joint law over output tuples does not factor, and we carry the correlation
    as a closed-form factor. This is the operators' sharpest contrast, and (as the
    related work discusses) the representation-error face of the lineage dependence
    known from probabilistic databases.
    \item \emph{Preservation of relational invariants, and a closure dichotomy}
    (\cref{sec:invariants}). Reflexivity, symmetry, single-valuedness, and
    transitivity are each preserved only with a probability that is a product over
    the constrained tuples, and so tends to zero as the ground universe grows. We
    give the per-invariant rate, and then a sharper structural result, a closure
    dichotomy. A class-induced invariant (reflexivity, symmetry) is \emph{free}:
    one canonical trial per orbit preserves it deterministically at the base
    rates with no space surcharge, and an optional one-pass OR-closure trades a
    $\log_2 m$-bit surcharge, in the orbit size $m$, for an $m$-trial recall
    boost. The antitone case (single-valuedness) is repaired by deletion at a
    closed-form rate. Transitivity, by contrast, is induced by no equivalence at
    all, and admits no bounded closure: its repair cascades to depth
    $\lceil \log_2 k \rceil$. The cut is decomposability, not arity.
    \item \emph{The approximate partial function} as the single-valued special
    case of the model (the dictionary), recovered directly from the relational
    treatment.
    \item \emph{A trapdoor-database application} (\cref{sec:trapdoor_rel_model}),
    in which cipher relations and cipher partial functions index an encrypted
    store, the relational operators run over the cipher relation, and the leakage
    profile is exactly the Bernoulli error rates of the model.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Related work.}
The nearest neighbor is the literature on \emph{probabilistic} and
\emph{uncertain} databases~\cite{suciu2011}, including systems such as
Trio~\cite{widom2005trio} and MayBMS~\cite{huang2009maybms}. That work models
uncertainty \emph{in the data}: tuples carry probabilities or lineage, a database
denotes a distribution over possible worlds, and a query computes the marginal
probability of each answer over those worlds. The Bernoulli approximate relation
is a different object. There is an \emph{objective} base relation, exact and
certain, and the uncertainty is injected entirely by a \emph{space-saving
representation} of it: the false-positive and false-negative rates are properties
of the data structure, not of the data.

Two technical points keep the relationship honest. First, the per-tuple marginal
rates we derive are not new. A join's product false-negative rate and a
projection's $1-(1-\fprate)^{k}$ false-positive rate are exactly the marginals a
tuple-independent probabilistic database obtains by extensional evaluation, the
latter being the familiar union-of-filters identity; we claim no credit for them.
Second, our sharpest operator result, that a join's output tuples sharing an input
component are correlated, is in possible-worlds language the well-known fact that
query answers sharing lineage are dependent, the same dependence that makes
extensional evaluation of self-joins fail and that the dichotomy theory of
probabilistic query evaluation is built to handle~\cite{suciu2011}. What this
paper adds on the operator side is narrower and concrete: it carries that
dependence explicitly through the Bernoulli error model, as a closed-form
correlation factor ($1/a$) in a setting where the rows are membership tests of a
compressed encoding rather than uncertain facts.

The genuinely new territory is the \emph{invariant} side. A probabilistic database
does not ask whether an approximated symmetric relation stays symmetric, or how a
projection's fan-in amplifies a structural false-positive rate, because in that
setting the relation has no underlying exact truth to be faithful to and no
representation-induced error to propagate. Those questions, and the per-invariant
preservation rates and closures that answer them, are about the fidelity of a
compressed encoding, orthogonal to possible-worlds semantics.

\paragraph{Companion papers.}
This paper sits in a family built on the Bernoulli set model~\cite{bernoulliSets}. The set-theoretic composition that the join and the subset and
equality predicates reduce to (closed-form rates for intersection, union, and
complement) is developed in the composition paper~\cite{bernoulliComposition}.
Approximate partial functions as first-class maps, and the error rates of map
\emph{composition}, are the subject of the maps paper~\cite{bernoulliMaps}; here
the partial function appears only as a single-valued relation. The
information-theoretic space bound (the bits per relation forced by a given error
rate) is established in the entropy paper~\cite{bernoulliEntropy}, and the
space-optimal perfect-hash-filter construction that meets it is the subject of a
further companion. We cite these rather than re-derive them, and confine this
paper to the genuinely relational territory: operator propagation, invariant
preservation, and the relational application.

\paragraph{Organization.}
\Cref{sec:relations} reviews relations and the relational operators (selection,
projection, join, composition). \Cref{sec:approx_rel_model} defines the random
approximate relation as a Bernoulli set over the tuple universe and records the
error-count machinery it inherits. \Cref{sec:rel_algebra} propagates error through
the operators. \Cref{sec:invariants} treats invariant preservation and the
symmetric closure. \Cref{sec:map} presents the approximate partial function as
the single-valued special case.
\Cref{sec:trapdoor_rel_model} develops the trapdoor-database application, and
\cref{sec:conclusion} concludes with the dichotomy as the takeaway and the open
problems.
